# game-ai

## combat mechanics
- [model] Dungeon-building idle games usually juggle interlocking systems: construction, output, capacity, worker dispatch, maintenance costs, guarding, and permanent upgrades plus lab work.
- [definition] A dungeon is a fixed challenge above normal area farming: the player must defeat a set enemy sequence ending in a boss, without swapping gear or food mid-run, for rewards unavailable elsewhere. When: Designing structured challenges beyond open-ended farming. Why: Fixed sequences and guaranteed rare rewards differentiate dungeons from farming.
- [definition] Combat level is the average of attack, strength, defence and hitpoints levels, rounded down. When: Computing a player's combat level for requirement checks. Why: A single averaged number gives one comparable measure of combat readiness.
- [mechanism] When combat resolves on its own, the player's role shifts from executing fights to choosing what to train and which gear to equip. When: when an idle RPG automates combat. Why: Removing manual execution moves meaningful decisions to planning and preparation.
- [mechanism] A boss phase threshold changes behavior once HP drops below a set value, here gaining damage reduction at 50% HP, giving a boss feel without a separate combat mode. When: Adding a boss feel within the existing combat loop. Why: A threshold switches the boss's stats or behavior inside the same tick-based combat.
- [mechanism] A boss special attack fires a guaranteed hit of 15% of its max HP, reduced by player damage reduction, when HP first crosses the threshold, and fires only once. When: Adding a scripted burst at a boss phase change. Why: A one-time guaranteed hit at the threshold creates a dramatic moment without repeating.
- [mechanism] Combat runs on a fixed tick, where each combatant's attack bar fills by speed times tick and attacks when full. When: Building idle combat with attack timing. Why: Fixed ticks make attack timing deterministic and simple to simulate.
- [mechanism] Accuracy and max hit use an effective-level formula, and hit chance depends on attacker accuracy compared to defender defence. When: Computing hit chance and max hit in combat. Why: Comparing attacker accuracy to defender defence determines how often attacks land.
- [rule] Require the player to plan their loadout before entering a dungeon, because gear and food cannot be swapped mid-run. When: Designing a dungeon run. Why: Fixed runs make preparation the main decision before the fight.
- [rule] Show dungeon difficulty through a required combat level on the dungeon. When: Presenting dungeon difficulty to the player. Why: A visible level requirement communicates difficulty before entry.
- [rule] Fight dungeon waves as sequential single enemies, with no pause between them. When: Structuring a dungeon enemy sequence. Why: Sequential one-at-a-time fights keep the pacing continuous.
- [rule] Tie each dungeon to a new content tier so every clear unlocks something new, such as an area, enemy set, crafting material, or endgame content. When: Designing the sequence of dungeons across a game. Why: Each unlock gives a reason to clear the next dungeon.
- [rule] Make Cooking a gate for harder combat, since cooked food is consumed directly in combat. When: Linking production skills to combat readiness. Why: Consumed food makes cooking directly relevant to survival in fights.
- [heuristic] A dungeon run or expedition can act as one loop inside a larger meta loop, feeding rewards into the next run. When: Structuring a dungeon inside a larger progression loop. Why: Nested loops let each run contribute to long-term progress.
- [procedure] 1) Start with easy enemies. 2) Repeat them once. 3) Introduce a new enemy type or mechanic. 4) Combine known threats into a first real challenge. 5) End with a boss. When: Ordering enemies in a fixed dungeon sequence. Why: Escalating in steps lets players learn each new threat before facing combinations.
- [tradeoff] Dungeons offer structured, planned challenge with guaranteed rare rewards, while open area farming offers open-ended play where the player can stop at any time. When: Choosing between structured challenges and open-ended play. Why: Fixed runs demand planning and commitment, while farming allows flexible stopping.
- [pitfall] Food and combat setup matter when offline, and underprepared combat can kill the player. When: Designing offline combat. Why: Offline fights run without player input, so preparation must be sufficient.
- [fact] Combat is the spine of progression, and other skills feed into combat power. When: Designing how skills connect to progression. Why: Linking all skills to combat gives every skill a purpose.
- [fact] Combat uses three styles (melee, ranged, magic) with auto-combat, and auto-eat triggers when HP drops below a threshold. When: Designing combat automation and styles. Why: Automation lets idle combat run safely while still offering choice of style.
- [fact] Some games make automation the theme as well as the mechanic: the fiction shows the player handing control over production to systems, mirroring what the mechanics do. When: when designing a game whose core loop shifts from player action to system-run production. Why: Fiction and mechanics that express the same handoff of agency reinforce each other.
- [fact] A boss special attack can kill the player, which must then trigger the normal death handler. When: Implementing a boss special attack. Why: Scripted damage is still real damage and must follow the death path.
- [fact] Max HP is 10 per Hitpoints level in the example, so level 1 has 10 HP and level 99 has 990 HP. When: Scaling player health with level. Why: Linear scaling keeps HP growth predictable.
- [fact] Critical hits deal 50 percent bonus damage. When: Designing critical hit effects. Why: A fixed bonus gives crits a clear, noticeable payoff.
