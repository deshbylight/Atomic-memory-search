# core-design

## generator progression
- [model] An optimal income-to-cost model ranks generators by production gained per cost spent, predicting purchase priorities, though players tend to buy in bulk rather than follow it exactly. When: Predicting which generator a player will buy next. Why: Players deviate from strictly optimal purchase order.
- [definition] In a derivative chain, each generator produces the tier below it, so tiers act as successive rates of change ending at the primary currency.
- [mechanism] Basing generator cost on purchased count rather than total owned keeps manual purchases meaningful when automation supplies large free counts. When: Generators also produced by higher tiers. Why: Free units would otherwise inflate cost and make purchases pointless.
- [mechanism] Each tier in a derivative chain integrates the tier above it, so the currency series is 1, x, x²/2, x³/6, x⁴/24 and so on. When: Stacking generator tiers where each feeds the one below. Why: Successive integration produces polynomial terms.
- [behavior] Incremental games remove manual input over time, so automation is progression: repetition is replaced by broader allocation decisions. When: Designing the progression of an incremental game. Why: Once the player understands an action, repeating it adds little.
- [behavior] Showing every upgrade and number at once removes the sense of mystery; reveal only the next few upgrades. When: Presenting upgrade lists in an idle game. Why: Full visibility removes discovery.
- [rule] Aim for a roughly constant time until the next meaningful purchase, even as raw numbers grow; tune the production-to-cost ratio to control that interval. When: when tuning progression pacing in an idle game. Why: Players feel pace through the wait between meaningful decisions, not the magnitude of numbers.
- [heuristic] Tune dungeon reward or enemy difficulty growth with the same cost-versus-output formulas, so rewards do not outpace player power. When: Designing dungeon run progression. Why: The same exponential-versus-polynomial seesaw governs both.
- [heuristic] Small changes to the cost growth factor or production multipliers can turn a satisfying climb into a runaway or a dead-stop wall. When: when adjusting idle-game cost and production ratios. Why: Compounding makes the progression highly sensitive to small parameter changes.
- [heuristic] Show a visible stock count for capped upgrades so the player knows how many purchases remain. When: Shop items have a purchase cap. Why: Without a visible limit, players cannot plan purchases.
- [heuristic] Growing upgrade costs by about 1.15× per level keeps upgrades reachable yet increasingly meaningful. When: Choosing default idle upgrade cost growth. Why: Exponential growth keeps each step feeling earned without becoming impossible.
- [heuristic] Use a derivative chain when you want the feeling of multiple stacked idle games, with lower tiers visibly growing large. When: Aiming for layered, multi-stage growth. Why: Visible lower-tier growth creates the stacked-game sensation.
- [heuristic] Keep lower tiers worth buying by tying them to purchased-count boosts or dependencies, so players keep spending resources in several ways. When: Lower tiers would otherwise be produced for free and stop being worth buying. Why: Free production removes the reason to purchase.
- [heuristic] Explore generator interactions beyond the two most common idle models; draw a flowchart of connections and experiment with the arrows. When: Designing an idle game's generator structure. Why: Many interaction designs exist beyond the classic models.
- [heuristic] When a build stalls in an idle game, let players re-specialize strategy rather than wait, which keeps optimization interest alive. When: when a single-strategy idle game would flatten into pure waiting. Why: Changing the strategy gives a new optimization problem instead of passive time-passing.
- [heuristic] Upgrades should change what the player can do, not merely scale numbers; flat percentage gains are fine in moderation, yet standout upgrades alter the plan.
- [procedure] Next generator cost equals base cost times growth rate raised to the number owned, so each purchase costs a fixed percentage more. Total production equals base production times owned times multipliers. When: Pricing repeatable generators with exponential costs and a fixed growth rate. Why: Exponential growth makes late purchases costly and forces strategic choices.
- [procedure] 1) Multiply base price by growth rate raised to owned count. 2) Multiply by (growth rate to the power n, minus 1). 3) Divide by (growth rate minus 1). The result is total cost of buying n generators at once. When: Costs follow simple exponential growth with fixed base and growth rate. Why: Closed form avoids looping over each purchase.
- [procedure] 1) Multiply currency by (growth rate minus 1). 2) Divide by base price times growth rate to the owned count. 3) Add 1. 4) Take log base growth rate. 5) Floor the result. This is the maximum number of generators affordable. When: Costs follow simple exponential growth with fixed base and growth rate. Why: Closed form avoids looping over each purchase.
- [procedure] 1) Avoid one-step production. 2) Chain raw resources into intermediate materials. 3) Craft intermediates into equipment or food. For example, copper and tin ore make bronze bar, which makes bronze sword at Smithing 15. When: Designing crafting systems. Why: Multi-step chains give production depth and reason to gather several resources.
- [procedure] 1) Ask whether the player can explain what changed without a calculator. 2) Ask whether the upgrade unlocks or improves a choice the player already understands. 3) Ask whether it stays relevant beyond the next thirty seconds. When: when reviewing any upgrade before shipping.
- [metric] Per-unit cost growth factor for generators typically falls between 1.07 and 1.15; 1.07 raises each purchase cost by 7 percent, 1.15 by 15 percent. When: Setting cost scaling for a chain of purchasable producers or idle-game generators. Why: Compounding production balances rising unit cost, and this band keeps costs rising without becoming unaffordable too fast.
- [tradeoff] Bulk purchase lets a player buy several room upgrades in one step, but only if those upgrade costs follow the same exponential pattern. When: Applying bulk-buy to dungeon room upgrades or unlocks. Why: The closed-form formulas require the same exponential cost structure.
- [tradeoff] Designers may let lower tiers stay unbought; the boost becomes a choice rather than a requirement. When: Deciding whether lower-tier purchases are mandatory. Why: Optional boosts preserve player agency.
- [pitfall] Applying bulk-buy or max-buy formulas to costs with shifting bases or changing exponents gives wrong results. When: Costs change over time or the exponent is not fixed. Why: The closed forms assume constant base and growth rate.
- [pitfall] If the newest generator always dominates on a log scale, older generators become irrelevant and purchase decisions lose meaning. When: Tuning generator cost and output so each new tier overwhelms earlier ones. Why: Log-scale comparison hides the older tiers' contribution.
- [pitfall] A cheap generator that looks better on paper may go unnoticed if its impact is too small for the player to feel. When: Comparing generators by numeric value alone. Why: Players respond to perceived impact, not calculated value.
- [pitfall] If output races ahead of costs, players purchase all at once and suspense dies; if costs race ahead of output, players hit a wall and stall. When: when tuning the ratio of income to cost growth. Why: Both extremes remove the tension between saving and buying, and the workable band between them is narrow.
- [pitfall] Linear upgrade costs create long stretches where progress feels glacial and players stall. When: Setting upgrade cost growth.
- [fact] Generator cost typically grows exponentially while production grows linearly or polynomially, so costs eventually outpace output.
- [fact] Exponential growth of the form n to the x eventually exceeds any polynomial growth, regardless of polynomial exponent or small base.
- [fact] With n tiers the derivative chain's series approaches e^x as n grows, yet any finite chain stays sub-exponential, so exponential costs still outpace production. When: Choosing the number of tiers in a derivative chain. Why: Finite tier counts bound the growth rate.

## offline progression
- [model] Idle RPGs have three play modes: active (manual choices and food management in combat), idle (leaving the game running or closing it), and offline (a welcome-back summary). When: Designing how players engage with an idle RPG. Why: Each mode offers a different level of player attention.
- [definition] Idle games balance exponentially rising generator costs against slower production growth; prestige resets and multipliers reset that balance.
- [mechanism] Offline caps and booster timers create scarcity, and a rewarded ad that relieves that scarcity at the return moment reaches non-paying players too. When: when designing offline earnings and rewarded ads together. Why: The return moment is when the player feels the windfall, so an offer there is both timely and broadly reachable.
- [mechanism] Crafting uses a queue, and offline crafting processes queued items until materials run out. When: Designing offline production in an idle game. Why: A queue lets production continue while the player is away until inputs are exhausted.
- [rule] Make active play more efficient than idle play while keeping idle play meaningful. When: Balancing active and idle progress. Why: If idle progress equals active progress, active play has no purpose; rewarding attention keeps both modes useful.
- [rule] Design the return reward to unlock something and open a player choice, not just display a larger number. When: when designing offline return rewards. Why: A bare total gives no reason to act.
- [rule] Use acceleration-based monetization only when the free loop is already satisfying, so paying players simply want the same progress faster. When: when deciding how monetization relates to the free game. Why: Selling speed on a fun loop feels fair; selling relief from a dull loop does not rescue it.
- [rule] Design the welcome-back payout as a deliberate set piece that pays out accumulated progress in one hit on return. When: when a player returns to an idle game after time away. Why: A single ceremonial payout turns returning into a rewarding moment rather than a status check.
- [rule] Use an offline-first structure when the game's depth lies in choosing what to pursue rather than in moment-to-moment execution. When: when deciding whether a game should progress mainly while the player is away. Why: Strategic choices can be made in short active sessions while the timer handles execution.
- [heuristic] Keep offline simulation of dungeon runs careful, since under-prepared offline combat is dangerous. When: Simulating dungeon runs while the player is away. Why: Offline fights without player input can end in death if setup is weak.
- [heuristic] Show a welcome-back summary of offline gains only when the gap is meaningful, skipping very short gaps. When: Player returns after being away. Why: Summaries for trivial gaps add noise without informing the player.
- [procedure] 1) Record active skill and action on leave, and timestamp on every save. 2) On load, compute elapsed time capped at 86,400 seconds (24 hours). 3) Divide by action time, rounded down, to count completed actions. 4) Roll loot and XP once per action, awarding mastery too. 5) Apply gains and show total on return. When: when implementing offline progress for an idle game. Why: Recording the activity lets the game replay it on return; a capped payout rewards returning without replacing active play.
- [metric] A common offline compromise pays full or near-full output for a cap of roughly a handful of hours up to about a day, occasionally at a slightly lower rate. When: when choosing an offline cap for an idle game. Why: A bounded window keeps return rewards generous without unlimited unattended progress.
- [metric] Offline earnings equal base rate times hours away times 0.5, rewarding absence at half the active rate. When: Computing offline earnings for an idle game.
- [tradeoff] Uncapped clock-based offline progress gives accurate full returns after long absences but removes reason to return often; a cap rewards regular check-ins. When: when deciding whether offline gains are capped. Why: Elapsed clock time gives an accurate production basis, but capping trades that accuracy for more frequent returns.
- [tradeoff] Too little offline payout punishes players who cannot check in often, while too much makes active play pointless. When: when setting how much progress accrues while the game is closed. Why: Offline generosity must balance rewarding absent players against keeping active play worthwhile.
- [pitfall] A model that treats income as per-second ignores timers, so slower high-tier generators are over-favored. When: Building a generator priority model that ignores production timers. Why: Timer-based generators produce in discrete cycles, not continuously.
- [pitfall] When basic progress for non-paying players is sluggish enough to feel like a paywall, a beloved game becomes a resented one. When: when setting free-player pacing alongside paid acceleration. Why: Players who feel forced to pay for basic progress resent the game and may leave.
- [pitfall] Requiring active play to make any progress causes burnout. When: Designing progress rates for active and idle play. Why: Forced attention wears players out.
- [pitfall] Offline simulation counts every action as fully completed and ignores interrupted or partial cycles, so it can overstate offline output. When: Offline progress uses whole-action division. Why: Division by action time assumes no partial cycle at the boundary.
- [pitfall] If the reduced offline reward is too small, absence feels wasted and players stop returning. When: Tuning offline reward rate.
- [pitfall] A shop item restock-time field can exist in data while the service ignores it, so restock behavior must be implemented explicitly. When: Shop data includes a restock time but the service does not read it. Why: A field with no logic gives players false expectations about availability.
- [fact] Common idle-game monetization forms are time skips that collect hours of production, timed production boosters, permanent multipliers bought with premium currency, and rewarded video ads. When: when planning monetization for an idle game. Why: Each form accelerates progress the player is already making, so it fits the core loop.
- [fact] Offline progression can simulate up to 24 hours, with mastery XP earned offline and farming not auto-replanting. When: Designing offline progression limits. Why: A cap bounds catch-up time and clarifies which activities run offline.

## idle game design principles
- [model] Incremental games have three subtypes: clicker (active), idle (passive), and idle RPG (both active and passive). When: Classifying an incremental game. Why: Subtypes differ in how much active input the player gives.
- [model] An idle RPG in this style needs several interlocking loops: skill, combat, mastery, and meta. When: Designing the loop structure of an idle RPG. Why: Interlocking loops feed each other so progress in one supports the others.
- [model] The idle core loop is earn, spend, repeat: resources grow at rate times elapsed time, the rate is base rate times upgrade multiplier, and a prompt appears once resources reach the next cost.
- [model] Idle dungeon management keeps the player engaged through four recurring decisions: build, staff, balance, and sacrifice. When: Designing idle dungeon management. Why: Ongoing decisions keep automated play interesting.
- [definition] Games are systems of meaningful decisions, so a shift from input to optimization matters because it moves the play toward choices.
- [definition] The incremental genre centers on exponential, steadily building resource accumulation via repeated actions or automation. When: Defining the incremental genre. Why: Repetition and accumulation distinguish this genre from other games.
- [mechanism] A timed gathering action accumulates delta each tick; when the timer reaches zero or below, it completes, grants rewards, and resets to the mastery-adjusted duration. When: Idle or skill actions running on a fixed cycle. Why: Repeated timed cycles make each completion a predictable, countable reward event.
- [behavior] Most skills run idle at full efficiency, while combat needs food management and can kill the player offline if set up wrongly, so combat rewards active attention. When: Deciding which systems should run idle. Why: Making only risky combat need attention keeps most play idle-friendly while rewarding focus where it matters.
- [behavior] Identical numeric systems wrapped in different themes feel different to players because the narrative shapes how they respond. When: Choosing a theme for a clicker or idle system. Why: Players respond to narrative around the same numbers.
- [rule] Let the baseline run without the player and make active play an optional multiplier on top; presence should help but never be mandatory. When: Designing idle game progression. Why: Mandatory activity turns the game into a chore.
- [rule] Give every core loop instant feedback at each stage: action, resource gained, spend or invest, then grow stronger or faster. When: Designing a core loop. Why: Immediate feedback at each stage keeps the loop satisfying.
- [heuristic] Identify where the fun lies, such as unfolding features, collecting achievements, or optimizing the prestige loop, before optimizing numbers. When: Early design, deciding which systems deserve most effort. Why: Systems that deliver the fun should be tuned first.
- [heuristic] The real content of an incremental game is the decision layer, such as which upgrade path to take, which currency to prioritize, and when to reset. When: Designing the mid and late game of an idle game. Why: Optimization decisions replace repetitive input as the source of engagement.
- [heuristic] Treat early clicking as temporary onboarding and phase it out quickly, since the interesting game is optimization rather than tapping. When: Designing early game input. Why: Repetitive tapping delays the decision layer.
- [metric] Idle games have about 30 seconds to show they are interesting and about 4 minutes to show what happens next. When: Designing idle game onboarding. Why: Players decide quickly whether to keep attention on the game.
- [tradeoff] Active play gives immediate advantages such as higher-tier actions and better combat setups, while idle play runs only the last selected action; balance them so neither makes the other pointless. When: Designing rewards for active and offline play. Why: If idle matches active play, active play loses value; if idle is too weak, idle play feels pointless.
- [pitfall] Relying on big numbers alone as the source of delight is no longer enough for players. Why: Novelty of large numbers has faded.
- [pitfall] Minimizing waiting, the usual instinct in other genres, works against an idle game, where waiting is part of the product. When: when designing pacing for a game built around absence. Why: Waiting creates the anticipation that gives the return its value.
- [pitfall] Depth on the scale of the largest established idle RPGs creates unmanageable scope for a solo developer. When: Planning scope for a solo idle RPG. Why: Many interlocking systems multiply content and testing work.
- [fact] Idle dungeon RPGs produce resources while the app is closed, show a summary on return, and run fully offline, combining buildings, production, storage, minions, upkeep, defense, research and upgrades.

## skill xp and mastery
- [model] A shared per-skill mastery pool takes 25% of mastery XP, spendable on any item; skill XP multiplies by 1.01, 1.03, 1.05, 1.10 at 10, 25, 50, 95% pool. When: Designing mastery progression across items in a skill. Why: A shared pool connects item mastery to skill-wide rewards.
- [model] The main XP table accumulates per level floor(n + 300 × 2^(n/7)), divides the running total by 4 and floors it, producing a steep curve; level 1 is set to 0 XP. When: Building an exponential level progression.
- [definition] Skill content is defined as data resources: a skill holds identifier, name, description, icon, type, action list and mastery checkpoints; an action holds required level, required items, base time, XP, mastery XP and loot table; a loot entry holds item, min and max quantity and chance.
- [mechanism] Hiring a manager for a generator makes it run permanently without input, so play shifts from collecting payouts to optimizing upgrades, multipliers and timing. When: when a clicker-style income source is automated by a hired unit. Why: Automation removes the repetitive action and leaves decisions about investment and timing as the remaining play.
- [mechanism] Per-item mastery from 0 to 99 can reduce action time, increase yield, or reduce crafting cost for that item. When: Designing per-item mastery benefits. Why: Item-specific bonuses reward repeated use of the same item.
- [behavior] An exponential XP curve feels slow early and steep at high levels, so late levels take far more XP than early ones. When: Designing long progression with many levels. Why: Cost grows exponentially with level.
- [rule] Require matching skill levels for each equipment tier, roughly level 1 for bronze and 15 to 20 for iron in both Mining and Smithing. When: Gating equipment tiers behind skills. Why: Matching gates tie each tier to skill progress in the relevant skills.
- [rule] Gate progression by skill level rather than by time alone, so players control their own path. When: Designing unlocks for skill-based content. Why: Agency over the path keeps players invested in choices.
- [rule] Only start a skill action when the player's skill level meets the action's required level. When: Starting any skill action.
- [heuristic] In an idle game, art and theme cannot rescue a failing progression curve, because the numbers do most of the emotional work. When: when evaluating an idle game's emotional impact. Why: Players feel progress through the numbers, so a broken curve cannot be hidden by presentation.
- [heuristic] Start with 5 to 8 skills rather than the full set for a first game, and start with one combat style before adding all three. When: Scoping a first idle RPG. Why: Full depth creates unmanageable scope for a solo developer.
- [procedure] 1) For each loot entry, roll against its chance. 2) If it succeeds, pick a quantity within its min-max range. 3) Add the items to inventory. When: When a timed action completes.
- [metric] In the example, Hitpoints XP is 1.3 percent of damage dealt, and Attack and Strength XP are each 30 percent of damage dealt. When: Tuning combat XP rewards. Why: Tying XP to damage rewards dealing damage in combat.
- [metric] Action time shrinks linearly with mastery, from 0% reduction at mastery 1 to 15% reduction at mastery 99. When: Computing mastery-adjusted action duration.
- [metric] Per-item mastery uses a gentler polynomial table of 100 × level^1.2 for levels up to 99. When: Setting per-item mastery progression. Why: A polynomial grows more slowly than the main exponential curve.
- [metric] A large idle RPG can run 29 skills, 9 combat and 20 non-combat, each reaching level 120, with an extra tier extending 60 levels beyond that. When: Scoping a large idle RPG skill set. Why: Large skill counts show the depth a mature idle RPG can reach.
- [metric] XP for level n equals a base times n raised to an exponent; exponent 1.2 suits mastery systems, 1.5 suits idle RPGs with many skills, and 2.0 gives steeper quadratic growth. When: Designing an XP curve for levels. Why: The exponent controls how steep the late-game wall feels.
- [pitfall] Check the XP exponent against target completion times, because small changes strongly alter how steep the late game feels. When: Tuning an XP curve. Why: Exponent choice compounds into large differences in total time.

## progression pacing
- [model] Idle RPG progression can run on two tracks: skill levels that unlock content, and per-item mastery that rewards repeated use of a specific item. When: Designing depth in an idle RPG. Why: Two tracks reward both breadth across skills and depth within each item.
- [model] Progression by hours played has four stages: early (0-2h) with fast unlocks and constant discovery; mid (2-20h) with interacting systems and clear goals; late (20-100h) with slow mastery and optimization; end (100h+) with final goals, prestige or expansions. When: Planning the pacing of a long game. Why: Each stage needs a different kind of reward density and goal type.
- [mechanism] Faction-style alignments that change building bonuses and playstyle make strategic choices genuine, not cosmetic reskins. When: when offering alignment choices in an idle game. Why: Alignments that alter the whole strategy give players a meaningful decision with real consequences.
- [mechanism] A progression wall is a stretch where cost growth outpaces income, leaving the player stalled for hours with nothing meaningful to do. When: Cost growth outpaces income in an idle or incremental game. Why: Income cannot keep pace with rising costs, so purchases stop and players stall.
- [heuristic] Space unlocks so a new item tier or ability arrives roughly every hour, e.g. every 10 skill levels; gaps of several hours with nothing new make players quit. When: Placing unlocks along the progression curve or designing level-based unlock intervals. Why: Regular unlocks keep players within reach of something new; long gaps make players quit.
- [heuristic] Time phase transitions to roughly 3 to 5 minutes of active play; rushed or front-loaded content burns players out, sluggish content drives them away; leave room for later phases. When: Pacing phase transitions in idle games, or moving early milestones earlier.
- [heuristic] Place milestones frequently early on, such as a level-up every few minutes, rather than only once a day. When: Early game of an incremental game. Why: Frequent rewards sustain engagement while players are still learning the systems.
- [heuristic] Even a simple visible milestone, such as a large resource target, gives players a direction to play toward. When: Adding goals to an idle game.
- [heuristic] The strongest upgrades change how the player plays, such as moving from manual clicking to passive income, rather than only enlarging a number. When: Designing upgrades for the early and mid game. Why: Changing the player's role keeps upgrades meaningful.
- [procedure] 1) Player supplies input by clicking. 2) First generator produces without the player. 3) Later automation removes collection too. 4) Mid-game the game shifts from input to optimization. When: Structuring an incremental game's early to mid progression. Why: Each step removes manual work in favor of decisions.
- [metric] Early targets: first upgrade purchasable within 60 seconds, first automation within 3 minutes, first prestige within 10-15 minutes. When: Pacing an idle game's opening.
- [pitfall] Idle games with no prestige or endgame goal lose players after the first session. When: Designing long-term structure of an idle game. Why: Players lack a direction to keep playing.

## achievement design
- [model] Achievements serve three purposes: they give goals beyond the main path, reward desired behavior, and form a completion log showing percentage of content completed. When: Designing achievements for a game. Why: Achievements extend play beyond main progression and signal completion.
- [model] Achievement categories include skill milestones, mastery milestones, collection, combat, crafting, economy, and completionist goals. When: Organizing achievements by type. Why: Covering several categories spreads goals across the game's systems.
- [rule] Use standard achievements for one-time events and incremental achievements for stats tracked against a target value. When: Choosing an achievement type. Why: One-time events unlock once while counts need progress tracking.
