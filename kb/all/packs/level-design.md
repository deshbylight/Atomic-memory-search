# level-design

## level layout
- [model] Level quality rests on three non-ranked elements: functional requirements (firmitas), usability (utilitas), and delight (venustas). When: Evaluating or planning a level's overall quality. Why: The three elements cover what a space must do, how easily it is used, and how it engages.
- [model] Nested spaces place separate spaces inside a larger one, such as interiors entered through gateway icons on a continuous outdoor map. When: Games with a continuous outdoor map where locations are entered through icons. Why: It is not geographically realistic, but it matches how people mentally model entering a building as a separate space.
- [model] Game spaces can be organized in five ways: linear, grid, web, points in space, and divided space. Designers often combine them. When: Planning the structure of a game space. Why: Mixing these organizing principles produces new and more interesting kinds of game space.
- [model] Four spatial relationships guide level building: vertical height, horizontal distance, the X/Z size of walkable platforms, and terrain incline angle. When: Designing level geometry for a platformer. Why: Object sizes and distances are tuned within the same system as jump height and running speed, so they evolve together.
- [model] Level design relies on contrast, sightlines, routes, dramatic build-ups, and uncertainty about where the path leads. Why: These elements shape the experience of arriving in a space, which is how the level communicates with the player and guides them onward.
- [model] Molecule design models a level as a graph, where nodes are play spaces and edges are visual or travel links between them. Why: Treating spaces as graph nodes and links lets designers allow or block access between them to shape play.
- [model] Tools for building tension in spaces are spatial psychology, prospect and refuge, lighting, negative space, and height. Why: Spatial contrasts draw on survival instincts, which designers treat as a key to pleasurable design.
- [model] Architectural portals such as doors, arches or gaps mark passage to a new opportunity; use them as a model for how game introductions signal what lies ahead. When: When introducing a player to a new space or first level. Why: A portal symbolically marks passing into a fresh opportunity, the same goal good game introductions aim for.
- [model] A tile can be split into nine scenes: four empty edge pathways connecting areas, and five gameplay scenes at the four corners and the center. Why: Edge pathways serve travel, while the corners and center suit gameplay patterns.
- [definition] Level design arranges architecture, props, and challenges to make play fun and interesting, translating mechanics into spaces players can inhabit; it is game design applied in detail. (x2)
- [definition] Spatial transposition gives an input combination or position a different meaning via chording (simultaneous inputs) or context sensitivity, where object locations relative to the avatar change the input's meaning.
- [definition] A toy box is a developer-only test level for building and testing mechanisms, their interactions, and how the character interacts with geometry, before they go into playable levels.
- [definition] Points in space is a sparse layout of locations that suits wandering with returns to a safe oasis, or games where players define the space themselves. When: Choosing points-in-space organization.
- [definition] Level design workflow covers how a level moves from idea to implementation and where production should start, which affects development schedules.
- [definition] A level is a medium of communication between designers and players, which is why designers bring players in to test it. Why: the level's message is only known through player reactions.
- [definition] The void is a deep, open center surrounded by clutter; placing a major encounter in a large hollow space shows this quality.
- [definition] A symmetric level mirrors one part of the level onto another; asymmetric levels leave most areas unmirrored.
- [definition] Linear space has the player moving forward and possibly back along a line, which may have two ends or loop back.
- [definition] A level designer uses a level editor or toolkit to build missions, scenarios, or quests, and lays out components that fit the game's theme.
- [definition] Edges are boundaries not formed by paths, such as walls, rows of buildings, vegetation changes or genius loci changes, that mark a change in character.
- [definition] Paths are channels of travel that connect important gamespaces, often intimately scaled with few aesthetic features.
- [definition] Nodes are intersections that serve as navigation references, gathering places, and strategic decision points.
- [definition] A node is a play area containing hostile meetings, collectible drops, spawn locations, or chances for action.
- [definition] A tour is a linear opening level that teaches mechanics while previewing later content.
- [definition] Good space has qualities such as completeness, comfort and egolessness, which resemble refuges where people feel safe.
- [mechanism] A change in spatial relationship between avatars, such as pairs being unable to fit through spaces a single avatar could pass, can communicate relational meaning. When: Designing spatial constraints tied to character relationships. Why: Altered physical constraints carry emotional or social meaning for the player.
- [mechanism] Content spread over time is remembered better than content packed into long pauses, since massed learning is hard to retain. When: when teaching a mechanic to players. Why: Tutorial texts stacked one after another overwhelm memory, whereas learning in a motivated situation sticks.
- [mechanism] Removing a game's boundaries changes the strategy and abilities it demands, because players gain options the rules once excluded.
- [mechanism] Rules kept as lists or abstract networks stay intact but can be unplayable, while placing elements on a 2D plane makes them playable through spatial reasoning. When: Abstract relationships among many elements must be played by a person. Why: Spatial reasoning is a strong human capacity.
- [mechanism] Removing connection points and usable screen space reduces the player's ability to improvise routes. When: Later level sections have fewer or smaller connectable areas. Why: Fewer attachment choices leave the player fewer ways to approach goals.
- [mechanism] A story detail, such as a habitual sound, can serve as a search cue, so narrative becomes part of solving a puzzle.
- [mechanism] Wraparound, where an exit on one edge reappears on the opposite edge, lets complex strategies fit inside a single screen. When: when action is confined to a single screen. Why: Wrapping an exit to the opposite edge simulates endless space on a small playfield.
- [mechanism] A large space feels even bigger when it is entered after enclosed spaces. When: when arriving in a large space from preceding spaces. Why: How a space feels depends on the conditions of the spaces that came before it.
- [mechanism] In narrow space, room becomes a scarce resource, so conflict arises as players or NPCs compete for it.
- [mechanism] Coordinate-based travel lets players reach any position by entering its coordinates, so access does not depend on the map's path structure. When: worlds with coordinate addressing. Why: Every point links directly to every other, so typing a destination's address substitutes for any route between places.
- [mechanism] Contrasting intimate, warm materials with cold, public transitional spaces makes the difference between refuge and passage readable. When: Designing spaces that should feel private versus public. Why: Intimately scaled, comfortably finished rooms read as safe, while cold, open transit spaces read as dangerous.
- [mechanism] Shaping limits interaction until players are ready, teaching the elements of a space gradually. When: Spaces offering many interactive possibilities, such as miniature garden worlds. Why: Players can only take in a world's possibilities when they are ready, so the designer holds interaction back until then.
- [mechanism] Rooms with loops let players place level geometry between themselves and enemies, enabling escape through another exit. Why: A second route lets players escape around level geometry that blocks pursuers.
- [mechanism] Super-block layouts sized for cars, with vast green space and highway links, leave few chances for residents to run into one another. When: Cities that separate living, working and government functions into super-blocks linked by highways. Why: Large distances limit casual encounters.
- [behavior] Single-tile platforms make players approach slowly and carefully, so many of them turn a run into a plodding sequence. When: Platforming levels with many one-tile-wide blocks and wide pits. Why: Very little margin for error makes players avoid the full-speed long jump the level could allow.
- [behavior] Early levels with many connection points relative to the player's goals give a large expressive range, so each approach to an enemy can differ. When: Area has many connectable platforms and no impassable ones. Why: Many valid attachment options let the player choose varied routes.
- [behavior] A player who lingers in one spot of a level for several minutes may signal frustration and weak level design. Why: Behavioural playing patterns map directly to content quality, offering an alternate measure that avoids the biases of self-reports.
- [behavior] Arcade players read more distinct screens as a sign of a more advanced game, while home buyers value expansive worlds as more content. When: arcade versus home console. Why: Home games must justify the buyer's purchase with expansive gamespace, whereas short arcade sessions suit a single screen.
- [behavior] In coordinate-addressed worlds, commerce concentrated on points along the axes and centre diagonal, which became busy thoroughfares. When: coordinate-addressed virtual worlds. Why: Such points are easy to travel to and remember, so they become busy thoroughfares.
- [behavior] Smaller floor patterns near high-value items make people slow down and browse, since the denser rhythm sets a slower pace. When: spaces near valuable goods. Why: denser rhythms slow walking pace.
- [rule] If another department cannot build a planned scene, find out what they can build that still serves the story. When: a planned scene is not buildable.
- [rule] Decide deliberately where a game's boundaries are defined and whether or when to breach them. Why: Boundaries mark which elements can interact with the game, and most games keep in-game and outside elements from interacting.
- [rule] Remove boundaries between an experience and its physical surroundings by using physical controls and tangible output instead of screens. When: when the goal is to integrate play with the physical space. Why: Screens create a boundary that separates the experience from its environment.
- [rule] Build levels toward three goals: adjusting player behavior, transmitting meaning, and augmenting space with information. When: Planning a level. Why: A clear goal separates deliberate design from an arbitrary arrangement of objects.
- [rule] Extend a space across several scenes only when the pathways between the screens are clear. Why: As enemy numbers grow, players must be able to escape encounters, which requires readable routes between screens.
- [rule] Use sparse spacing where cruising is the intent and dense spacing where demanding navigation is the intent. When: Choosing spacing for a stretch of play. Why: Spacing sets how much attention the route demands.
- [rule] Size special-tier walls and platform spacing to the specific special jump required to reach them. Why: Spacing and sizes were tuned in the same system as jump height and running speed, so distances match what the jump can cover.
- [rule] Treat learning the game as part of the experience and design it with level design methods. Why: Learning is part of play, not a separate step before it.
- [rule] Lay out each level's design as detailed as possible, since the level layouts are what builders implement. Why: Detailed documents answer the questions teammates would otherwise have to guess at.
- [rule] Pin hand-placed level geometry with positioning constraints so the generator never moves it and minimises the number of position changes it makes elsewhere. Why: Pinned geometry lets the generator respond to designer edits in real time without undoing the designer's placements.
- [rule] Study why bad levels are bad as well as why good ones work, since both teach design lessons. When: when analyzing level design.
- [rule] Keep a separate scene, room, or level file in the engine for building and testing the parts of a level. Why: Being inaccessible to players lets you test geometry and mechanisms without polishing the space.
- [rule] Follow light spaces with dark ones, dangerous spaces with safe ones, and busy areas with sparse ones. When: When a player arrives in a space from a preceding space in the level sequence. Why: The preceding space's conditions shape how the arriving space feels, so an enclosed predecessor makes a large space seem larger.
- [rule] In single-player games, use intimate space for friendly areas or to give the player an advantage. Why: Vantage points and sight lines let the player use their abilities against foes, whereas multiplayer intimate spaces aim for comfort for many players.
- [rule] Use walls or gates for abrupt edges; for gradual ones, use cues such as burned trees or arrows that warn of danger. When: When entering an area that is the site of an event or the realm of a specific group. Why: Landscape rarely changes suddenly, so abrupt borders need architectural marking, while gradual shifts build anticipation for the next zone.
- [rule] Give sandbox worlds distinct districts with unique gameplay; without them, many landmarks can still leave the world feeling empty. Why: Districts hold distinct narrative events and gameplay challenges, which landmarks alone do not supply.
- [rule] In side-scrolling levels, show the far end of any pitfall within one screen width of where the player stands. Why: Players should not have to commit to landings they cannot see, so unseen leaps of faith are avoided.
- [rule] In side-scrolling levels, give enemies and projectiles enough time between entering the screen and reaching the player to be seen and avoided.
- [rule] Let core mechanics decide node form: place intense uses such as gunfights, sharp turns, boss battles, or hard platforming in dedicated nodes. Why: Intense uses of core mechanics mark which spaces are significant gameplay nodes, which is otherwise hard to tell.
- [rule] Make main-route elements bright or warm and side-passage collectibles cooler or analogous, so the main route stands out. When: when a level has both a main route and optional side areas. Why: Contrast separates the path players should follow from optional spaces.
- [rule] Design spaces that are rich in meaning, not merely clear, by using contrast and ambiguity between safe and dangerous qualities. Why: Games can use danger as a tool, and the emotional release after overcoming it is a source of pleasure.
- [rule] Gate paths between areas with a condition, such as main quest progress or a strength threshold the player can reach. When: In open-world games where new districts open as the player progresses through the game. Why: Skill gates stay closed until the player has learned a district's choices, and mastering its last skill triggers the next opening.
- [heuristic] Choose a space model by the design question: 2D continuous for boundary changes, 3D continuous for height changes, discrete zones for analyzing which play happens where. When: Changing a game's space or analyzing its play areas. Why: Each model exposes different interrelationships. No hard rule fixes whether a space is 2D or 3D, or discrete or continuous; the model is a thinking tool, so use whichever is most useful at the moment.
- [heuristic] Draw the curve to find the exact location of a flat section before choosing a fix for it. When: after drawing an interest curve. Why: The drawn curve made the flat stretch visibly obvious as the real problem, while the fix stayed unclear.
- [heuristic] Pairing simplicity with godlike power to create and destroy makes abstract worlds compelling.
- [heuristic] Give layout and story a strong center; a strong avatar at the center of the game world is generally preferred.
- [heuristic] Architecture helps designers understand how people relate to spaces, which matters when designing game worlds and cities. When: Designing environments. Why: Spatial design depends on human relationships with space.
- [heuristic] Altering a game's boundaries is an interesting design exercise for exploring new play. Why: Boundaries define a game's identity, so changing them alters the strategy and the play experience.
- [heuristic] When unsure how to structure a game world, consult a catalog of spatial patterns; matching patterns to the world can make the structure seem obvious. When: stuck on world structure. Why: The catalog's detailed patterns change how a designer views everyday spaces, so the world's structure becomes easier to see.
- [heuristic] Custom levels built in an editor show how a level is assembled from a game's formal elements, a practical reference for designing level structure. Why: Editors expose the same formal building blocks, such as grids, rooms and objects, that underlie level structure.
- [heuristic] A board with a linear path can act as an interactive diagram that shows designers and players the intent of a linear experience. Why: A path with minimal forks makes the emphasis on scripted story beats over player choice visible at a glance.
- [heuristic] Design in one-screen increments so each moment of play shows the core mechanics clearly.
- [heuristic] Withhold weapons and tools in a tutorial's opening area and use a strict path to focus player actions on the lesson. Why: Denying the tools that enable combat keeps the player's actions on the lesson rather than on other mechanics.
- [heuristic] Natural features such as cliffs, valleys, caves, trees, bluffs, and high points can serve as thresholds, and a viewpoint on arrival can stage landmarks visited later. When: When creating entry points or overview points without artificial barriers. Why: Natural features let players survey the world without interrupting immersion.
- [heuristic] Avoid layouts with adjacent navigable regions, since thin walls, walkways through rooms and heavy vertical movement fit Dirichlet domains poorly. Why: Such layouts make the automatic region division misclassify locations, which is costly to diagnose through playtesting.
- [heuristic] A dungeon can be built as its own separate space, reached through a gateway from a larger map. When: when designing a dungeon entered from a larger map.
- [heuristic] Decide deliberately where players should feel constrained and where they should feel free when sequencing rooms and paths. When: Arranging generated or designed rooms and paths. Why: A sense of control lets players project their imaginations into the world, so the feeling of freedom matters more than true freedom.
- [heuristic] A few well-placed details can make a game world seem deep, so spend detail where it matters most. When: you cannot detail everything. Why: Close-up detail that exceeds the impression from afar makes viewers feel the place is deep, beautiful and real.
- [heuristic] Challenge is often built by pairing hazards with wide, precise jumps made in quick succession, usually leaving one optimal full-speed path that the player learns to master. When: designing a sequence of jumps with enemies. Why: A single best route turns the section into a course that players can master through repetition.
- [heuristic] Favor rounded or angular passages over blocky hard-edged geometry so the character's collider keeps flowing along edges instead of catching. When: Laying out traversal spaces for a capsule-shaped character. Why: Rounded edges preserve the edge-sliding flow that makes steering forgiving.
- [heuristic] Subtrees of a partition tree can serve as themed zones with one entrance that can be locked, decorated or given a shared difficulty. When: when a partition of the dungeon is connected to the rest of it by only one corridor. Why: One entrance isolates the group, so it can be locked, dimmed or stocked with harder monsters as a unit.
- [heuristic] Adapt a space's layout to its specific site and terrain so the environment feels native to its location. When: Designing a space tied to a specific natural setting. Why: Responding to the existing site creates a sense of place, a principle often called genius loci.
- [heuristic] Place large open plazas where routes converge so they can hold the crowds that the connecting paths bring. When: Designing the endpoint of multiple converging routes. Why: Convergence concentrates visitors, and open space absorbs them without congestion.
- [heuristic] Restate genre-specific spatial rules as general principles so they apply across genres, such as treating advantageous high positions as a principle for many game types. When: Extracting reusable lessons from a genre-specific level rule. Why: Genre-neutral principles transfer to other games and levels.
- [heuristic] Many keys before one lock, or one key before many locks, implies open-ended exploration; a graph read this way shows how many choices players have. When: when judging how open or linear a lock-and-key layout is.
- [heuristic] Keep design player-centric and view a level as a series of scenes, which helps communicate it to players.
- [heuristic] Even a straightforward linear path stays interesting when it includes twists, turns and challenges. When: when a level is navigated along a single linear path.
- [heuristic] Use side-scrolling for mechanics best understood from the side, such as jumping, climbing, flying, and shooting. When: when the core mechanics are jumping, climbing, flying or shooting. Why: Side-scrolling teaches these mechanics well because everything the player needs is within one screen's distance of the avatar.
- [heuristic] Make narrative staging spaces unique, large, and visible from a distance, using vaulting, arches, domes, and tall structures so they are recognizable as goals. When: designing a space for a climactic or scripted event. Why: Players notice and approach spaces that stand out from their surroundings.
- [heuristic] Give each node a distinct spatial character, sometimes called genius loci, so every landmark offers a different experience. When: when a level is a set of landmark nodes linked by transitional edges. Why: Nodes host the notable gameplay, while edges only move players between them.
- [heuristic] Transitions can use different geometry from nodes, such as sloped ramps as edges with corridors or mazes as nodes. When: when intense gamespaces must be linked by transitional passages. Why: Edges carry players between intense nodes, which keeps the contrast between transition and intensity clear.
- [heuristic] Divide the view into thirds both vertically and horizontally and place key elements along the dividing lines rather than at center. When: when composing a view or framing a key element. Why: Off-center placement keeps the eye moving and makes the composition more interesting.
- [heuristic] Small left or right offsets along a path create a more interesting route than a straight line. When: when designing a path through a space. Why: Gentle shifts add visual interest and direct movement without full turns.
- [heuristic] Levels that combine competition with exploration in a large open world must plan both activities together. When: when a large open world combines multiplayer combat with exploration. Why: Resources found through exploration reward searching, whereas regenerating health speeds combat but undercuts that reward.
- [heuristic] Spaces with several simultaneous functions, such as both-and rather than either-or features, are richer than spaces built on one function. Why: One feature can both aid and threaten the player, such as elevation and hiding spots strengthening or weakening sneaking.
- [heuristic] Make environments quickly reachable and clearly bounded, abstracted into symbolic assets, so players can take them in at a glance. When: when a game world's boundaries and assets are kept limited. Why: Contained, limited spaces let players learn the symbolic system and interact with it more effectively.
- [heuristic] Grouping features in a triad, with two shorter ones beside a tall central one, implies stable horizontal movement. Why: Triangular arrangements read as stable, and pieces pointing at one another pull the eye sideways.
- [heuristic] Give a linear section of context before open areas, rewarding later freedom with new abilities. When: when previewed open areas are reached only after a required linear passage. Why: The linear section gives previewed content context, and later free exploration reads as a reward.
- [heuristic] Keep interactive areas close together, with routes that pass near other areas, so players and residents meet and interact. When: laying out towns and multiplayer maps. Why: Chance encounters sustain activity and safety.

- [procedure] 1) Try different data layouts. 2) Measure the speed difference of each. 3) Keep the layout that performs best. When: When the hot and cold split is unclear. Why: Rarely used data bloats each component, so fewer components fit in a cache line and more misses occur during frequent updates.
- [procedure] 1) Identify the game's physical boundaries. 2) Identify its conceptual boundaries. 3) Compare both to see where play separates from everyday life. When: when analyzing any game's structure.
- [procedure] Building a toy box level: 1) Create at least three simple mechanisms in a comfortable engine. 2) Find at least two ways their objects combine into new interactions. When: exploring how mechanisms combine. Why: combinations of simple objects produce new play.
- [procedure] 1) Play an online multiplayer game. 2) Identify spaces that push players toward a distinct strategy or play style. 3) Sketch each space and its layout.
- [procedure] 1) Make all puzzle pieces feed one final challenge. 2) Nest small pyramids into a hierarchy of increasingly challenging elements leading to the final challenge. 3) Make the top challenge interesting, compelling, and clear so players want to reach it. When: A set of puzzles lacks a common goal, or when designing a multi-stage puzzle structure. Why: A shared final goal gives scattered puzzles a purpose and direction.
- [procedure] 1) Check whether the ship's position exceeds the screen size on X and on Y separately. 2) If it does, move the position to the opposite edge. When: objects must stay on a bounded playfield. Why: Separate per-axis checks keep the object in play without leaving the screen.
- [procedure] 1) Outline all levels and name them. 2) Write a one-paragraph description of each level. 3) Design a map for each level. 4) Populate each map with content. When: Designing a set of levels. Why: Each part of a game system depends on the others, so earlier sections get revised while later ones are written.
- [procedure] 1) Ask how players know where to go and what the landmarks are. 2) Ask whether new mechanics such as flight or teleportation are needed. 3) Ask whether existing assets can build it, and whether new assets can be reused. 4) Identify key visual elements. 5) Define governing world rules. 6) Define daily life beyond the hero's story. Why: A believable world gives its inhabitants their own problems rather than existing only to serve the protagonist.
- [procedure] 1) Choose a level from a game. 2) Describe how its layout, sight lines and boundaries shape player movement and choices. 3) Note the emotional or narrative effect of the space. When: Analyzing an existing level to learn spatial design. Why: Structured analysis turns observation into transferable spatial knowledge.
- [procedure] 1) Draw a straight critical path from entrance to exit. 2) Write the number of locks on the critical path on that path. 3) Branch sub-paths with keys off the critical path. 4) Place puzzles and significant obstacles on the graph, marked as locks but labeled with the obstacle. 5) Count the keys and locks ahead of the player to read linearity. When: when graphing a dungeon or lock-and-key level. Why: A flow-chart style graph gives an objective, shared way to read a dungeon's progression and its blocking obstacles.
- [metric] A 10-hour RPG reaching level 30 averages about three levels per hour, faster early (around four) and slower later (one or two). When: As a starting point for a 10-hour RPG; other values may fit other designs.
- [tradeoff] Arbitrary bounding shapes are hard to support in editors, so tools usually offer boxes, which may be aligned at any angle. Why: Arbitrary bounds are complex to author and support.
- [tradeoff] Single-screen rooms give a tight, contained feel at the cost of the traversal space a movement mechanic needs. Why: A single-screen room confines the view and space, so free swinging cannot take place inside it.
- [tradeoff] Designers can control how much and what kind of information reaches players by choosing what each room communicates. When: when deciding what a level should convey. Why: Each room sets a bounded load, so information is paced and selected.
- [pitfall] Placing objects without a plan, or building around appealing but untested ideas, often yields levels that are not fun once prototyped. When: Building a level in an editor. Why: Without a stated goal, layout lacks a purpose that play can confirm.
- [pitfall] Crowding many complex mechanics into one space overwhelms new players. When: When a single level introduces many new mechanics at once. Why: Too much new information at once exceeds what players can process.
- [pitfall] Tightly packed objects relative to movement speed feel clumsy and oppressive and can cause frustration. Why: Object placement is one variable balanced against avatar movement, so spacing relative to motion sets how the game feels.
- [pitfall] Confining a swing-based traversal mechanic to single-screen rooms leaves no room to swing, making such levels feel claustrophobic and painful. Why: A swing needs open room, and on a single screen nearly every arc runs into a wall.
- [pitfall] Equating good level design with fun narrows the range of experiences a designer can aim for. Why: Many games aim for playful, visceral or thrilling experiences that go beyond simple fun.
- [pitfall] Structural methods about compression and tension apply weakly to games, because most game art is not a rigid body under engine physics. When: when applying architectural analysis to game levels. Why: Many game elements are not rigid bodies in the engine, so they do not respond to its physics like real structures.
- [pitfall] Reading a molecule graph's shape as the level's physical layout is a mistake; the shape shows how spaces interact. Why: The same molecule can yield different level layouts, so the diagram describes how spaces interact rather than their geometry.
- [pitfall] Locations reachable only by typed coordinates stay hidden from players who do not know those coordinates.
- [pitfall] Confusing layouts usually come from poor environment art, such as bland, repetitive spaces or meaningless spatial configurations. When: when players cannot tell where to go. Why: Without distinct visual cues, players cannot read the space.
- [pitfall] A space built from one idea, such as open ground with cover, tends to feel flat and simplistic. Why: A space that serves several functions at once lets one mechanic be both aided and challenged by the same features.
- [pitfall] Linear levels with few choices, such as long hallways between cutscenes and encounters, can leave players detached and less likely to finish the game. When: When a game strips away choices such as customization, job selection or side quests from linear play. Why: Players lose attachment to places where they have little choice or sense of dwelling.
- [pitfall] Placing trees, rocks and buildings at random makes a scene feel unplanned; plan each position deliberately. Why: Players read a world through where its scenic assets sit, so haphazard placement gives them nothing meaningful to interpret.

## level pacing
- [model] Mixed time systems combine turns with a continuous clock, such as a per-player total time limit in turn-based tournament play. - [procedure] Running a graybox playtest: 1) Have a player new to the level, or a partner unfamiliar with a commercial game, play it. 2) Do not interrupt. 3) Take detailed notes on where players get stuck and what is unclear. When: a graybox level is ready for testing. Why: uninterrupted play shows real difficulties.
- [procedure] 1) Replace cutscenes with still images or text pop-ups. 2) Replace complex interfaces with labeled buttons. 3) Replace sounds with synthesized beeps. 4) Replace dialogue with text-to-speech or on-screen text. When: when building a graybox of a game with these elements. Why: Placeholders still show what is happening, so the game stays playable and testable.
- [procedure] 1) Build a rough space from simple geometry. 2) Playtest it. 3) Edit the geometry. 4) When edits shift from major spaces to small details, begin environment art. When: Developing a level before art production. Why: Edits shrinking to details signal the layout is stable enough for art.
- [procedure] 1) Sketch the gameplay as spatial diagrams. 2) Use large masses for major areas, thin zones for connecting paths, and small shapes for secondary areas. 3) Test which uses of each mechanism are worth pursuing. When: Exploring a level concept early. Why: Diagrams reveal the spatial relationships gameplay depends on.
- [procedure] Represent gameplay spaces such as boss rooms, sniping spots or finish lines as bubbles sized by type, with line type showing priority and connection kind. When: planning adjacency of gameplay spaces. Why: Bubble size indicates a space's required size, a convention borrowed from building proximity diagrams.
- [procedure] Vary hatch orientation to distinguish zones, and mark shared spaces with intersecting hatches. When: diagramming zones that differ in use. Why: Varied hatch directions can carry distinct meanings, so zones and mixed-use areas stay readable.
- [tradeoff] Graybox testing suits mechanics-driven games, while experiences that depend on audiovisual emotional triggers lose most of their impact in graybox. When: when deciding whether graybox testing will reveal the game's real appeal. Why: Mechanics-driven play survives crude visuals; emotional audiovisual impact does not.
- [pitfall] Young artists often avoid showing rough work for fear it will make people misjudge their talent. When: When artists are young or early in their careers and must show rough work. Why: They fear rough quality will lead people to misjudge their talent.
- [pitfall] Group decisions about grayboxes are especially vulnerable to the halo effect, which is often the biggest problem in graybox review. When: When a group reviews a graybox. Why: Group judgment amplifies a shared negative impression of visuals.
- [fact] A graybox can cost about a hundred times less than the finished version to build, so most failed ideas are cheap to discard.

## plan drawing and diagrams
- [definition] A parti is a simple formal study that isolates the shape a building or level will take; it can be made on paper or as a model.
- [definition] A plan is a top-down, perspective-free cut of a space, assumed viewed from about 4.5 feet above ground, with cut walls filled solid or hatched.
- [definition] Blueprints reveal how areas are laid out and connect, serving as level maps for challenge and pacing.
- [definition] An overview shows the scope of a space from an elevated or omniscient view without granting full access to it.
- [rule] Plan maps that blend spaces for different play styles with proximity diagrams, placing related spaces near one another before building. When: multiplayer map layout.
- [rule] Proximity diagrams analyze function and are not final spatial plans. When: When a building program is translated into spaces, before a spatial layout is drawn.
- [heuristic] Sketch many uses of a single mechanism early, including its variants, before working out how each will function. When: Developing a mechanism for a level. Why: Breadth of uses reveals which variants are worth building.
- [heuristic] Shape a building's plan around occupants' need for light and around household functions that should be close together. When: Laying out interior rooms. Why: Plans organized around use and light serve occupants better.
- [heuristic] When building exteriors are not shown, plan shape must carry the type; when they are shown, exteriors should resemble familiar forms. When: When building exteriors are not visible to the player. Why: Viewers link a building's use to its form through societal associations, so types help them read their surroundings.
- [procedure] 1) Design a mechanism the player must react to. 2) Sketch three zones for it: safe introduction, slight danger, and high danger. 3) Check that each zone teaches or tests the mechanism at its intended stakes. When: Planning how a new mechanism is introduced in a level. Why: Graded danger lets players learn the mechanism before it is tested under pressure.
- [procedure] 1) Gray-box a level that gives a tour or overview. 2) Note what the overview shows. 3) Check how it prepares players to interact with the level. When: When designing an opening overview or tour of a level. Why: An overview orients players before they act.
- [procedure] 1) Rotate a plan 30 or 45 degrees. 2) Project it upward to create 3D forms. When: showing three-dimensional form without perspective distortion. Why: Plans and sections must be combined to show 3D space, whereas a projected drawing shows three-dimensionality directly from one plan.
- [procedure] Sketch a short game-world stretch taking roughly thirty seconds to a minute to cross, noting three or more spots where the layout highlights a particular mechanic. When: analyzing how a level teaches or emphasizes mechanics.
- [procedure] 1) List the required functions and spaces of the program. 2) Draw bubbles for spaces, sized by required area. 3) Connect bubbles with lines weighted by how important their adjacency is. When: analyzing which spaces must sit next to each other.
- [pitfall] Designing solely from a top-down plan yields a largely flat 3D space, offering few spots where players must glance upward or move vertically. When: When a 3D game's level is laid out using only plan drawings. Why: Without vertical design, height adds little to play.
- [pitfall] Overviews are passive; overusing them or fly-over cutscenes makes pacing stagnant and breaks the sense of discovery.
- [fact] Outdoor terrain often already uses a grid-based height field, which can serve directly for tactical analysis.
- [fact] Most people find it difficult to draw an accurate 2D map of a familiar place, even though they know which doors lead to which rooms. Why: Spatial knowledge is relational rather than cartographic.
- [fact] Plans are usually an architect's first drawing, so they generate the spatial arrangement of a building.
- [fact] Plans cannot show the third dimension; multilevel areas need dotted lines or a section.
- [fact] In 2D games the camera looks from the top or side, and turning off perspective produces an orthographic view.

## hub design
- [model] A hub journey is linear like a ladder, while the player largely decides how to overcome each rung. Why: The general level order is fixed, while players choose missions, backtracking and exploration pace.
- [model] In a hub-and-spoke structure, players complete a set of stages in any order, and after all are done the story becomes linear toward a finale. When: a set of stages can reasonably be done in any order. Why: free ordering gives choice, and a linear finale gives a climax.
- [model] Hub-and-spoke dialogue returns to a root topic list after each topic resolves: the root list is the hub, and each topic branch is a spoke. Why: Looping back to the root list avoids infinite story branching.
- [model] In the hub and spokes structure, each spoke is a self-contained content nugget independent of the others.
- [definition] A hub is an intimate lobby connecting to levels, called spokes, through portals or doors; it is a central point to return to and jump between gamespaces, and is semi-rhizomatic.
- [mechanism] Interactive hotspots can simulate depth within a 2D space. When: When large environments are broken into smaller screen spaces in 2D games. Why: Borrowed painting techniques applied within fragmented screen space create the impression of depth.
- [rule] Use a hub when levels should load separately and the game is built around a central lobby; avoid it when a seamless large world is the goal. Why: Levels load one at a time instead of all at once, which keeps the performance cost low.
- [rule] Make quest hubs larger and slower-paced than spawn points, so they invite exploration and interaction. Why: Such spaces are meant to be carefully explored and are packed with things to do, which encourages interaction between players.
- [rule] Each middle hub must show the NPC reacting to the player's choice, with a distinct response for each option. When: Within a dialogue that has several middle hubs, before splitting it into two encounters. Why: Otherwise the conversation feels scripted and the player's choice seems inconsequential.
- [heuristic] Place casual gathering areas along routes players pass regularly for important tasks, so socializing happens naturally during play. When: designing hubs in online games. Why: Players gather where they already travel, not in places they must go out of their way to reach.
- [heuristic] Hub settlements can grow from vendors and trainers to include inns, auction houses, banks and guild spaces. When: When a multiplayer settlement grows from a town into a larger city. Why: Extra functions in cities push players toward social interaction.
- [heuristic] Hub-based quests should push players outward, with many requiring travel to other hubs, so they form tours of the game's possibilities. When: As players gain power and travel between hubs becomes easier. Why: Quests that send players onward from each hub build a tour of the game's possibilities.
- [heuristic] Players remember one detail per hub, the last one stated, so end each hub on the concrete problem. When: Writing any player-facing hub. Why: The final words of a hub are what players still hold in mind when choosing replies.
- [heuristic] Word order in a hub often affects player understanding more than writers expect, so place key information deliberately. When: Before the player faces their own dialogue choices in a hub. Why: The final words are what players carry into their choices, so they must anchor the problem.
- [heuristic] A hub can supply a story justification for characters heading to thematic realms, such as frozen, volcanic or jungle settings. When: When characters would otherwise have no logical reason to travel to a themed world.
- [procedure] Non-decision hub: 1) Offer one correct guess about the NPC's next line. 2) Offer one incorrect guess. 3) Offer one 'Tell me more' option. This lets players characterize themselves. When: The NPC will continue speaking regardless of the player's choice.
- [tradeoff] Loading levels separately is efficient, but it loses the seamless feel of a large sandbox world. Why: Only one level loads at a time, which lowers the performance load, but the separation through portals or doors breaks the continuous world.
- [fact] Hub spaces are typically non-threatening, letting players explore within the abilities their character already has.

## hidden areas and secrets
- [mechanism] Showing a threat the character cannot see creates suspense, because players know danger is near while the character remains unaware. Why: Gap between player knowledge and character knowledge.
- [behavior] A narrow maze with hidden enemies is stressful for a low-health player but only risky for a healthy one. When: In games where player states shift quickly, such as those managing survival resources. Why: Low-health players are most vulnerable entering a space that disadvantages them, while healthy players must keep their state through danger.
- [heuristic] Place secret rooms above and below a main scrolling space to reward curious players.
- [heuristic] Players can see items placed inside solid terrain that the character could not logically perceive. When: In side-view 2D games that reveal areas beyond the character's sight.
- [heuristic] Hiding spaces such as lockers, vents and crawl spaces restrict both the character's mobility and the player's view. When: In stealth games with lockers, vents, or crawl spaces. Why: Such spaces reinforce that the player character is weaker than its enemies in these games.
- [heuristic] Hide optional items in obscure alcoves and cantilevered ledges so exploring players find refuges that reward looking. When: in exploration-focused levels.
- [heuristic] Meditative spaces can be kept off the main path and hinted at through partial reveals. Why: Incomplete information makes the brain seek verification, which draws players toward the hidden space.
- [heuristic] Reused assets such as textures, existing geometry and recycled characters can make memorable secrets when a studio lacks resources for full side quests. When: the studio has limited production resources. Why: Reused assets are fast to produce and place, and can still create memorable rewards.
- [procedure] 1) Place the reward in view but out of reach. 2) Route the path through other rooms, or behind walls that hint at it through windows or cracks. Why: Making the player locate the path turns the route into a puzzle they solve.
- [pitfall] Rewards that mismatch their context feel arbitrary or unrealistic: a story reward in a dull area with no earning behavior, or a valuable reward for a trivial chore. When: when placing story progression between fixed milestones (for the story-reward case). Why: No player behavior seems to earn the reward in that context; mismatched reward and task breaks believability. (x2)
- [pitfall] Hiding the exit or placing large obstacles that conceal enemies makes a room frustrating, especially in single-player play. Why: Visible goals and routes let players plan around enemies from a safe vantage point.
- [pitfall] Triggers are hidden by default and invisible during play; unhide one while testing to see where it sits. When: When testing a trigger's action in the level editor. Why: Without seeing the trigger's position, the designer cannot verify where it sits or whether it works.
- [pitfall] Hidden lethal danger with no clues can feel unfair and bring players back less often. When: when a hazard can kill without any prior hint. Why: Players cannot anticipate or prepare for the danger, so deaths feel arbitrary.
- [pitfall] Dark alcoves are hard to see from a lit area, so enemies hidden in them can ambush the player who looks into the light. When: when the player moves from light into the surrounding dark. Why: shadow hides danger from a viewer in the light.
- [fact] Small content is often given to new writers; it is not critical-path dialogue, yet the game still needs it.

## reward placement and vaults
- [definition] A reward vault is a room holding items, information, or resources, set apart from ordinary space by lighting, music, and architecture; some also serve as save rooms.
- [definition] A rewarding vista is an impressive view that gives the player a pause after intense action, usually without gameplay resources, and its value lies in pacing and celebrating a climb.
- [definition] Reward clues suggest that a reward may exist nearby without showing it directly, so the player is motivated to check.
- [mechanism] Rewards placed off the direct route draw players onto riskier or less direct paths, much as scenery draws detours; use this to structure exploration and pace play in generated dungeons. When: when side areas should be explored or when placing rewards in generated dungeons. Why: players are drawn toward perceived value even when it costs a detour.
- [mechanism] Placing rewards in narrow areas makes players linger where they are most vulnerable, so reward and danger rise together. When: In a looting mode where players grab items while avoiding a pursuing horde. Why: Tight spaces are meant to waste player time while the player stays exposed to the threat.
- [rule] Give reward rooms their own lighting, sound, or architectural motif so they read as special spaces. When: in generated or hand-built layouts where rewards must be recognized. Why: distinct signals mark a room's function apart from normal play.
- [heuristic] A consistent reward pace, such as one reward every few rooms, keeps players moving forward through a level. When: When players know that rewards arrive at a consistent pace. Why: regular payoffs maintain expectation.
- [heuristic] Chart a level so it prompts the player to return and search for a required item before a later challenge. When: planning backtracking in a level. Why: Flow charts approximate what the player thinks while playing, exposing where the player must be told to return.
- [heuristic] Rewards break up tense moments and encourage players to keep exploring. When: Following stretches of danger or conflict in a level's pacing.
- [heuristic] Reward vaults are often small and may use high ceilings, vaulting, or arches that celebrate the space, like a church apse. Why: Such features set the reward space apart from ordinary level areas through lighting, sound and form.
- [heuristic] For reward vaults, show the reward while obscuring the path to it. When: When the reward sits within the player's line of sight. Why: A visible reward with a hidden route turns the path into a mystery that drives curiosity and exploration.
- [heuristic] Place rewards such as resource replenishment, a sought-after artifact, or a dramatic escape in the falling action or dénouement, where players can slow down. When: Placing rewards near the end of a level or quest arc. Why: Falling action gives players a pause in which rewards can be appreciated.
- [procedure] Analyzing a game's reward pacing: 1) Identify its reward spaces. 2) Describe how it alternates them. 3) Describe how they pull players forward. 4) Describe how they are spaced. When: studying how a game paces rewards. Why: structured analysis reveals pacing patterns.
- [procedure] Create reward clues with reusable modular assets, textures, lighting, and prefabricated symbols that recur throughout the game. When: When a reward should be hinted at rather than shown directly. Why: Recognizable recurring symbols let players learn the signal and pursue it, leaving them a mystery to solve.
- [fact] Spaces themselves can act as rewards, including reward vaults, rewarding vistas, meditative spaces, and narrative stages.

## suspense
- [definition] An uncertainty node is a decision point offering a choice between equal options whose contents are unknown.
- [mechanism] Tying a game event to a real-world value, such as money or physical punishment, gives small in-game failures much more emotional weight. When: When in-game outcomes can be tied to real wealth or physical sensation. Why: The stake is felt beyond the screen.
- [behavior] Withholding part of a landscape from view invites players to explore further. When: In top-down games with expansive worlds where parts of the map stay off-screen.
- [rule] Control how a scene is first revealed using thresholds or entrances that let players pause unseen and survey the space before engaging or entering. When: When designing the entry to a new area, especially in 3D. Why: The first view determines whether the space is readable or confusing.
- [rule] Give players things to look into instead of making everything visible from the start. When: Designing the layout of a VR room. Why: Hidden detail gives players reasons to look around.
- [rule] Use uncertainty nodes only once the player understands the space well enough to take educated risks. When: Once the game has shaped players' understanding of its possibility space. Why: Familiarity with the space lets players judge which uncertain paths are worth the risk.
- [heuristic] Raise stakes gradually so that the climax has everything hanging in the balance.
- [heuristic] Use a winding, forced approach with staged reveals rather than a straight one to build anticipation. When: When a player enters a space and must read its layout from what they can see. Why: Controlling what each view reveals steers the viewer's perspective, making successive reveals more satisfying.
- [tradeoff] Naming unseen places enlarges the perceived world cheaply, but detailing them later when players ask multiplies the content needed. When: When players might ask for detail about places they never reach in the game.
- [pitfall] Designers of 2D games often underuse showing players what the character cannot see, a source of suspense. When: top-down or side views. Why: Players can be shown threats the character cannot see, so suspense comes from the gap in what each knows.
- [pitfall] Suspense sags into boredom when nothing is at stake. When: When a moment has no meaningful consequence. Why: Without stakes, the anticipation that drives suspense has nothing to act on.
- [pitfall] Building areas players never see adds no freedom and wastes effort that could improve the places they do visit. When: When a clearly marked fastest route guides players, so they rarely explore side areas.
- [fact] Risk and randomness arise wherever players meet the unknown, not only in dice or generated numbers.

## grids and measurement
- [definition] Grid space divides the play area into repeated cells, which can be squares, rectangles, hexagons, or triangles.
- [rule] Use the Top and Perspective views together when judging layout and height.
- [rule] Snap geometry to the grid to avoid leaks that stop a level from compiling; some editors snap to power-of-two units such as 16, 32, 64 and 128. When: building geometry in editors that compile levels. Why: Off-grid gaps can leak and block compilation.
- [heuristic] Encounter fixes range from small changes, such as better enemy lighting, to structural changes, such as rebuilding a flanking route. When: After a playtest reveals a list of encounter problems.
- [heuristic] For levels not built on tiles, impose a grid over the geometry and analyze that grid. Why: Tactical analysis methods already run on tile grids, so imposing a grid on geometry lets them apply.
- [heuristic] Grid layouts suit games where players need easy understanding, alignment, and consistent proportion, and where computers must handle the space easily. When: Choosing grid organization. Why: Regular cells are easy to read and compute.
- [heuristic] Base level measurements on human-scale metrics of interaction, which underpin how spaces are sized and felt. Why: Renaissance proportion systems were based on human-body geometry.
- [procedure] Comparing grid types: build one 3D level on a non-orthogonal grid and one on an orthogonal grid, then observe whether sightlines to points of interest change exploration. When: choosing a grid for 3D level layout. Why: sightlines can steer where players go.
- [metric] A first-person camera set above about seven feet or below about five feet distorts the view, because players judge scale using an eye level close to their own. When: Setting first-person camera height. Why: Players judge scale from their own body height.
- [pitfall] Levels built without regard to the player character may not fit it; some doors can be too small for the character to pass through. Why: Geometry sized without the character's metrics can block movement.
- [fact] Hexagonal grids let units move diagonally, which a square grid does not allow.
- [fact] Tabletop map makers rely on grid paper, and many game engines measure space in grid-based units.

## optional routes and branching
- [definition] Web space joins several marked points with paths so players can reach destinations in different ways, with travel that is meaningful or instantaneous.
- [definition] A Steiner point is an added intermediate point, connected to several spaces, that gives the shortest connection among them when they are not directly linked.
- [mechanism] Steiner spaces act as shortcuts: players jump or climb from a node to a Steiner space, then to another node, or cross open space to skip long-route parts. When: Where a level lets players climb or jump vertically between nodes.
- [behavior] Players in a room with two exits almost always leave through one of them, because doors invite opening. When: An empty room with a few exits. Why: Even two options count as a choice.
- [heuristic] Web layouts suit cases where several destinations should be reachable through a number of different routes. When: Choosing web organization.
- [heuristic] Early areas can offer optional routes using special moves to reach higher ledges sooner. When: early level progression.
- [heuristic] Gate a zone behind a key found in another zone, and split monster difficulty between the child nodes. When: Designing locked sections from a partition tree. Why: Only one corridor joins the two cells, so that corridor can be locked off to seal the section.
- [heuristic] Optional dead ends and side branches off a main route can hold treasure or coin caches that reward exploration.
- [heuristic] Steiner shortcut spaces can be rewarded with power-ups to encourage players to use them. When: When players can reach play spaces vertically by climbing or jumping between nodes. Why: Dropping from high ledges onto lower ones saves time, which makes the shortcut worth taking.
- [heuristic] Offer risky optional routes with extra supplies, so skilled players can take them while others skip them.
- [heuristic] Give narrow spaces two exits so players keep an escape route when enemies clog one of them. When: Narrow spaces in a level where enemies may block one exit. Why: Playtesting revealed that a blocked single exit would unfairly kill testers; a second exit removes that trap.
- [procedure] 1) Find a refuge that overlooks the prospect. 2) Read enemy placement and patrol timing from it. 3) Move to a secondary refuge. 4) Wait in refuges until the best moment to advance. When: in stealth levels with patrolling enemies. Why: Refuges let players observe threats before committing to movement.

## scene capture and blockout
- [model] Spaces are single-scan, understood from one spot with one camera pan, or multi-scan, needing scans, movement around obstacles, and further scans.
- [definition] A scene is one screen's worth of designed level space the player sees at one moment, and is the basic unit of pacing. When: When dividing a level into units for teaching. Why: Screen-sized units match what players can process at once.
- [definition] Scanning is the number of looks, such as camera pans and repositioning, a player needs to understand a space.
- [definition] A scene is a single-screen or room-sized gameplay chunk that feels complete and self-contained, a trait many architectural patterns share.
- [rule] Use the simplest geometry that simulates the colliders of the final level when grayboxing. When: Building graybox test levels. Why: Simple shapes capture physical interaction without wasting effort on art.
- [rule] In 3D, treat rooms, hallways and other architectural sections, along with transitions between them, as scenes. When: In 3D games that lack the restricted cameras of 2D and isometric games. Why: Scenes concept helps structure transitions, which are the connections between spaces.
- [heuristic] Block out and playtest levels, and test scoring or level layout, with cheap physical prototypes before committing to full construction. When: When scoring or level layout needs fast testing. Why: Cheap physical prototypes allow fast play-testing and quick multidisciplinary fixes.
- [heuristic] Prototype a single room first to focus on enemy movement and character interaction, then add hallways and connected rooms in later iterations. When: stealth or enclosed-space levels. Why: A single room isolates movement and interaction questions before adding complexity.
- [heuristic] Build levels in order when the game is simple or its progression is linear and upgrade-driven. When: When the game is simple enough to skip planning extensive teaching levels. Why: Each level can add complexity on top of what came before.
- [procedure] 1) Graybox a level using rhythmic architecture. 2) Have playtesters report whether it changes their pace and draws them along.
- [procedure] 1) Set column widths to match row heights so cells are square. 2) Draw a bordered grid, such as 20 by 20. 3) Fill cells with black to form walls, mazes or dungeon layouts. When: sketching grid-based levels by hand. Why: Square cells let a spreadsheet work as graph paper for sketching layouts.
- [tradeoff] Single-scan spaces give quick understanding for casual players; multi-scan spaces give hiding places and surprises for competitive players. When: choosing how readable a space should be for a skill level. Why: Casual players need quick reads of a space, while competitive play rewards hiding spots for surprise.

## prospect and refuge
- [model] Prospects and refuges each come in primary and secondary types; primary ones are occupied or looked onto, secondary ones lie in the distance.
- [model] Refuge-based spaces use low broad ceilings, natural materials, a central hearth, depressed sitting areas, and large overhangs that conceal occupants.
- [definition] A prospect is a large open area that leaves its occupant exposed to attack.
- [definition] A refuge is a small, sheltered space where an occupant can stay hidden while looking out to assess threats.
- [behavior] Players tend to move through prospect spaces and stay still in refuge spaces, so each space type suits a different activity. Why: This draws on early humans who searched open plains for food and returned to sheltered spots to assess threats.
- [rule] Set spacing between refuges in generated spaces, since the distance players cover between them controls pace at any scale. When: when generating arenas or landscapes. Why: The travel distance between refuges sets how quickly play moves, at any scale of arena or landscape.
- [heuristic] Fog, music and shadows can heighten the vulnerability a player feels in a prospect space. When: In open prospect spaces where players already feel exposed, such as wide multiplayer maps. Why: Wide open ground produces an anxiety similar to fear of open space, and atmosphere amplifies that exposure.
- [heuristic] Pair each prospect with a refuge the player can watch it from, so the player can evaluate threats in the open space. Why: A sheltered spot overlooking open ground lets the player judge danger from a position of safety.
- [heuristic] Set refuges on a different level: higher for a wide view, or lower for a cloistered hiding place. Why: Lower ceilings make a space feel more protected, while a raised position gives an advantageous view.
- [heuristic] Place refuges as protrusions overlooking a large circulation prospect, so occupants can watch the flow below. When: in large atrium or hub spaces. Why: Large circulation spaces are uncomfortable to linger in, so a raised refuge gives a static place to watch the flow.
- [heuristic] Large prospect structures pull players from one battle to the next, while explorable interiors hold richer reward and narrative content. Why: Large-scale rhythms keep battles flowing, while interiors reward players who detour to explore.

## skill gating and progression
- [definition] A skill gate is a section that blocks progress until the player successfully performs a specific in-game action. When: When designing obstacles that require a mechanic to pass. Why: Gating progress behind skill ensures players practice the mechanic.
- [mechanism] A movement ability that exceeds existing level constraints forces every level to block its unintended side effects with crates, invisible walls and misplaced props. When: adding a new capability late in development. Why: each level must contain the new ability's consequences.
- [mechanism] Setting a clear rule early and then breaking it later creates delight, because players who learned the rule notice the exception and seek hidden routes. When: After a rule has become part of players' understood logic, such as across a series. Why: A broken rule reads as a surprise only once players already expect the rule.
- [rule] Use environmental changes such as wall colour or texture to signal the player is moving deeper into a large dungeon. When: Designing large dungeons with multiple depth regions. Why: Visual change marks progress and prevents the sense of walking through identical levels.
- [rule] Let progression unlock before every stage is cleared, so stuck players can advance and return to the remainder later. When: When designing progression gates for a set of levels. Why: Requiring full completion can trap players who are stuck on a few stages.
- [rule] Keep a district border closed until the player has learned all the skills of the previous district. When: players must master one skill set before facing the next.
- [heuristic] Progressions can be built by choosing each level according to which skills it forces the player to use.
- [tradeoff] Gated expansion structures are not spatially complex, but they produce achievement and drama as players test their limits. When: When a game confines the player to a small area until upgrades let them cross or defeat blockers. Why: Overcoming enemies or obstacles that once stopped the player gives a sense of achievement and drama.
- [pitfall] Removing the early easy steps of a puzzle makes it hard from the first moment and removes its lasting appeal. Why: Gradually increasing difficulty is what gives a puzzle its lasting appeal.
- [fact] Skill gates range from simple, such as an enemy to jump over, to complex, such as using a newly acquired ability to escape the room where it was found.
- [fact] Short cliffs that the player must jump over can serve as skill gates that require jumping to continue. When: When gating progress through platforming skill. Why: A jump requirement tests the mechanic directly.

## terrain and movement mechanics
- [mechanism] A slight change in terrain, such as adding hills steeper than the tuning space allowed, can make a vehicle bottom out and lose all its momentum. When: When terrain features are steeper than those the vehicle was tuned on.
- [mechanism] Editing terrain, such as placing a forest patch, updates AI movement costs behind the scenes, so the designer never sees the AI data. When: In tile-based games whose level tool calculates AI data behind the scenes. Why: The tile tool calculates movement costs automatically, so no separate pathfinding infrastructure is needed.
- [mechanism] Tile flag combinations set grapple behaviour: connectable-only tiles can be attached and pass through in air; impassable-only tiles make the grapple ricochet without connecting; both-flagged tiles can be attached to, but swinging upward into them knocks the avatar off. When: Tile carries one or both traversal flags. Why: Each flag combination determines whether the grapple connects, whether the avatar passes through, and whether he bounces off.
- [mechanism] When the avatar alters terrain, the dug space changes state, so the layout becomes a mechanic rather than backdrop. When: When the avatar can dig or remove terrain as a movement action.
- [mechanism] Vertical shadows converging on a vanishing point can make a height feel more dangerous, just as horizontal shadows set rhythm. When: When the view looks down into a pit or drop. Why: Vertical elements pull the eye down toward the base, intensifying the feeling of depth.
- [rule] Draw walls as lines on the grid, and treat them as blocking both movement and shots. When: When building a physical hex-grid prototype of a first person shooter arena.
- [rule] Make platforms at least 2 units wide when players must balance on them with tilt controls, since narrower tiles feel unfairly hard. When: Designing narrow platforms for tilt-controlled movement. Why: Two units is the narrowest width players can balance on without frustration.
