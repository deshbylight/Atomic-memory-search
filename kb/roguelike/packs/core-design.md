# core-design

## permadeath and death penalties
- [model] In an afterworld model, the character is never truly dead; the player must regain health and mana there within a limited time while being pulled back to the physical world, keeping only what was regained. When: Designing a recovery system that replaces permanent death. Why: Gives the player a chance to escape death while making it cost something.
- [model] Dead players return to a town-like area some distance from danger, with a penalty such as lost money or dropped equipment. When: Designing a respawn point after death. Why: Distance from danger gives a safe restart while the penalty keeps death costly.
- [definition] Permadeath means no good or bad event, including death, can be undone by reloading a save: the save is deleted on death and the player restarts from the game's beginning.
- [mechanism] Non-death failures such as losing an item or having levels drained add interesting stakes without ending the game.
- [mechanism] Permafailure, where a final decision permanently removes resources, can create tension even in multiplayer games.
- [mechanism] A lost expensive item that can be bought back later encourages players to try new items. When: Designing item loss on death with a recovery option. Why: A recoverable loss lowers the cost of experimenting with items.
- [mechanism] Losing experience works as a penalty because players can recover it by defeating more creatures. When: Choosing an experience-based death penalty. Why: Recovery through normal play turns the penalty into a temporary setback.
- [mechanism] Resurrection sickness reduces stats, possibly greatly, and they recover over time. When: Designing a temporary penalty after the player revives. Why: Time-limited weakness penalizes death without a permanent loss.
- [mechanism] A knocked-out player can wait for a friend's rescue code; the rescuer's reply code lets the player resume from the level where they fell. When: when the game has multiplayer and each copy holds one save. Why: ties death to social play.
- [mechanism] Paid temples as optional save points let death be survivable at a cost: the player pays a priest to set the respawn point, or earns a free resurrection through quests. When: Designing death penalties short of permadeath. Why: A cost keeps death meaningful without ending long play.
- [mechanism] Respawn can depend on where the player last prayed, allowing town temples, roadside shrines or altars to act as respawn points. When: Respawn location should vary across the world. Why: Respawn tied to a place makes shrines useful to the player.
- [mechanism] Resurrection can depend on standing with a deity: some gods never raise the character, while others raise it only at certain favor levels. When: Designing divine or favor-based resurrection. Why: Favor makes resurrection a reward tied to player behavior.
- [mechanism] A raise costs one experience level and one point of Constitution, returns the character to the dungeon start or local temple, needs a minimum character level, and has a survival chance near certain the first time that drops with each death. When: Designing a raise-dead mechanic. Why: Escalating cost and falling odds make repeated deaths increasingly dangerous.
- [mechanism] Limited lives let eliminated players return and seek revenge, which keeps competitive tension alive after elimination. When: Multiplayer games where players can be eliminated. Why: Eliminated players who can return create ongoing competitive pressure.
- [mechanism] Discarding each level after leaving keeps only inventory, character state and shops persistent, so a bad situation can be wiped away as long as escape is possible. When: when the world is largely regenerated. Why: discarding a level is cheap when content is infinite.
- [mechanism] When each run is new, dying costs little because nothing is lost by restarting; in linear games, death can force repeating authored content. When: when designing the cost of failure and restart.
- [mechanism] Permadeath keeps outcomes unpredictable when a random reward can be repeated, because reloading after each attempt would allow unlimited rewards. When: Random rewards tied to repeatable actions, such as a chance of a wish per drink. Why: Reloading would let players farm the reward.
- [mechanism] Frequent respecs reveal which abilities are over- or underpowered, because the rate at which players pick each ability shows its strength. When: a respec system is active and tracked. Why: Pick frequency reflects how players perceive and value each ability.
- [mechanism] Reward should rise with difficulty, because new items stronger than the player's current gear are interesting, allowing some variance.
- [mechanism] Each missing mana point of summoning debt costs 1d3 hit points, so a large debt can kill the player. When: Mana is insufficient to pay a summoning debt. Why: Converting unpaid mana into hit point loss ties the penalty directly to the player's survival.
- [rule] Do not make death cost rare or unique items that cannot be recovered. When: Designing penalties for player death. Why: Losing hard-to-replace items makes players feel cheated.
- [rule] Use shared storage between runs when players should keep some rewards after dying. When: when deaths are frequent.
- [rule] Keep older enemies dangerous so players feel growth; a shallow curve suits games needing constant challenge across many levels.
- [rule] Balance good and bad mood events in both frequency and size.
- [rule] Randomised rewards: use an N-armed bandit model with M of N arms pulled (1 ≤ M ≤ N, N > 1), keeping the underlying reward relation non-random.
- [rule] Limit resets to a finite number, because unlimited resets have no stopping point and remove the cost of failure. When: Designing escape mechanisms such as teleport or restore options. Why: Without a limit, players never face consequences for failing.
- [rule] Make the risk of a blood debt visible to the player before they commit to a summon, so a lethal penalty is a choice rather than a surprise. When: A summon can create a debt that drains hit points. Why: Hidden lethal costs feel unfair, while disclosed costs let players weigh the risk.
- [heuristic] Permadeath suits games that have no sudden-death situations, many failures that do not end the game, and enough balance that one failure is not game over.
- [heuristic] Mistakes should not always be fatal, so players can focus on character growth. When: Setting how punishing errors are. Why: Survivable errors let players invest in growth.
- [heuristic] Respawning enemies often exist to drop rewards that restock supplies, not only to add difficulty. When: when designing respawning enemies. Why: respawns supply resources the player needs.
- [heuristic] Permadeath is a strong way to keep risk attached to progression.
- [heuristic] Penalise losses more on higher difficulty, while making losing harder, so that difficulty carries real stakes. When: Designing mini-game difficulty. Why: Scaled penalties make hard modes meaningfully riskier.
- [procedure] 1) List the resources a death could cost. 2) Rank each by renewability; the more renewable a resource is, the less its loss hurts. 3) Assign the penalty to the most renewable resources, such as gold or experience. When: Designing a death penalty that does not end the run. Why: Renewable losses are recovered through play, while rare losses feel unfair.
- [procedure] 1) Simulate about 10 rounds of combat against the target. 2) For each attack, multiply chance to hit by damage and a typical player resistance factor. 3) Take the highest value across attacks as the threat estimate. When: estimating monster difficulty.
- [tradeoff] Recoverable death gives tactical danger without ending months of play after one mistake, but lowers the single-life tension of permadeath; avoid it if the game must stay pure permadeath. When: Choosing between permadeath and recoverable death. Why: Recovery reduces stakes but preserves long progress.
- [tradeoff] Permadeath can reinforce horror by making every mistake costly, but whether a roguelike can fully reach true horror is uncertain, since roguelike structure may limit how fully dread builds. When: choosing whether a roguelike should aim for horror. Why: Lasting loss sharpens tension, yet roguelike structure may limit how fully dread builds.
- [tradeoff] Permadeath increases responsibility, anticipation, caution, difficulty and satisfaction, and guarantees new content on replay, but makes sudden-death situations frustrating.
- [tradeoff] Death penalties can be paid recovery fees, being left for dead with a random chance, or reincarnation with lost gear; fees suit games where money is plentiful. Why: each keeps the story going while limiting free recovery.
- [tradeoff] Plain-text saves are easy to edit, so developers must decide whether editing is acceptable for their community. When: Designing the save file format. Why: Readable saves invite tampering that can affect fairness.
- [tradeoff] Resets differ in how they fail: scrolls fail when blind or confused, staffs depend on device skill, spells need mana, and potions almost always work and can be carried in quantity. When: When choosing which reset type a player relies on. Why: Each failure mode changes when a reset is dependable.
- [pitfall] Persistent changes or lost key items can make progress impossible, such as a corpse blocking a route or a lost puzzle piece that never reappears; detect or prevent these states. When: Designing persistent levels, items, or runs that can lose key items or supplies. Why: A run that cannot finish is a designed failure, not only a player mistake.
- [pitfall] Dropping equipment at the death location invites players to rush back and retrieve it, which can be exploited. When: Designing item loss on death. Why: Players may take dangerous shortcuts to recover dropped gear.
- [pitfall] Losing persistence breaks the sense that the world is real and invites stair scumming. When: when a game drops world persistence. Why: players stop taking the world seriously and exploit regeneration.
- [pitfall] Roguelites often break roguelike principles by making early runs unwinnable, replacing generation with plain randomness, and offering pick-one-of-three upgrades that remove player choice.
- [pitfall] Repeated death and item retrieval can whittle down a boss, so resurrection systems must block loops that trade death for free damage.
- [pitfall] Games whose saves were converted by a version upgrade may be marked as edited, so tournaments may exclude them. When: Running competitive play across game versions. Why: Conversion alters the save, which looks like tampering to verifiers.
- [pitfall] Too many good events make the high wear off.
- [pitfall] A poor respec system makes a wrong early choice permanent, which frustrates players. When: a build system lacks adequate respec options. Why: Players cannot recover from a mistake made before they understood the trade-offs.
- [pitfall] Enemies that survive unusually many hits, or die to unusually few, make encounters less predictable and can undermine the risk curve. When: Tuning enemy hit points and damage variance. Why: Outlier survival breaks the expected risk curve players learned.
- [pitfall] Unbounded dynamism can drain the world; a system that spawns or moves entities without limits can depopulate the game space. When: A dynamic population or spawning system runs without caps. Why: Entities are consumed or removed faster than the system replaces them.
- [fact] Permadeath fits poorly in FPS games, which break all three suitability conditions, and RTS campaigns work better with branching missions, since reloading wastes work on off-path missions.
- [fact] Dead characters can keep gold and gear on the body so the player can retrieve them; a bank account or stash can offer backup gear. When: Designing death consequences. Why: Recoverable loot softens death without removing its stakes.
- [fact] Many genres use restart without permadeath, such as RTS maps with intra-level restarts and arcade shooters.
- [fact] Class and skill games rarely respec well unless death and respawn into a new class is part of the design, while inventory-based games allow flexible respec with suitable limits. When: choosing a respec approach for a game genre. Why: Inventory-based abilities can be swapped without restructuring the whole character.
- [fact] In information-complete roguelikes, threat rises faster than rewards with depth, so late play favours evasion over fighting. When: Games with full information and permadeath. Why: Rising enemy strength outpaces reward growth, shifting the best play from fighting to avoidance.
- [fact] Roguelike resets include teleporting self or others, destruction, reality alteration, healing and curing.

## character classes and roles
- [model] All combat answers three questions: the chance to hit, the amount of damage, and any secondary effect. When: Designing a combat system. Why: Every combat outcome is decided by these three answers.
- [definition] A character class defines common tendencies in attributes, the actions a character can take, and the equipment they can use. Why: Classes borrowed from tabletop RPGs shape how characters play.
- [definition] Ability acquirement has three main axes: currency, item systems and achievements, each carrying its own risks, especially grind and unequal ability value.
- [definition] A magic system is any game ability whose use is limited over time by a mechanic meant to balance it, such as mana, double jumps, sprint limits or fire rates.
- [behavior] Redirecting outside download requests to the home page briefly raised impressions and click rates, but these figures then stagnated. When: Trying to recover ad impressions from direct download traffic. Why: Routing downloads through pages exposes visitors to ads, though the gain does not last.
- [rule] Do not judge a class's balance from pick rates alone; option imbalance does not prove a class is broken, and novelty or bragging-rights effects can inflate short-term pick rates.
- [rule] Class choice should express a playing style, not a difficulty level, and difficulty should be handled through separate design choices. When: designing player classes. Why: Tying difficulty to class forces players to pick a style based on challenge.
- [rule] Protect each class's distinctive mechanic, since giving other classes similar buffs or alternative attacks dilutes that uniqueness. When: Adding items or abilities to a class with a signature mechanic. Why: Overlap makes classes feel interchangeable and weakens their identity.
- [rule] Class progression commits the player to an early decision that sets their available abilities.
- [heuristic] Give each class a distinct way of handling shared resources such as light, food, healing, mana and escape, so classes differ in how they solve the same problems. When: Designing multiple classes that share a resource-based survival system. Why: Distinct resource handling makes classes feel different and gives each its own strengths and weaknesses.
- [heuristic] A pet death costing only a small ongoing damage penalty is too weak; the loss should change stats more strongly. When: Designing companion death penalties.
- [heuristic] Use a build system when players should experiment and learn trade-offs; inventory-based ability systems suit this best. When: choosing between build systems. Why: Inventory swaps let players test options and learn their trade-offs.
- [heuristic] Keep starting spell counts small, such as 3 or 4 per tier, so the choice of a class or faction matters more. When: when designing starting spell sets for distinct classes. Why: Large starting sets make the choice less consequential.
- [heuristic] Compare pick rates between alternatives in the same slot; a strong split where one option dominates signals imbalance, while an almost even split suggests balance. When: Finding weak options within one slot.
- [procedure] 1) Restrict abilities to balanced sets, such as classes, skills or an inventory of abilities. 2) Let players learn each option's advantages and disadvantages early. 3) Make respec easy but limited so it cannot happen mid-encounter. When: designing a build or respec system. Why: Balanced sets with early learning and encounter-limited respec keep choices meaningful.
- [procedure] 1) Identify the class's mastery moments and check whether they are rare or unreliable. 2) Make rewarding them more reliable with changes that need no new art or features. 3) Test that high-level viability is unchanged. When: When a class has few reliable mastery moments and buffs risk breaking viability.
- [tradeoff] Human commander roles make results depend heavily on the commander's skill; AI commanders would level the field. When: Designing asymmetric team roles in blended RTS and FPS games. Why: Outcome quality then varies with the human player's competence rather than the design.
- [tradeoff] If classes become as narrow as each other in spell choice, the separate classes must be justified by other differences. When: when two classes share a similar spell system.
- [tradeoff] Classes bound spell alternatives and suit themed sets, while talent trees become too inflexible when many effects need prerequisites and exclusions. When: Choosing between class and talent tree progression for spells. Why: Prerequisite and exclusion networks become unwieldy in a tree.
- [tradeoff] Removing functionality is much harder than adding it, because code, art and player habits resist nerfs, so overshooting in either direction is costly.
- [pitfall] Some hit and damage methods do not combine well, so their combinations must be checked with judgment. When: Mixing hit and damage formulas. Why: Incompatible pairings produce broken combat.
- [pitfall] Keyword-driven ad networks can match unrelated products to genre vocabulary, such as love potion ads on a game blog. When: Running ads on pages that use game terms. Why: Ad matching follows words, not context, so genre terms can attract irrelevant advertising.
- [pitfall] If enemies scale as fast as the player grows, rewards feel purely cosmetic.
- [fact] Class choice means different things by mode: in multiplayer it complements a team, in single-player with several avatars it expresses a chosen role, and a lone avatar must survive alone. When: designing class systems for a given play mode. Why: The role a class plays depends on whether allies and other avatars exist.
- [fact] Intelligence governs accuracy, breakage of thrown or fired items, spells learned per level, device use, lock picking and trap disarming. When: Defining what the intelligence stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value across combat, magic and utility.
- [fact] Dexterity governs extra blows with light weapons, to-hit, lock picking, trap disarming and some casters' spell failure, and gloves can penalise casters. When: Defining what the dexterity stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value across combat and utility.
- [fact] Constitution governs hit points, recovery speed while resting, and mana, and drawing on extra mana risks health. When: Defining what the constitution stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value for survival and resources.
- [fact] Charisma governs shop prices, earlier monster fleeing, fewer ranged attacks and summons from monsters, and control of summoned and charmed monsters. When: Defining what the charisma stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value across economy and monster interaction.
- [fact] Agility governs speed and defence, and size governs hit points and heavy-weapon charge damage, with size above strength lowering agility by one per two points over. When: Defining what the agility and size stats affect. Why: Listing their effects clarifies how they interact with other stats.
- [fact] Free downloads mirrored by third-party sites starve a game's own pages of impressions, because direct file links bypass the pages that carry ads. When: Distributing a free game with ad revenue. Why: Direct download links skip the ad-carrying pages, so ads never load for those downloads.

## spell schools
- [definition] Light schools cover healing and protection from harm; dark schools cover weakening and harm.
- [definition] Conjuration casts area damage meant to kill monsters; summoning calls temporary allies that may not be friendly.
- [definition] Necromancy raises corpses as undead, in variants that either consume a soul or do not.
- [definition] Escape magic teleports self or monsters, changes speed, and opens portals; divination reveals the map, traps, monsters, and items.
- [definition] Transmutation changes creatures and items; enchantment binds properties to items and creatures.
- [definition] A class, or school, is a bounded set of spells that share a thematically consistent spell list, which limits which alternatives a player can take. When: Structuring spellcasting progression. Why: Bounding the set keeps alternatives balanced against one another.
- [mechanism] In a single-pool model, spells cost points from one resource, such as MP or SP, and casting is otherwise unlimited. Why: A single cost paces casting simply.
- [mechanism] In a slot-based model, spells must be prepared before casting, and casting usually consumes the prepared slot, so the number of prepared spells is capped. Why: Preparation forces deliberate loadout choices.
- [mechanism] Spellcasters store one spellbook per slot, and learning rate and mana regeneration further restrict how many spells they can use. When: A magic system ties spells to physical books in a slot-limited inventory. Why: Book slots are one limit; learning one or two spells per level and a slowly refilling mana pool add more.
- [mechanism] A random spell granted from a chosen book means that a narrow choice of book has little effect when the pool of valid spells is large. When: when a character chooses a spell book and receives a random spell from it.
- [mechanism] Classes that build on synergies within their own abilities, such as growing a tree that then entangles foes or heals, create tactical depth from combining their own powers. When: A class has several abilities that can interact with one another. Why: Combinations of abilities produce effects no single ability achieves, rewarding planning.
- [mechanism] Alternative spells add meaningful choice only when each covers a distinct function or does one function significantly better; otherwise one version supersedes the rest. When: Designing alternative spells or abilities. Why: Overlapping functions leave one option strictly dominant, removing real choice.
- [behavior] Players who risk self-damage from area spells tend to step back before casting them. When: Players using area spells with self-harm. Why: Damage risk makes distance the safer way to cast.
- [rule] Give each class a spell school with distinct strengths and weaknesses so its spell list reflects a playing style. When: Designing spellcasting classes. Why: Distinct schools make each class play differently rather than interchangeably.
- [heuristic] A spell with two functions, such as escape and tactical use, is near its best when its range balances both, since lengthening it helps one function and hurts the other. When: Tuning the range of a multi-purpose spell. Why: Each function prefers a different range, so extremes sacrifice one of them.
- [heuristic] Spell learning must be both stricter and more flexible to stay interesting. When: Designing how players learn spells. Why: Strict limits create choice, and flexible paths keep choices reachable.
- [procedure] 1) Count the spells in a class's starting book. 2) Split it into focused volumes by magic area. 3) Make slot trade-offs push players to concentrate on one area. When: a starting spell book is large and mostly offensive. Why: Focused books make players commit to a playstyle rather than cover every case.
- [metric] Choosing 3 items freely from 7 gives 35 combinations, whereas exclusive buckets of 2, 3 and 2 items give only 12. When: Comparing free slots against category-bound sockets. Why: Free slot picks multiply combinations, while category buckets cap them.
- [tradeoff] Easily swapped slots or spellbooks trade long-term commitment for flexibility, and if every alternative can be acquired early, the choice collapses. When: Slots or spellbooks can be changed freely during play. Why: Cheap swaps lower the cost of changing loadout, so commitment matters less; free access to every alternative removes the cost of choosing.
- [tradeoff] Restricting escape spells to specific classes or conditions gives other classes value from alternative tools, but overly inflexible restrictions can make one class the default choice. When: Designing character classes whose escape abilities differ in flexibility. Why: Flexible escapes dominate when they are unrestricted, so restricting them spreads power across classes, but excessive restriction can make one class too attractive.
- [tradeoff] Summoned minions handle most offence and defence, but their death costs mana or blood, tying power to the caster's resource pool. When: Designing minion-based magic. Why: Losing minions drains resources, creating risk to the summoning style.
- [pitfall] Illusions in many games are mostly ineffective, such as enemies that vanish when attacked or mirror images, which makes them weak choices.
- [pitfall] A spell drawback, such as missing invisible monsters, can be mitigated by alternatives that each cover a different class of target, if the player can learn several. When: Designing spells with blind spots. Why: Complementary alternatives cover each other's gaps.
- [pitfall] Making spells equivalent across classes can conflict with keeping each class's playing style consistent. When: Balancing spell lists across classes. Why: Equal power across classes can erase the distinct identity of each school.
- [pitfall] Warrior abilities copied from a mage's spell list, presented as a menu, turn the warrior into a re-skinned spellbook. When: when designing special moves for a melee class. Why: menu selection removes the physical identity of melee play.
- [fact] Spells may be cast by reading a scroll or book, praying for a holy effect, or casting from memory after learning. Why: Different sources of casting shape how magic is acquired.
- [fact] Spells are usually grouped into schools such as good, evil, clerical, arcane, conjuration, summoning, enchantment, necromancy, escape, divination, transmutation, and elemental types, each with a typical role. Why: Grouping gives each class a distinct role and toolkit and aids design and balance.
- [fact] Wisdom governs armour class, resistance to monster spells, and spell points per level for most casters. When: Defining what the wisdom stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value for defence and casting.

## character progression and talents
- [model] Class-based systems force early commitment, skill-based systems need to equate points across skills, and classless inventory systems let players try options as they find them. When: choosing a character progression model. Why: Each model distributes commitment and decision timing differently.
- [model] Strength, intelligence, wisdom, dexterity, constitution, charisma, agility and size each have several roles, so no stat is useful to only one class. When: Designing character stats that every class should value. Why: Multiple roles per stat spread the value of each stat across archetypes.
- [definition] Player progression is growing mastery of rules, implications, control and memory; character progression unlocks or changes game rules through in-game choices and actions.
- [definition] Slots hold any chosen item from a shared pool, while sockets are fixed positions that each accept one option from a category, such as one weapon or one ring. When: Designing equipment or progression limits. Why: The distinction determines how freely players can combine options.
- [mechanism] Rising costs deeper in a talent tree let players catch up on cheap skills later, making characters homogeneous mid-game. When: Designing talent tree cost curves. Why: Cheap skills remain reachable by everyone, erasing differences between builds.
- [mechanism] Synergies and prerequisites can narrow a talent tree to a few paths, so meaningful choice shifts to the tips of the tree. When: Designing talent trees with synergies and prerequisites. Why: Dependencies force most picks along a path, leaving real choice only at the end.
- [mechanism] When a class's only action is bumping into enemies, tactical variety must come from the weapons and enemies rather than from many abilities. When: when designing a melee class with few actions. Why: the attack is the only lever players have.
- [rule] Progression should raise the stakes, so a failure costs more as the player advances, rather than only making the game easier. Why: Rewards that only ease play can feel like a dressed-up reward loop.
- [rule] Make every starting or first-level option useful in some situation, so no early choice is consistently worthless. When: designing early-game abilities, spells or starting kits. Why: Weak early options stand out sharply in a finely balanced early game and make choices feel forced.
- [heuristic] Rework a weak option by adding a situational benefit that matters when the player is in trouble, even if the benefit is brief. When: an option is rarely chosen because its base effect is too minor. Why: A strong effect in an emergency makes the option worth holding even if its duration is short.
- [heuristic] Use abstract stats mainly to differentiate characters; in a game with one controlled character, show the concrete effects of items and rolls directly. When: designing character attributes for a single-character game. Why: Stats exist to tell characters apart, which matters little when there is only one character.
- [procedure] 1) Order the full ability pool roughly by power. 2) Show a window of N options. 3) Let the player pick from the window at set intervals. 4) Slide the window forward by a set distance, expiring earlier options and unlocking later ones. When: Building a progression of many options with limited simultaneous choice. Why: Keeps each decision small while spreading the full pool across the whole game.
- [metric] In one example, a 10-ability window with a 2-of-10 pick every four levels across roughly 120 abilities yields about (10×9)^12 possible choice sequences. When: Estimating combinatorial variety of a windowed progression. Why: Small per-decision choice sets compound into very large total build variety.
- [tradeoff] Talent trees restrict choice with prerequisites, exclusions, or a pooled minimum of points, while skills offer choices more often; the two differ mainly in how frequently the player chooses.
- [tradeoff] Window size and slide distance are independent parameters: a window can hold more or fewer options than the distance it advances each slide. When: Designing a sliding window progression. Why: Tuning the two separately controls both how many choices are visible and how fast the pool is consumed.
- [tradeoff] Sockets offer more meaningful choice but can have degenerate cases where a talent tree performs better; sockets are generally easier to design. When: Choosing between socket and talent tree progression. Why: Sockets give cleaner choice and easier design at the cost of rare degenerate builds.
- [tradeoff] Mages have teleport for escape, while warriors lack an easy escape and must carry many escape items. When: when designing escape options across classes. Why: without a built-in escape, the class depends on consumables.
- [pitfall] Progression can become a solution looking for a problem, so decide what each progression type is meant to solve before adding it.
- [pitfall] Stat thresholds that change effects at non-intuitive values force players to memorise tables rather than reason about their character. When: a stat has break points that alter outcomes. Why: Players cannot predict thresholds that have no clear meaning.
- [fact] Skills resemble open progression, but each skill can still scale, and some abilities may unlock only at high skill levels.
- [fact] In a typical talent tree, skills are rarely exclusive, so choosing one first versus another makes little difference and choices reduce to ordering decisions. When: Evaluating talent tree progression. Why: Non-exclusive options let players catch up later, so choices lose meaning.
- [fact] Strength governs melee damage, weapon weight limits, tunnelling, carrying capacity, thrown and sling range, and crossbow firing speed. When: Defining what the strength stat affects. Why: Listing its effects clarifies its value across combat and utility.

## hunger and food
- [model] Hunger implementation requires choices about its lower limit, its upper limit, what raises it, whether eating is automatic or explicit, and how much of the counter the player sees. When: Implementing a hunger system. Why: Each choice changes how hunger feels as a time limit.
- [model] Hunger can serve as a resource to manage, a time limit, or realism, and each use has trade-offs. When: Deciding the purpose of hunger in a game. Why: The purpose determines how hunger should behave.
- [mechanism] Hunger as a time limit creates urgency: players must search deeper for food, cannot rest indefinitely, and are driven forward through the level. When: Using hunger or a food clock to pace play, such as in a roguelike. Why: Limited food forces progress and prevents endless resting; the timer keeps the player moving.
- [mechanism] Magic items or skills that reduce hunger in the mid and late game stop starvation from being a constant problem. When: Designing progression for a hunger system. Why: Later-game tools reduce food pressure as players grow stronger.
- [mechanism] Predators that hunt prey at nearby resources, and after enough kills attack the prey's home, make pressure on prey escalate over time. When: Designing predator-prey systems. Why: Repeated losses trigger a shift from raiding to direct assault.
- [mechanism] Food prevents starvation, and corpses often count as food; limiting food supply challenges the player to manage resources.
- [mechanism] Treating hunger as a resource like HP, MP or gold makes min-maxing easier by removing the time pressure. When: Deciding whether hunger should be a managed resource. Why: A resource is spent and optimized rather than running out on a schedule.
- [rule] Hunger needs an upper cap when it controls healing or intrinsics, because removing the cap removes the need to manage food. When: Designing hunger tied to healing or intrinsics. Why: Without a cap, players can stockpile food with no cost.
- [rule] Use explicit eating when intrinsics are granted by food. When: Designing food that grants permanent or semi-permanent abilities. Why: Players must choose what to eat when food has lasting effects.
- [rule] Use invisible hunger only in atmospheric games or games where hunger only heals. When: Deciding whether to hide hunger from the player. Why: Hidden hunger that can kill surprises players who did not see it coming.
- [rule] Avoid making food a plain instant heal, since it adds yet another way to heal. When: Designing food effects in a game with other healing sources. Why: Redundant healing options dilute the value of other healing resources.
- [heuristic] Choose the purpose of eating before deciding how hunger works. When: Starting design of a hunger system. Why: Purpose determines the mechanics, so mismatched mechanics fail the design goal.
- [heuristic] Lethal hunger is managed like HP, while non-fatal hunger effects make players lose any sense of time to live. When: Choosing whether hunger can cause death. Why: Non-fatal penalties remove the urgency that makes hunger matter.
- [procedure] Reclaim persistent resources by removing or dissolving objects the player left behind, such as removing abandoned vehicles or corpses. When: when persistence would clutter the world. Why: removing leftovers prevents blocked routes and clutter.
- [procedure] 1) Give gifts as the first payoff. 2) Then food theft. 3) Then meat. 4) Then fruit. When: Ordering rewards for stealing from rivals. Why: Ordering payoffs by value sets clear incentives.
- [tradeoff] Letting hunger bottom out at zero, rather than tracking a negative value, weakens the time limit but is easier to represent. When: Choosing the minimum value of a hunger counter. Why: Negative values keep pressure on the player but are harder to display.
- [tradeoff] A roaming threat in deep levels can make the risk of searching for healing outweigh the risk of the final fight. When: Designing deep levels with a roaming dangerous enemy. Why: Resource hunting exposes the player to danger that can exceed the boss fight's risk.
- [pitfall] Hunger that interrupts an exciting task can feel petty. When: Placing hunger penalties within play. Why: Interruptions that feel unfair disrupt engagement.

- [fact] Eating can prevent death, allow healing, allow regeneration, enable special actions, grant intrinsics, and add atmosphere. When: Listing what eating can do in a game. Why: These functions define what food contributes to play.

## roguelike genre
- [model] Traditional roguelikes rest on five mutually reinforcing aspects: permanent consequences, character-centric play, procedural content, turn-based play, and clean runs. They are not a strict checklist. When: Judging how closely a design matches the traditional style.
- [model] Three interdependent elements define roguelikes: randomized levels, emergent gameplay and permanent death, each depending on the others.
- [model] Roguelike skill develops in two phases: discovering emergent play, then trading exploitation of resources against evasion of risk. When: when analysing how players grow in a roguelike. Why: players first find options, then weigh resources against risk.
- [definition] High-value roguelike factors are random environment generation, permadeath, turn-based play, grid-based world, non-modal actions, item and monster interaction complexity, resource management, hack-and-slash player-versus-world combat, and exploration with unidentified item discovery.
- [definition] Low-value roguelike factors are a single player character, monsters following player rules, tactical challenge beyond early knowledge, ASCII display, rooms-and-corridors dungeons, and visible character numbers. Why: These factors are listed as less essential, so they are not required for a roguelike.
- [definition] Linear progression grants abilities in a fixed order, no progression grants every ability at the start, and open progression lets the player take abilities in any order.
- [behavior] Most genres carry player skill from one run to the next, while roguelikes make the skill being learned less obvious. When: when comparing roguelike learning to other genres. Why: permadeath resets progress so the learned skill is harder to see.
- [rule] Include mid-game constraints such as time and resource limits, mutually exclusive choices, irreversible decisions, one-way gateways and checkpoints with minimum standards. When: Designing the middle of a game where plans must matter. Why: Constraints make plans testable and give choices weight.
- [heuristic] An endless mode can serve players who want no limits, but it should sit beside a goal-driven mode rather than replace it. When: Deciding whether to offer an endless mode. Why: Goal-driven play needs its own structure to give depth.
- [heuristic] Endings that break the game's own rules for the final boss tend to feel explosive or unexpected, though they risk feeling unfair. When: Deciding how a game concludes. Why: Rule-breaking surprises players, but only works if foreshadowed.
- [heuristic] A roguelike whose progression runs out of new content benefits from a defined endpoint rather than extending indefinitely. When: When new items, spells or enemies stop appearing at higher levels. Why: Without new toys, the game loses its reason to continue.
- [heuristic] Treat long-term survival of a game as a design goal to check against from the start, not as a feature added later. When: Designing a game intended to be played for years. Why: Properties that keep a game alive are hard to add after rules are locked in.
- [heuristic] Lean on genre conventions, such as a starting magic missile spell, when players already share those expectations. When: Players are familiar with a genre's common conventions. Why: Experienced players recognise familiar conventions immediately, reducing the need to explain them.
- [heuristic] A run with a fixed number of levels, such as a 50-level ascent, gives a clear pacing structure from start to finish. When: designing the length and progression of a roguelike run. Why: A known endpoint helps players judge progress and pace their play.
- [procedure] 1) Start with improvised weapons such as branches, iron bars, knives, and chains. 2) Introduce ranged weapons as the player reaches populated areas. When: Low-tech scarcity settings.
- [tradeoff] Roguelites add changes such as real-time play or meta-progression, and whether a change is critical is subjective, so many such games are still labelled roguelikes.
- [tradeoff] A third vertical axis enables flying, falling, climbing and rooftop play, but it makes distance, spotting and display harder to handle. When: Considering adding height or elevation to a roguelike's core activities. Why: Height changes how enemies reach the player and how the player sees the map.
- [pitfall] A genre definition broad enough to cover everything stops being useful for studying a shared set of features.
- [fact] Most games are played only a few hours; competitive titles lasted about 8 and 12 years, while poker, chess and Go have lasted about 100, 2,500 and over 4,200 years. When: Comparing how long competitive games remain played. Why: These reference points show the range between short-lived and ancient games.
- [fact] Roguelikes lean heavily on play as a game and role-play, and offer little room for watching or story appeal beyond item and quest discovery. When: Assessing what a roguelike offers players. Why: The genre's core loops emphasise rules and world interaction over presentation.
- [fact] Other roguelike themes include steampunk, a supernatural Wild West, cosmic horror, and a satirical dystopia of hidden computer control.
- [fact] Common mid-game failures include treadmills, grinds, false economies, fool's gold, mudflation, and gear reversals that strip all equipment. When: Reviewing the mid-game design of a game. Why: Each pattern makes progress feel empty or wasted.

## player choice and decisions
- [mechanism] Positive reinforcement, such as feeling right or dominant over a poor-choosing opponent, shapes choices more strongly than negative reinforcement, which reminds players of alternatives and causes frustration. When: when designing feedback for a chosen option. Why: positive feedback confirms a choice, while negative feedback highlights what was given up.
- [behavior] Fewer available choices leave a player with no answer when a situation calls for one, which makes survival harder. When: designing the option set for a class or build. Why: Missing options remove the means to respond to threats.
- [behavior] Players can keep engaging in a session even when the rewarded activity has no meaningful goal, so session length can rise with a reward-only task. When: An optional, unrewarded hunt is added to a social game. Why: Reward-based repetition keeps players active even without a meaningful goal.
- [behavior] Spoilers rarely ruin well-designed games, because depth comes from play decisions rather than hidden rules; games with fully visible rules stay engaging. When: well-designed games with fully visible rules. Why: Enjoyment depends on choices within known rules, not on surprise.
- [behavior] Players in single-player games feel an internal observer, the moral censor, that intervenes when a choice conflicts with their values. When: A player makes a moral choice in a single-player game. Why: Internalised values act like an audience, making choices feel personal.
- [behavior] Self-imposed limits such as iron man rules, vows or challenge runs give moral weight because the player sets them. When: Players choose their own constraints. Why: Constraints the player chose carry personal commitment that imposed rules lack.
- [behavior] Push payoffs tend to produce a sense of wonder from narrative resonance, while pull payoffs tend to produce satisfaction from operant gratification.
- [behavior] Players feel guilt when a choice affects a named character who remains in the world, such as an ally they can heal or an enemy they can leave to die. When: a choice affects a named, persistent character. Why: Named characters and visible consequences make the outcome personal rather than abstract.
- [behavior] Players facing a decision feel doubt and look for evidence that they chose well, so choices need moments that confirm them. When: when a player has picked among options. Why: decisions create uncertainty that players want resolved.
- [behavior] An option that is not competitively viable can still be widely chosen if using it gives frequent confirming feedback. When: when an option is weak at high level but gives repeated positive moments. Why: each confirming moment reinforces the pick.
- [rule] Maximise decision density by removing downtime and trivial decisions, presenting information clearly, and using adaptive animations and auto-explore to cut wasted time.
- [rule] Anything presented as a choice must be a genuine choice, not an illusion of one.
- [rule] Reduce random spell assignment when the player's choice should have a clear effect on the character. When: when a choice of spell group should matter. Why: Random grants dilute the effect of the player's choice.
- [heuristic] Good rule spaces tend to contain prisoner's dilemmas, risk-versus-reward choices, and unpredictability from randomness or opponents. Why: These structures create meaningful decisions for players.
- [tradeoff] Every player choice needs its own balancing; limiting choices to cosmetic effects lowers design cost but makes the choices less interesting.
- [pitfall] Class and skill choices made early are hard to reverse, which can trap players in unsatisfying builds. When: designing progression with early commitments. Why: Players commit before they know the costs and benefits.
- [pitfall] Full-disable effects such as sleep, paralysis and stun leave almost no margin between harmless and fatal, and paralysis with no warning carries the highest lock risk.
- [pitfall] An in-world teaching event is missed unless its conditions are clear to the player. When: Presenting an ability unlock as a world event. Why: Players who do not recognise the event's availability will not trigger it.
- [pitfall] A choice that is only punished, with no positive moments, creates frustration rather than validation. When: when an option has drawbacks but no rewarding moments. Why: players get reminded of alternatives without any confirmation.
- [fact] Fun has two layers: immediate fun from presentation and novelty, and long-term fun from replayability and sound design. Presentation-heavy games lean on the first; roguelikes depend almost entirely on the second.
- [fact] Classes make one early decision, talent trees make a few spaced decisions, and skills make frequent incremental ones.

## game design principles
- [model] Freedom in roguelikes has four forms: creative freedom with no wall between player and developer, simulationist freedom where the world reacts to unusual actions, self-set rules with strict or relaxed options, and no gatekeeping, often free and needing little hardware.
- [model] Every game mixes four aspects of play in different proportions: play as a game with rules, play as role-play in a world, play as watching, and play as sport with competition. When: Analysing which aspects of play a game emphasizes. Why: Different aspects appeal to different players and need different design support.
- [definition] Failure in games covers three distinct kinds: losing a game, falling into rote play after the logic is understood, and failing to learn. When: Analysing why players fail or disengage. Why: Each kind has a different cause and needs a different design response.
- [definition] Games are defined by play and interaction with rules and feedback, whereas art conveys ideas and can be passive. When: Distinguishing games from other creative media. Why: Rules and reward-and-punishment feedback set games apart.
- [mechanism] Games evolve, breed and die; a design can only evolve if it is played, since a game that is not played cannot be refined.
- [mechanism] Emergent play comes from many verbs and interactions, such as throwing a carried object or combining many player actions. When: when designing for emergent gameplay. Why: more verbs create more possible interactions.
- [behavior] Once a player has mapped a rule set's logic, decisions become mechanical, and rule sets with a single solution collapse into a drawn or fixed outcome. When: Designing rule sets with few viable strategies. Why: Full knowledge of the logic removes meaningful choice, as with a solved game such as tic-tac-toe.
- [behavior] Expressiveness comes from many permutations of a few interactions; water, for example, can douse fire, clean an altar or create a wave, from many sources. When: Designing a small set of interactions. Why: Combining a few interactions across many sources yields varied outcomes without new mechanics.
- [rule] Design for the player you want: permadeath players and steady-progress players are distinct groups, so build for one and avoid fighting save-scumming. Why: hidden rules make save-scumming an easy workaround.
- [rule] Levelling should not make a character better at a specific action; instead it should grant luck, health or other accruable benefits. Why: A level 10 character should match another level 10 character at backstabbing, spellcasting or swordplay.
- [rule] When a removal hurts teammates, invert it into a narrower restriction that keeps support abilities intact. When: a feature removal is being considered. Why: Narrow restrictions preserve team support.
- [heuristic] Themes can be combined, but some combinations clash.
- [heuristic] Progression types can be mixed, for example a class granting some abilities linearly and others through a talent tree or skills.
- [pitfall] Suddenly and artificially restricting the rules, such as a boss immune to abilities the player has used freely, feels like a designer prison. When: Introducing new limits late in a game. Why: Players feel that the rules changed unfairly after they invested in an approach.
- [pitfall] Twists that change game mechanics can leave players stuck, so mechanics should be revealed in advance to keep players able to progress. When: when a story twist alters how the game plays. Why: players cannot adapt to a mechanic they have not been told about.
- [pitfall] A period after migration with little pressure removes the sense of threat when only one group faces a food timer. When: Designing time pressure across groups. Why: Uneven timers leave some groups with no urgency.
- [fact] Emotional weight in a procedural narrative can come from moment-to-moment mechanics such as fire, weather, AI and gun jamming rather than the story.

## weapons and item design
- [model] First-person weapon behaviours include hitscan, projectiles, physics weapons, and placements; any of these can add splash damage that falls off with range from the impact point.
- [definition] An item is an object with one or more uses triggered by a character's actions.
- [definition] Slots let abilities fill freely, sockets limit each item to matching socket types, encumbrance caps the total number of abilities, containers fit shaped items into 2D grids, and paper doll views show valid placements.
- [mechanism] Rarer items spread out learning, and unidentified items keep the player learning over time. When: when designing item distribution in roguelikes. Why: scarce and unknown items give the player new things to discover.
- [rule] Items should change how the player plays, so that the first item found can shape the run. When: Designing item effects. Why: Players value items that alter strategy over minor stat gains.
- [rule] Apply object-owned abilities only when the item is used, not to all of the player's attacks, so dual-wielded items do not affect each other's attacks. When: Designing dual-wield or multi-item combat. Why: Dual wielding shows that a dagger's damage should not apply to sword attacks.
- [heuristic] If guns are limited, make stealth worthwhile and account for gunshot noise. When: Settings with scarce firearms.
- [heuristic] Give items a specific counter-role that answers an identified frustration, rather than adding generic power. When: Designing items for a multiplayer class-based game. Why: Targeted items address a known problem and are easier to justify than general power.
- [heuristic] Treat coolness and utility as separate axes, and judge items on both rather than on one alone.
- [heuristic] Check that every item or build path offers a recognisable moment that confirms the pick. When: when designing weapons, items or build paths in a dungeon or roguelike. Why: players need confirmation that a pick was worthwhile.
- [tradeoff] Capping item types or equip slots forces prioritisation, so each option carries real weight, even when the cap has no realistic basis. When: Setting an inventory slot limit or designing progression with a large option pool. Why: A limit that forces prioritisation makes each option count. Scarcity of equip slots makes each option carry real weight.
- [tradeoff] A carry limit can create a river-crossing puzzle; splitting the item into parts with inventory trade-offs gives the player a choice instead of a failure state. When: a quest item is too heavy or bulky to carry with everything else. Why: Splitting converts a hard blocker into a meaningful inventory decision.
- [pitfall] Automatic grenade throws and scripted doors reduce player choice, even when they simplify control. When: designing throwables or doorway interactions. Why: Removing player-chosen timing and aim takes away meaningful tactical decisions.
- [pitfall] A wrong weapon choice is wasted effort, so the effect of weapon matchups must be visible to the player. When: when designing weapon matchups. Why: players who cannot see the effect cannot learn the correct choice.
- [fact] Item categories follow the game's theme: classic fantasy uses potions, scrolls, wands, rods and staves, while sci-fi uses guns, canisters, floppy disks and similar equipment. Why: Categories serve different functions depending on theme.
- [fact] Amulets are usually more valuable than rings, since rings modify attributes or grant powers while amulets are rarer.
- [fact] Brands add extra damage from an element such as fire, cold or acid, while slays add extra damage against a monster race, and monsters may resist or be immune to them. When: when designing weapon damage bonuses against monsters. Why: these bonuses let weapons match different enemies.

## combat damage and attacks
- [model] A critical hit can add a status effect, an instant effect, or a second critical roll instead of only multiplying damage. When: Designing critical hits for a roguelike. Why: Crits can create tactical events beyond extra damage.
- [model] Combat compares an Attack Rating, built from stats, class, level and equipment, against a Defense Rating; a hit lands when attack rating plus a random roll exceeds defense rating. Why: Comparing two ratings with randomness gives a simple hit model.
- [definition] An attack shape is the volume an attack damages, and its behaviour is how that volume changes across game ticks.
- [definition] Hit points are a single scalar pool that damage reduces; a target at zero or below hit points is killed and removed from play.
- [mechanism] A character who attacks an unaware monster with a blade from a hidden position earns a large damage multiplier, with no class or skill check required.
- [mechanism] An attack meter that builds with melee damage grants extra blows against targets other than the primary opponent; resting resets it and heals the character. When: Rewarding melee play in crowds. Why: Ties sustained melee damage to bonus attacks on secondary enemies, encouraging engagement with many foes.
- [rule] Split damage bonuses by context, such as shooting, setting traps and melee, so one bonus does not apply where it should not. When: Designing item damage bonuses. Why: A single bonus applied to all uses can let thrown items out-damage the weapons they belong to and double-count the bonus.
- [heuristic] Shapes that can hit the caster, allies or useful items rank lower, while controllable shapes without drawbacks rank higher; the most effective shapes should carry drawbacks such as placement limits or forced proximity.
- [heuristic] Use hit points as the base damage model and layer multiple pools or status effects on top only when needed. When: When designing combat damage. Why: Simple base models are easier to tune, and complexity can be added later.
- [heuristic] Effects that apply instantly to awake targets are more useful than effects with a delay before the target succumbs. When: When comparing status effects with and without delays. Why: Delays give the target time to react, reducing the effect's value.
- [tradeoff] Hit points avoid the perpetual conflict of tug-of-war models and the early endings of incapacitation values, while adding no penalty as the pool falls. When: When choosing a base damage model. Why: Gradual incapacitation creates positive feedback loops that can end a game before anyone is defeated.
- [tradeoff] Direct line-of-sight damage suits ranged casters with fast attacks, while indirect damage suits casters who set up traps and minions, needing more complex setups. When: Choosing a damage model for a spell school. Why: Direct and indirect damage reward different playing styles and setup effort.
- [tradeoff] Binary status effects block an action outright, while decremental effects let it occur at reduced effectiveness; decremental effects are simpler to balance. When: choosing how a status effect modifies an action. Why: Reduced effectiveness avoids the all-or-nothing states that make balancing hard.
- [pitfall] Letting monsters heal the player would reduce the attack meter, a counter-intuitive link that may confuse players. When: Designing the attack meter's interaction with healing. Why: The reduction runs against the usual expectation that healing is beneficial.
- [fact] Instant-effect crits can disarm, damage or destroy weapons or armor, knock back one tile, swap positions, prevent the next attack, or grant a free action or speed bonus.
- [fact] Damage can be instantaneous or over time, and can use elements for resistances and shapes for areas of effect, all on a hit point base.

## status effects and resistance
- [definition] A lock is a state in which combined status effects keep an enemy from acting effectively, so no effective action is possible. When: status effects are combined on one target. Why: Locks remove the opponent's ability to respond, breaking the intended contest.
- [mechanism] Applying oil to a monster and then striking it with fire while it is vulnerable gives an easy kill.
- [mechanism] Making a resistance fail a percentage of attacks, with a warning, ties feedback to individual attacks and rewards gambling on a full-strength hit. When: Designing resistances that should stay readable. Why: Each attack gives a clear outcome instead of a hidden multiplier.
- [mechanism] Excess application of a milder status effect overflows into a more severe one, so repeated impairment escalates in a graded way. When: a status effect is applied beyond its own limit. Why: Overflow creates a ladder of impairment instead of a single abrupt state change.
- [rule] Only petrification, fear, blindness and amnesia fully disable alone; other effects need combinations or overflow. Make incapacitating effects partial, leaving consumables usable, so recovery stays possible. When: designing or classifying effects that disable actions. Why: Partial effects leave the player room to act, keeping the game recoverable.
- [rule] Illusion damage should apply only while the target has a specific status, such as sleep, so that status effects are central to the caster's identity. When: When designing a caster whose role centers on status effects. Why: Conditional damage rewards setting up effects over raw attacks.
- [procedure] 1) Lock all of a monster's ability types: melee, ranged and spell. 2) Hold the locks for a sufficient duration. 3) Confirm no unlocked allies can see the fight. Then the monster surrenders. When: defining when a monster gives up without hit point damage. Why: Surrender by status needs every route to attack disabled, not only one.
- [tradeoff] Use element differences only when they are meaningfully exploitable; if all elements are equivalent, the elemental system adds nothing. When: Choosing whether to add elemental variety. Why: Equivalent options give the player no decision.
- [tradeoff] Status locks are broad attacks that need several effects on one target, while damage attacks are deep, repeatable against one vulnerability. When: choosing between status-based and damage-based combat. Why: Breadth and depth trade off in how many effects must be combined versus how many vulnerabilities must be found.
- [pitfall] Overlapping status effects such as stun and paralysis blur together unless each is clearly defined. Why: Players cannot tell apart effects that look alike.
- [pitfall] A vulnerability-finding minigame that grows too large shifts the game from gore toward mystery.
- [pitfall] Stacking by a broad item tag, such as all swords, may tip the balance too far toward simplicity and remove meaningful differences. When: Simplifying inventory stacking by item category. Why: Broad grouping merges items that players should still tell apart.
- [pitfall] An effect that can only be removed by an item the player cannot reach may cause permanent incapacitation. When: an effect's only removal method is unavailable to the player. Why: With no reachable cure, the incapacitation is effectively permanent.
- [pitfall] Hallucination is a weak lock component, even if it blocked ranged attacks, so it should not be relied on to lock a monster. When: considering hallucination as part of a lock. Why: Its contribution is too small to count toward a reliable lock.
- [fact] Status-effect crits deal normal damage and add an effect such as stun, bleeding, paralysis, slowed movement from a leg hit, or blindness from an eye hit.
- [fact] Games vary from binary modes, to percentile tables, to few common elements where creatures are either resistant or not, with rarer elements unpredictable. When: Comparing resistance designs. Why: Each point on the spectrum trades legibility against depth.

## trap, puzzle and movement
- [definition] A trap is a deliberate sequence of player actions that locks an enemy for a short time, as opposed to a lock that emerges from status effects.
- [behavior] Risky traps with tempting rewards let the player choose to fight and sometimes die. When: Players face an optional dangerous reward. Why: Tempting rewards make risk a voluntary choice.
- [rule] Give the player a push-past move that swaps places with an adjacent enemy, bindable to the run command, with dodging also applying to it. When: Designing melee movement that rewards engaging many enemies.
- [rule] Give each lock several paths, so immunity to one effect does not prevent the lock. When: designing status locks against immune targets. Why: A single path makes immunity to that effect decisive.
- [heuristic] Use event objects when complex interactions span game time, or when delays, logging or interception are needed.
- [heuristic] Escalate multiplayer puzzle rooms by adding new tools, hazards and needs one at a time, so each room adds a distinct trust or cooperation problem. When: Designing a sequence of multiplayer rooms.
- [heuristic] Push an ability's core idea as far as it can go, for example cold freezing water so it can be crossed or blocks swimming monsters. When: Expanding an ability beyond its obvious effect. Why: Extreme applications of one idea create memorable play.
- [procedure] 1) Make the enemy a serious threat. 2) Let the trap sharply reduce its threat. 3) Let the player deduce the trap as a puzzle. 4) Execute it with the timing the audience suits. 5) Remove or reduce the enemy once solved. When: When designing traps against computer enemies. Why: Solved traps become skill tests and lose their puzzle value.
- [tradeoff] Sharing a standard core set of movement moves across military shooters aids quick learning and skill transfer, but may weaken a game whose movement should be distinctive. When: Designing movement for military first-person shooters or deciding which movement features are mandatory. Why: Standard required features reduce distinctiveness, while optional features keep it.
- [tradeoff] In multiplayer a trap creates a guessing game against a human opponent, while in single player against a computer it works as a puzzle rather than a bluff. When: When designing traps for multiplayer versus single-player games. Why: A computer opponent has no theory of mind, so it cannot be bluffed.
- [tradeoff] Push or kick mechanics need command-based input rather than a simple bump, which can pull a roguelike back toward menu-driven play. When: when adding push or kick actions to a bump-based game. Why: the extra action needs its own input.
- [pitfall] Using traps to punish a player's behaviour in single player usually produces learned helplessness. When: When designing single-player traps.
- [pitfall] A weak fast action can bait an opponent's interrupt, letting a trap lock them when the interrupt's duration exceeds the time to apply it. When: interrupts can stop another action. Why: Predicting an opponent's interrupt lets the setter exploit the timing gap.
- [fact] Puzzles are almost entirely pull-based: given this set of tools, how does the player reach the payoff, with little backstory needed.

## design process and methods
- [model] A revised community consensus organises open-ended roguelike design into four areas: freedom, immersion, a new experience every time, and decision density. Some of these aspects deliberately conflict. When: Using the principles as a design checklist. Why: They fill gaps left by the original 2008 consensus.
- [definition] An event system represents in-game actions as objects that can be queued with a delay, logged, intercepted, modified or dropped.
- [behavior] Once an emergent property is known, it becomes a line in a community FAQ. When: when a roguelike has been played widely. Why: players document discovered interactions.
- [rule] Use the community consensus as shared vocabulary, not a constraint: missing some listed factors does not disqualify a game as a roguelike, and having some does not qualify it. Why: It was written to describe the community's features, not to restrict design.
- [rule] Keep variables independent; a single attack serving both air and ground targets is hard to tune, and separate attacks fix this at the cost of extra art and animation.
- [rule] Store game content in editable data so players can extend the game without learning code or scripting. Why: Players can add content without breaking the game.
- [rule] Design the ending of a roguelike first, and give the game a simple goal that drives play toward a conclusion, like checkmate in chess. When: Planning the overall structure of a roguelike. Why: A clear conclusion gives every earlier choice a direction.
- [heuristic] Most games need about five core data types: hero, foes, arena, collectibles and status changes; hero and foe may be merged into one. When: Designing the data model of a game. Why: A small set of core types keeps the system manageable.
- [heuristic] Incremental improvement is a basic feature of games, so the design question is where the reward is delivered, not whether it is delivered. When: Designing progression systems. Why: Improvement is expected by players, so the delivery method is the main design choice.
- [procedure] 1) Gather many concrete examples. 2) Play with the data until it fits together. 3) Develop the gameplay from the resulting data.
- [tradeoff] Event objects make control flow more complicated but can make multi-turn interactions easier to describe.
- [pitfall] Critics call the community consensus inaccurate and outdated, arguing that ASCII and dungeon layouts are irrelevant to a genre that prioritises mechanics over aesthetics.
- [fact] The 2008 community consensus lists roguelike factors in high-value and low-value groups, naming five canon games. It is a reference, not a strict definition. Why: Provides shared vocabulary for studying the roguelike genre.

## player skill and testing
- [model] Difficulty comes from seventeen sources, including randomness, external forces, other players, risk trade-offs, finite time, self-limitation, asymmetry, learning the rules, incomplete information, incorrect information, interface, skill disparity, complexity, emergence, and play and pleasure. When: when varying how hard a game feels. Why: difficulty is many factors, not one dial.
- [definition] The path to victory is the minimum number of actions or amount of time needed to win, and a damaging attack shortens it. When: measuring how quickly a player can win an encounter. Why: Comparing lock effort against this minimum shows whether a lock is worth pursuing.
- [behavior] Players disagree on difficulty and progression: some want permadeath-free progress, some want tight difficulty, and some prefer minimal character advancement. When: Gathering feedback on a roguelike design. Why: Audiences hold different goals for the same game.
- [rule] Play as sport needs a clear win condition, score comparison, and challenge modes such as ironman. When: Designing competitive or challenge play. Why: Competition requires measurable outcomes and comparable results.
- [rule] When the aim is to test ability rather than persistence, use arenas, puzzles, skirmishes or mini-games, which measure skill more directly with fewer boundary conditions. When: a challenge should test player skill. Why: These formats have fewer hidden assumptions that can break the measurement.
- [rule] Change only the element that needs it; treating similar elements the same way may break their roles. Why: Other elements serve different roles and would be damaged.
- [rule] Mechanics that teach rather than entertain belong only in games meant to be played once, and not in games played by children.
- [rule] Let suspension of disbelief outweigh clean, regular code when an ability must feel believable. When: Choosing between elegant code and an effect that feels right to players. Why: Players judge abilities by believability, not by code structure.
- [heuristic] Moments of lost player control, such as scripted movement or automated actions, are among the weakest parts of a game and should be minimised. When: designing scripted sequences or automation. Why: Players want agency, and removing it reduces engagement.
- [heuristic] Give the game a visible endgame landmark, since the endgame gives meaning to the mid game and its presence shapes every mid-game decision. When: Designing the long-term structure of a game. Why: A distant goal makes mid-game choices feel purposeful.

## ability lock mechanics
- [mechanism] Melee lock requires fear with petrification; ranged lock requires terror alone; spell lock requires amnesia with blindness or terror with daze. When: designing locks that disable a monster's ability types. Why: Each ability type has a specific effect combination that disables it.
- [rule] Prevent locks by keeping actions immune to status effects or giving repeated application diminishing returns, or by keeping a damaging attack and reset always available or shortening combined durations. When: designing status effects or incapacitation systems that can combine. Why: Without an unaffected action or diminishing returns, combined effects can incapacitate indefinitely; a lock is only dangerous when it blocks all damage and all recovery at once.
- [heuristic] Full-disable effects like sleep suit games built around capturing enemies, and they fit computer games poorly.
- [heuristic] Small hacks change one behaviour, such as fire burning and cold freezing; large hacks may need a new supporting system before the ability feels interesting. When: Implementing an ability that needs a distinct effect. Why: Some effects depend on systems that do not yet exist.
- [heuristic] Monsters with fewer ability types are easier to lock, since fewer locks must be achieved for surrender. When: choosing which monsters can be status-locked. Why: Each additional ability type is another lock that must hold at once.
- [procedure] 1) List each status effect and its minimum duration. 2) Check whether applying each in turn keeps the target incapacitated until the earliest effect expires. 3) If so, a lock exists. When: checking whether a set of status effects can create a lock. Why: Sequential application can cover the full duration window without a gap.
- [tradeoff] Lock risk may be ignored when lock effort equals or exceeds the path to victory; but when effort roughly matches killing, a lock is a viable alternative for status-based casters. When: assessing whether a lock is a design problem; a caster class relies on status effects rather than damage. Why: A lock costing as much effort as simply winning gives no real advantage; when the lock's effort matches the kill effort, both are viable paths to victory.
- [tradeoff] Removing upgrades from all buildings for faster building makes every building unupgradable, while restricting only one building keeps upgrades for teammates' buildings.
- [pitfall] Locking doors until all monsters are defeated removes much of the roguelike fun. Why: It removes the tactical freedom that generated maps are meant to allow.
- [pitfall] Locked-in rule fundamentals and a steepening training curve make new characters harder to build over time, causing problems later. When: A game is designed to last many years with ongoing player investment. Why: Early rule choices constrain later changes, and growing entry costs push players away.
- [pitfall] Cumulative effects can lock a player even when each effect alone is safe. When: several status effects can be applied to one target. Why: Combined durations and overlaps can produce a state no single effect causes.
- [fact] Detecting failed states is easy when a game over follows, but subtle failed states may be very hard to detect.

## ability design and unlocks
- [model] Abilities belong either to the player, applying whenever the item is equipped, or to the object, applying only while the item performs its task. When: Designing item abilities. Why: Ownership determines whether an ability is inherited by the wearer or applied only during use.
- [model] Ability choice types define which abilities a player may take and in what order. Options include linear, no progression, open, skills, talent trees, and slot or socket systems, and they can be mixed.
- [rule] Grant abilities to any player who performs the matching action rather than gating them behind a class or skill, so design effort reaches every player and item. Why: Class-gated abilities help only a fraction of players, and gating frustrates players whose play style the gate does not suit.
- [rule] Each attack or defence should have a counter: magic users need limited mana, archers run out of ammunition, and summoners must not overwhelm the player.
- [rule] Give each magic ability its own code path so it changes play in a distinct way, even when this reduces code reuse. When: Designing abilities that must feel different to the player. Why: Shared code paths make abilities feel similar, weakening their identity.
- [rule] Reserve inventory slots for mandatory abilities such as food for survival and light for seeing in unlit areas. When: Designing a slot-limited inventory where some abilities are required to keep playing. Why: Without food or light the player cannot survive or see, so these must be carried.
- [rule] Deliver discrete abilities as in-world events, such as an enemy offering to teach its secrets in exchange for sparing its life, rather than as experience-point unlocks. When: An ability is a discrete unlock that can be presented as a world event. Why: In-world delivery keeps the player immersed and avoids reward rules outside the game world.
- [heuristic] Carry escape abilities such as teleport, since a character can be killed in one turn on arrival at a new level. When: Random level entry can drop a player into lethal danger immediately. Why: Immediate lethal danger leaves no time to respond, so an instant exit is needed.
- [procedure] 1) Start with a limited set of abilities. 2) Require mastery of them before unlocking more. 3) Optionally mix with open exploration. When: Games that can combine teaching with exploration. Why: Gating keeps early play manageable.
- [pitfall] Action-based abilities push design toward equipment loadouts and inventory management, and a single ability can force costly changes elsewhere, such as line-of-sight-dependent waking and multi-route levels.
- [pitfall] Using one ability name in two different ways makes balance and comparison harder. When: Naming and defining item abilities. Why: Different meanings for one name confuse balance and comparison.

- [fact] Healing and detection spells are close to mandatory for a priest-like class; without a starting healing spell, the character dies quickly. When: when designing starting kits for support classes.

## rule systems and design
- [model] Three RTS-style strategies map to tribe types: a turtle (social), a rush (military) and a tech strategy (industrious), where gifting can serve as a third attack route. When: When designing traps for multiplayer games. Why: A safe counter would make the trap useless against a predicting opponent.
- [heuristic] In team games, prefer designs that support teammates over those that reward individual prowess, such as abilities that expose enemies or deny areas.
- [heuristic] Seek a repeatable, random and entertaining alternative to combat for social encounters. When: designing non-combat encounters in roguelikes.
- [heuristic] Single-player design should prepare players for multiplayer, since high-level play tends to emerge months after release. When: when designing single-player content for a game that will later have competitive play. Why: advanced play develops slowly after launch.
- [pitfall] Recruitment timing games with no feedback on the opponent's peak, and with near-free retries, are almost impossible to lose and offer little challenge. When: Designing timing-based mini-games. Why: Missing feedback and cheap retries remove the cost of failure.
- [fact] Multi-avatar games vary: the player may control avatars directly, recruit them into a party, or control units indirectly through progress.

## quests and bounties
- [model] A quest with no tangible item reduces to a Go To quest; Go To and Do Something are the two atomic actions from which all quests are built. When: classifying or decomposing quest designs. Why: Every quest can be expressed as combinations of these two actions.
- [definition] An atomic action is a single unit of player input whose effect is complete in itself, such as firing through movement rather than clicking each target.
- [mechanism] A bounty quest, such as killing n targets, silently assumes the targets exist, can be found, cannot be lost to other causes, the evidence is countable, and the reward can be verified. When: designing kill-count or collection quests. Why: Each hidden assumption can break the quest if it fails, so it must be guaranteed or deliberately varied.
- [behavior] Quests that count actions rather than successful outcomes measure determination more than skill. When: a quest asks the player to perform an action a number of times. Why: Completion depends on persistence, since the count does not reflect how well the action was done.
- [heuristic] Keep repeated kill quests interesting by making each target harder than the last, for example by placing tougher targets further away. When: a quest repeats the same action many times. Why: Identical repetition quickly becomes tedious.
- [fact] A fetch-item quest rests on around fifteen hidden assumptions, including that the item cannot be dropped, sold, destroyed, found elsewhere, or be useful to the player. When: designing item-retrieval quests. Why: Ordinary-object behaviour of the item can violate these assumptions and break the quest.

## save and reload
- [mechanism] Save games capture progress that may hide critical mistakes, whereas a full reset guarantees all players learn the same lessons. When: when choosing between save games and permadeath. Why: saves let players skip the consequences of their mistakes.
- [rule] Do not allow savescumming, which is backing up saves to undo outcomes, because players widely see it as cheating.
- [procedure] Allow a single quicksave only when no enemies are visible and none know the player's location; delete or overwrite it when loaded. When: when players may leave at any time. Why: limits save-scumming while keeping a suspend option.
- [tradeoff] Save-reload undoes death but breaks identification and hidden information, while resurrection in the same world allows irreversible choices but can enable exploits.
- [pitfall] Unlimited save-reload lets players learn unidentified items and deeper contents, which destroys identification mechanics.
- [fact] Save files in roguelikes are either deleted on loading, acting as a pause, or deleted on death.

## character control and customisation
- [definition] Player characters are controlled by the player, while non-player characters are controlled by the computer through action selection, often called artificial intelligence.
- [mechanism] Controlling one character strengthens player attachment and raises the stakes of permanent consequences.
- [mechanism] A gift improves one party's view of the giver, or worsens a rival's, but never fully allies them; neutral is the best outcome. When: Designing diplomacy with gifts. Why: Capping gift effects prevents diplomacy from trivialising conflict.
- [pitfall] Creature customisation where most options have no effect on play is criticised, since players notice that creative choices do not change gameplay.
- [fact] The most common character progression unlock is a new ability; existing abilities can be scaled up, and abilities can be removed by capture or near-death.

## monster design and naming
- [definition] A monster is any non-human being a character can interact with, traditionally shown as a letter.
- [heuristic] The same naming template can read as cute or ferocious, so check the tone a name conveys before using it.
- [procedure] 1) Choose a naming technique: well-known creatures for small sets, exotic letter mixes for weird creatures, constructed languages, borrowed natural-language words, or adjective plus noun. 2) Add variety with double adjectives, suffixes like 'of the Deep', or double nouns. 3) Generate names from word lists and keep the usable ones as design starting points. Why: Names guide the monster's design and set its tone.
- [procedure] 1) Take a name as a starting point. 2) Define its appearance, movement and attacks. 3) Give it intelligence, spells and senses. 4) Set its group behaviour, such as appearing in flocks. Why: Turns a name into a complete monster design.
- [pitfall] Overly long monster names make game messages hard to read.

## monster effects and rewards
- [mechanism] Positional triggers fire a special move from movement patterns, such as two steps in one direction for a charge, sideways movement for a dodge, or standing still for a block. When: when designing melee special moves tied to position. Why: movement becomes a way to select abilities without menus.
- [fact] Killing monsters is the usual way to gain experience, and monsters are the main source of treasure, sometimes carrying inventories or dropping items.
- [fact] Basic monster status effects include wound, poison, armor-penetration, stun, paralysis, sleep, fear, confusion, possession, slow, grab, silence, blindness, amnesia, hallucination, seduction, and charm. Why: A status catalogue supports designing varied monster threats.
- [fact] Monster item effects include curse, disarm, corrode, acid, rot, burn, and disenchant. Why: Item damage gives monsters threats beyond hit points.
- [fact] Terrain and position effects include traps, darkness and light, forced movement, teleport, switching places, and phasing out. Why: Position effects alter where the player is and what can reach them.

## moral choice design
- [rule] Every moral choice needs a game system that represents it, since a choice cannot exist without one. When: Designing player moral choices. Why: Without a system that reflects the choice, the player has nothing to respond to.
- [heuristic] Use conduct tags that the player can observe and understand. When: Choosing behavior constraints for characters or monsters. Why: Hidden or obscure constraints cannot guide player choices.
- [heuristic] Design game systems that use moral labels like any other system, with interesting options and no dominant path. When: Building an in-game moral system. Why: A dominant path lets players optimise the choice away.
- [heuristic] A choice without in-game consequences can feel personal, because the player's real-world values carry its weight. When: Designing moral choices with little mechanical reward or punishment. Why: Real-world values, not game rewards, give the choice its weight.
- [pitfall] Calling design rules moral conflates bad design with immoral design, and their force depends on what designers believe games are for.

## turn-based combat timing
- [mechanism] Turn-based play gives unlimited time to think, so the challenge shifts to choosing the best move rather than acting quickly.
- [metric] Total turn choices equal the number of non-equipment commands plus each way each inventory item can be used. When: measuring how many options a player has each turn. Why: Counting options shows how much decision space a build or class offers.
- [pitfall] Too many action-dependent delays can make the turn order hard for players to follow.
- [pitfall] Changes to turn timing are delicate because turn sequence is central to a roguelike's balance. When: when altering timing of actions in a turn-based roguelike. Why: turn order shapes the whole balance.
- [fact] Turn-based games can support more status effects than real-time games, because players have more time to recognise each one. When: comparing status effect counts across game genres. Why: Extra decision time lets players process more distinct effects.

## narrative and setting
- [heuristic] Twist the setting with a distinct feature, such as an unknown randomly generated land, or monsters that are kind, indifferent, or hostile.
- [procedure] 1) Choose the setting and theme first. 2) Check that weapons, enemies, and tech level fit it. When: Starting a roguelike concept. Why: A setting determines what weapons and enemies are plausible.
- [pitfall] Without deep content, mood events become repetitive and lose impact.
- [fact] Narrative's main function is to keep players playing by telling them what to do next and reminding them what they have already done.
