# game-ai

## combat tactics and defence
- [model] Tactical monster patterns include hit-and-back, run-and-gun, entrenching, trap-setting that uses other monsters, and alarms that notify superiors. When: Brainstorming monster tactics.
- [mechanism] Status attacks such as blinding, poisoning, confusion or disarming change nearby monster strength and can let monsters affect each other through the player. When: Designing status effects that spread between monsters.
- [mechanism] After a move, a character automatically dodges, so missiles may target the square left behind and sideways steps reduce area-effect damage to splash; after standing still, the character blocks, raising armour class and doubling shield effectiveness. When: deciding whether to move or hold position in combat. Why: Movement and stillness each grant a different defensive bonus for the next turn.
- [mechanism] A monster faster than its target gets two attacks per target turn, but in large battles scaled hit points and damage quickly erode this speed lead. When: when a faster monster fights a slower target. Why: scaled combat stats reduce the value of extra attacks.
- [behavior] Monsters that move only diagonally and retreat from orthogonally adjacent enemies are hard to hit yet easy to avoid, so a chase can turn into a trap for the pursuer. When: Designing evasive creature movement. Why: Their movement rule and retreat behavior make pursuit inefficient.
- [behavior] An illusory attack against a monster immune to that element can cause it to notice the attack and wake, revealing its immunity. When: When an illusion targets an enemy that is immune to its element. Why: The monster reacts to the attack even though the damage is ineffective.
- [behavior] Adjacent enemies can block directed ranged attacks aimed at the player, and area attacks aimed at the player damage the adjacent enemies too. When: A player fights in a crowd of melee enemies. Why: Surrounding monsters act as physical cover and take collateral damage, so ranged attackers are less effective against a surrounded character.
- [rule] Telegraph enemy plans before they act via speech or visible cues (e.g., announcing a grenade throw, flagging the player's hiding spot); report actions afterward, as hidden actions feel unfair. When: when the player must understand enemy intent. Why: telegraphed plans make enemy actions readable and fair.
- [heuristic] Players value tactical combat such as ambushes and terrain use over simple repeated attacks. When: Designing combat encounters. Why: Tactics give players meaningful choices.
- [heuristic] Judge a path's safety by modelling future effects, such as a monster's mana regeneration before it crosses a symbol area. When: A pathfinder must decide whether an area is safe to enter. Why: Current state alone can misjudge danger when conditions change before the crossing.
- [heuristic] Give monsters that a dominant brand would trivialise more immunities, and let some monsters use that element against the player. When: when a brand outclasses the designed counter for a monster group. Why: counters keep the weapon matchups varied.
- [procedure] 1) Use straight and diagonal directions only, numbering facing 0-7 clockwise in 45-degree steps. 2) Subtract facing from attacker offset and take modulo 8. 3) Take absolute value: 0-1 is front, 2 is flank, 3-4 is rear. Why: Integer arithmetic classifies attacks by relative direction.
- [procedure] With free angles, compute atan2 of the attack direction and classify using thresholds at pi/4, 3*pi/4 and pi; C lacks a float modulus, so a float modulo helper is needed. Why: Continuous directions need angle thresholds instead of integer steps.
- [procedure] 1) Detect that a monster is adjacent to its target and the target acts before the monster's next turn. 2) Slightly increase the monster's best range. 3) Let the monster step away. When: when a monster is adjacent to a target that will act before it. Why: stepping away lets the monster avoid being hit while staying in range.
- [pitfall] Enemy AI must guard against all indirect attacks, which is harder than guarding against direct ones.

## monster fear and emotion
- [mechanism] Fear/love and hate centers are blended toward each entity by the ratio of the smaller to the larger absolute total, so a lone entity pulls the center onto itself while several entities pull it between them. When: Computing the center point a creature moves toward or away from. Why: Weighting by relative totals makes a single source dominate and groups balance out.
- [mechanism] A monster switches to escape behaviour when its hit points fall below a fear threshold, and the threshold drops as nearby allies increase; mindless creatures have a very low threshold. When: Deciding when a monster flees. Why: Monsters with allies are braver, and mindless creatures rarely flee.
- [behavior] A psychological effect can install a new AI, for example a panic effect giving a goblin a flee AI. When: Designing status-driven behavior change.
- [rule] Mindless creatures assign zero fear/love to friends and enemies, so social feelings never drive their movement. When: Scoring entities for a mindless creature.
- [rule] Rate movement by totals: wander at low priority when fear, love and hate are zero; move toward friends at normal; flee at highest when fear exceeds hate; otherwise attack the hate center at high. When: Choosing a creature's movement action. Why: Fleeing outranks everything, so a frightened creature retreats before other needs.
- [heuristic] Invest in monster fleeing behaviour, because a fleeing monster that stops advancing exposes weak AI; adding bleeding lets players track a wounded monster out of sight. When: Monsters flee from the player. Why: Once monsters stop pressing forward, their poor decisions become visible to the player.
- [procedure] Aggression for a direction equals (own strength plus friends' strength) times (6 minus enemy distance), plus twice basic aggression and special hates. Fear per enemy equals its perceived strength times (6 minus distance), plus special fears, stored as a move away. When: Scoring monster motives from nearby enemies. Why: The 6 minus distance term makes only nearby monsters matter.
- [procedure] 1) Score each perceived entity for fear/love and hate. 2) Remember the most feared, most loved and most hated entities. 3) Give each creature perceivedStrength (strength times current over maximum HP) and perceivedDanger (from equipment). When: Each perception update for a creature.
- [metric] For instinct-driven creatures, fear/love equals 1000 times the other's strength divided by own strength, divided by distance; the sign is positive for friends and negative for enemies. When: Instinct-driven creatures scoring another entity.
- [metric] For mind-driven creatures, the 1000-point fear/love budget splits 500 to strength and 500 to danger, so equipment changes how much an entity is feared or loved. When: Mind-driven creatures scoring another entity.
- [metric] Hate toward an entity of a different race equals 1000 divided by distance; toward the same race it is zero. When: Scoring hate for perceived entities.
- [tradeoff] A larger negative fleeing coefficient makes monsters prefer distant safety and pillar-dance, while a small one leaves them trapped in corners. When: Tuning fleeing monsters with an inverted map. Why: The coefficient sets how strongly distant cells dominate the gradient.
- [pitfall] Pits and traps should affect Aggression and Desire but not Fear, or frightened monsters walk around them and get caught. When: Monsters near hazards. Why: Fear-driven movement avoids hazards it should be drawn toward.
- [pitfall] Packs of friends that love each other too much may never move, because their mutual love center stays near them. When: Groups of friendly creatures with strong mutual love. Why: The love center stays at the group, so no move action wins.
- [pitfall] Gravity-style pulls that move the player through walls can leave them stuck, so limit such pulls by distance and frequency. When: Designing pull or area effects that move the player. Why: Forced movement can place the player where no path leads out.

## pack and herd behaviour
- [mechanism] Local rules let blocked monsters step diagonally at no extra cost, forming a wide front; a single added push flag yields similar group formations without global coordination. When: Monsters need to move as groups on a grid or in an encounter. Why: Local movement rules produce group shape without formation code, because local rules interacting across individuals create coordinated-looking patterns.
- [mechanism] Each creature emits sound that spreads outward and weakens one ring per tile of distance; creatures moving toward stronger same-species sound gather into herds. When: Making creatures form groups without explicit leaders. Why: Local sound gradients pull creatures toward nearby kin.
- [mechanism] Talking monsters wake their neighbours, while stunned or cut monsters cannot talk. When: when designing monster communication. Why: speech spreads awareness and is disabled by status effects.
- [behavior] A creature with a herding tendency herds only on some turns, letting it break away from the group when it hears no kin. When: Creatures that herd intermittently. Why: Skipping herd logic on some turns lets a creature wander off once no kin are heard.
- [behavior] Swarmers wait just outside the player's light radius until they gather into a group, then attack together. When: Designing group-attacking monsters. Why: Waiting until grouped makes the attack coordinated.
- [behavior] Pack monsters advance only when enough free squares exist around the player to make the player vulnerable. When: when designing pack monster movement. Why: advancing into cramped ground gives the pack no advantage.
- [rule] Ignore the player's sound when judging herd membership; only same-species sound should count. When: Computing herd cohesion from sound. Why: Mixing in the player's sound would pull creatures toward the player rather than their group.
- [rule] Assign one shared AI object to several creatures to make them act as one pack, only when the AI holds no per-creature internal data. When: Making groups behave together. Why: Shared state across creatures breaks when each needs its own data.
- [heuristic] A cheap call for help from a surviving enemy can make nearby rooms seem to hold late reinforcements, without sending any backup. When: when simulating reinforcements cheaply. Why: the player assumes nearby soldiers are arriving late.
- [procedure] 1) Sample a limited number of random neighbouring squares. 2) Score each by path distance minus weighted same-species sound. 3) Move to the best-scoring square. When: Choosing a creature's move while herding. Why: Sampling a few candidates keeps per-turn cost low while still favouring kin.
- [pitfall] Regenerating sound every turn is costly with many creatures, so caching recent sound is a useful optimisation. When: Running sound-based herding with large populations. Why: Rebuilding the sound map each turn repeats work that changes little.
- [pitfall] Checking sound on the creature's home square is needed; otherwise creatures can keep their distance from each other instead of gathering. When: Implementing sound-based herding. Why: Without the home-square check, the scoring rewards separation rather than cohesion.
- [pitfall] Monster groups can clog passages or hit with area attacks that harm the player and allies, which can frustrate players. When: Designing groups of monsters in narrow spaces.
- [pitfall] Pack vulnerability rules can break the principle that monsters should advance to stay interesting, so they are often criticised. When: when applying pack-based target weighting. Why: monsters that hold back may feel passive.

## monster behaviour styles
- [model] Monster actions can be chosen by weighted ratios across four categories: movement, escape, attack and defense. A roll over the summed weights picks one slot. When: Dungeon and wilderness monsters where a simple probabilistic choice is enough. Why: Weighted random selection gives varied, tunable behaviour with little code.
- [mechanism] A chase recalculates its path occasionally, more often as the target nears, and immediately whenever the target shifts position since its previous refresh. When: Pursuing a moving target. Why: Close and moving targets change the correct path quickly.
- [behavior] Monsters that hate light close in when no light source is present and flee until outside the light radius when one is present. When: Designing light-sensitive creatures. Why: Light sensitivity flips their approach from attacking to fleeing.
- [rule] Give each monster a behaviour suited to its abilities: weak melee monsters stay at range, melee monsters can open or bash doors, and most monsters have a ranged attack.
- [heuristic] Keep speed differences fine-grained when pursuit or escape must feel tense, such as running from same-speed monsters or catching a fleeing player.
- [procedure] 1) Patrol until an intruder is seen. 2) Estimate the risk of an attack. 3) Compare risk with an adjustable aggressiveness. 4) Chase only if the risk is acceptable. When: Guard behaviour toward intruders.
- [procedure] 1) Store a movement pattern string per townsperson with a current index and a scheduled time. 2) Each loop, when the clock reaches the scheduled time, set the routine flag. 3) While the flag is set, execute the next letter and advance the index. When: Giving townsfolk predictable daily routines. Why: Time-triggered scripted steps create routines with little code.
- [procedure] 1) Let a monster push another in a corridor. 2) Flag both monsters. 3) Block pushing a flagged monster until its next move starts. When: Monsters can push past one another on a grid. Why: Without a guard, monsters in a corridor swap places forever, and repeated pushes give free moves.
- [tradeoff] Thief creatures steal weapons and hoarded items in their den, while stalkers keep distance and hide when watched. When: Designing special creature archetypes.
- [tradeoff] Scripted patterns are predictable and cheap to write, but they replay the same route and do not adapt to the player. When: Choosing between scripted townsfolk routines and adaptive behaviour. Why: Fixed routes cannot respond to what the player does.
- [fact] Type 0 monsters move randomly two steps per player turn in any open direction and suit open rooms with some corridors.
- [fact] Type 1 monsters step one grid per turn toward the player and stand still when no closer step exists.
- [fact] Type 3 monsters move two steps per turn, always turn right at intersections, take two hits to kill, and suit mazes with loops.

## ai architecture and composition
- [model] Each creature holds one AI object whose action method is called on its turn; behavior changes by replacing that reference. When: Structuring monster AI for runtime behavior changes. Why: Swapping objects avoids large switch statements and supports changing behavior during play.
- [model] Event objects suit a publish-subscribe AI, in which AI actors receive and react to event types.
- [definition] A go-to module computes a path to a position once and then moves one step per turn along it.
- [mechanism] Components let a generated monster or item receive traits from a mix of parts rather than a fixed class hierarchy. When: Generating monsters or items with varied traits. Why: Mixing components creates combinations a fixed class tree would not allow.
- [behavior] First-generation monsters choose spells at random, which keeps them in character, and otherwise move toward the player's direction or the two flanking directions. When: Monsters with simple pursuit logic. Why: Simple direction-based movement is cheap and gives reasonably in-character behaviour.
- [rule] Use N, S, E and W to move, H to hold for a turn, and X to end the pattern and reset the index. When: Writing townsperson movement patterns as strings. Why: A compact code keeps routines short and easy to edit.
- [heuristic] A precomputed library of good opening moves can tame the complexity of early-game decision making in strategy games.
- [procedure] 1) Write small AI classes behind one abstract interface with a single step method, each for a single behavior such as patrol, attack, lurk or flee. 2) Wrap or chain them, for example sleep handing over to another AI or a sequence switching after a set time. 3) Assign the result to the creature; a separate controller executes the moves. When: Building complex monster behavior from simple parts when many creature types share building blocks. Why: Routines can be recombined without changing the creature itself.
- [procedure] 1) Actor begins preparing an action and joins a watchers list. 2) Each time another actor acts, watchers are notified. 3) Watcher either lets the preparation continue or cancels it, moving the actor to the front of the queue to choose a new action. When: Implementing interruptible actions in a queued turn system. Why: Lets actors react to events during a multi-step action.
- [procedure] 1) Check whether an enemy is seen and choose a movement ratio. 2) Check the fear threshold for escape. 3) Choose attack or defense using ratios. 4) Wander when no other check applies. When: Ordering the decision checks of a ratio-based monster. Why: The check order changes which behaviour wins, so it must be fixed.
- [pitfall] A regular turn pattern enables pillar dancing and hack-and-back tactics, which an AI could also use. Why: Fixed turn ratios make the timing of attacks and retreats predictable.

## ranged combat and ammunition
- [mechanism] Allied ranged units attack any target in line of sight, while allied movement routines move toward the closest enemy grid. When: Designing allied units that fight alongside the player. Why: Splitting attack and movement lets allies shoot anything visible while still closing on enemies.
- [mechanism] A ranged unit ignores a closest target nearer than its best range and moves toward the player's position or targeted grid instead. When: Ranged allies near enemies inside their minimum distance. Why: Stepping back to preferred range keeps ranged units from fighting at melee distance.
- [mechanism] Each enemy beyond two within two grids of the player reduces the chance of ranged attacks, by roughly 10% per enemy as a first estimate. When: Tuning ranged attack chance against a crowded melee character. Why: Crowd cover scales with the number of adjacent blockers, so a character surrounded by eight monsters is largely safe from ranged attacks; the figures are untuned guesses.
- [behavior] Huge monsters resist non-charge attacks and are vulnerable to heavy weapons; they attack out to range 2 and often do not follow, leaving the player room to charge back in. When: designing large monsters that attack at range. Why: Their reach and reluctance to follow create gaps where a player can safely close in and charge.
- [heuristic] Units keep a best range from their targets: about 6 for archers and 1 for warriors, so each type stands at its preferred distance. When: Setting approach distances for melee and ranged allies. Why: Preferred distances let each unit type fight from where its attacks work best.
- [procedure] 1) If a neighbour uses the same ammunition, average the amounts. 2) If you need your ammunition, give half your inventory. 3) Otherwise give everything. When: Monsters in a group share ammunition with neighbours. Why: Repeated local averaging and conditional giving spread supplies so group members hold roughly equal ammunition.
- [pitfall] If ranged attacks count as useful work, archers can block melee monsters in corridors. When: Group AI values ranged attacks as useful work. Why: Archers stay in place and occupy the corridor that melee monsters need.
- [pitfall] Archers follow the player into battle when no grid is held, and targeting a hold grid keeps them back. When: Ranged allies without a set position. Why: Without an anchor point, ranged allies chase the player into melee.
- [fact] Type 2 monsters never move; after the player has been in their line of sight for two or more turns, they fire at the player's previous position for one damage, and they suit cave-like mazes with pillars.
- [fact] Type 6 monsters move once per turn, retreat when the player is within four grids and approach otherwise, fire spells like type 2, and pass through doors.

## scent and sound tracking
- [model] Instead of a per-tile field, each mover leaves scent objects that grow in radius and lose intensity each turn, and monsters follow a trail they detect above their own smell threshold. When: Per-tile scent maps cost too much but a trail effect is enough. Why: Trail objects are cheaper than updating every tile.
- [mechanism] Heat spreads only from the newest frontier, so a player can slip back into already-searched areas unnoticed. When: Shared heat search with frontier updates. Why: Previously spread tiles no longer emit heat.
- [mechanism] Each tick the player's scent is added to their tile, then each open tile takes the average of itself and open neighbours, minus a decay percentage, so scent spreads and fades. When: Monsters track the player through a per-tile scent map. Why: Averaging spreads scent to neighbours while decay limits how far it reaches.
- [mechanism] Sound is applied instantly: the source tile gets its volume, and each step outward gives surrounding open tiles one less, until zero. A monster is alerted when a tile's value exceeds its attention threshold. When: Monsters hear noise from fights, actions or traps. Why: Volume falls with distance through open tiles, so walls and corridors shape what is heard.
- [rule] Cap the growth of scent left by stationary characters at about 150 percent, otherwise a resting character can fill the map with scent. When: A character leaves scent while standing still. Why: Uncapped growth from a stationary source accumulates without limit.
- [heuristic] An alerted monster steps to the neighbouring tile with the highest scent, or moves directly toward the player if it can see them. When: A monster is following scent rather than sight. Why: Scent gradients lead toward the source of the trail.
- [procedure] 1) When the player is seen, reset the heat and place heat on the player's tile. 2) Each turn, spread heat from the newest frontier to walkable neighbours at the player's maximum speed. 3) Remove heat from tiles any searching monster can see. 4) Pathfind to the nearest heated tile. When: Guards must locate a hidden player without knowing the exact position. Why: Spread rate matched to player speed keeps the probable area covering the player; if heat spreads slower than the player can move, the heat can miss the player, since the probable area must grow as fast as the hidden target can move.
- [procedure] 1) Each turn, grow every scent object's radius by one. 2) Multiply its intensity by 0.8. 3) Remove it when intensity drops below 1. When: Updating expanding scent objects each turn. Why: Growth spreads the trail while decay ensures it eventually disappears.
- [procedure] 1) When idle, sniff at each pause for scent objects within their radius. 2) If a scent is strong enough, add a follow-scent behaviour above the current one. 3) Revert when the trail fades or the target is not reached within a set number of turns. When: A monster should follow a detected trail. Why: Temporary override lets monsters pursue without losing their normal behaviour.
- [pitfall] Per-tile scent maps are slow: ten iterations per turn on an 80x50 map was noticeably slow on an old machine. When: Updating scent on every tile each turn. Why: Each update touches every open tile, so cost grows with map size and update rate.

## motive and desire drive
- [model] Monsters score every direction by summing five weighted motives, Aggression, Fear, Desire, Wander and Misc, into one movement vector. When: Monsters need distinct personalities and group behaviour. Why: Weighted summed motives produce varied actions without per-monster scripts.
- [mechanism] Need-driven monsters run a three-step loop each turn: observe and score perceived entities for fear, love and hate; rate every candidate action by priority; execute the highest-rated action. When: Designing many creatures with different fear and hate profiles from one shared system. Why: Separating perception, scoring and execution lets one system produce cowards, pack hunters and kamikazes.
- [heuristic] Single-player AI should be designed to lose in an entertaining, readable way rather than to win, so the player feels they won by outplaying it. When: AI whose role is to be beaten in single-player play.
- [procedure] 1) Keep one distance map per desire, such as player, treasure, food, water or sounds. 2) Give each monster a coefficient per desire. 3) Move to the neighbour with the highest weighted sum. When: Monsters with distinct personalities sharing one map system.
- [procedure] 1) Give each monster a weight per behaviour type, with zero meaning ignore. 2) Look in all 8 directions. 3) Compute motive values per direction. 4) Compute all monsters' values before any moves. 5) Normalize the final vector and pick the strongest motive as the action. When: Each monster turn in a group-behaviour system. Why: Computing all values first keeps groups from splitting on order of movement.
- [procedure] Desire for an object equals its value plus (6 minus distance) plus basic desire plus particular desire, with values added rather than multiplied. When: Scoring monster attraction to objects.
- [tradeoff] A need-driven system suits instinct and mind creatures and gives varied fear and hate profiles; mindless creatures ignore social feelings and need a simpler model. When: Choosing an AI architecture for a roguelike monster set.
- [fact] The motive-weighted approach aims for the illusion of interesting behaviour rather than perfect AI.

## action scoring and priority
- [definition] Action priorities use a fixed scale: none 0, low 50, normal 100, high 200, highest 800. When: Rating candidate actions in the need-driven AI.
- [mechanism] Giving goals different starting values, such as negative for valuable items and zero for the player, makes actors weigh targets by desirability rather than raw distance. When: Several goal types with different importance. Why: Lower starting values pull the gradient toward those goals more strongly.
- [rule] Open a closed door adjacent to the creature at normal priority, and pick a locked door adjacent to it at normal priority. When: A creature is next to a closed or locked door.
- [rule] Set an action's weight to zero whenever its precondition fails, so the monster cannot pick an impossible action. When: A possible action depends on a condition such as available mana. Why: A non-zero weight lets the roll select an action that cannot be performed.
- [procedure] 1) Give each candidate action a rate() value. 2) Run the action with the highest rating. 3) Call its execute() method. When: Every creature decision step. Why: A single highest-rating choice keeps urgent needs ahead of routine tasks.
- [procedure] 1) Sum the weights of the slots. 2) Draw a random integer from one to the total. 3) Add weights into cumulative thresholds. 4) Return the slot whose threshold the draw falls under. When: Choosing one action from a set of weighted options.
- [pitfall] Fixed priority numbers and the 500/1000 scoring weights are tuned values that need balancing before the system behaves as intended. When: Adopting a fixed priority scale or weight scheme.

## ally control and formation
- [model] Players command allies in four ways: target a monster to approach it, target a race to prefer it, hold a grid, or assault a grid so troops ignore nearby combat. Escape cancels the order. When: Designing command systems for allied units. Why: Using targeting and grids avoids separate ordering commands while still giving direct control.
- [mechanism] Monsters flagged to take items pick up whatever lies in the grids they enter, so allied archers reuse ammunition and allies may eat food or corpses to recover wounds.
- [behavior] Melee allies charge and spread across the width of a fight until most are engaged, while archers stop about 6 to 8 grids away and shoot. When: Allied squads entering a battle. Why: Spreading melee units engages the enemy broadly, and holding archers back keeps them shooting safely.
- [rule] Allies should act sensibly without orders, such as moving aside when the player walks into them and firing at what the player is shooting.
- [heuristic] A fuller orders system becomes necessary as battles grow turn-based and tactical, letting the player select part of a force or relay orders through a unit. When: turn-based tactical combat with allied forces.
- [pitfall] Summoned allies that leave service after a timeout take their carried items out of play unless they drop them first.
- [pitfall] Friendly AI at close range causes friendly fire and confusion when allies and enemies look similar beyond a few metres. When: allies and enemies share a crowded close-range space. Why: Players cannot reliably tell friend from foe when they are close together.

## hidden enemy visibility
- [rule] Remove a hidden enemy's model from view rather than rendering it with artefacts, since artefacts reveal enemies more easily than intended; games rarely model noisy environments. When: An enemy is hidden from the player. Why: Visual artefacts in models and environments can reveal hidden enemies more easily than intended.
- [rule] Make spotting chance depend on elevation difference between observer and target. When: Implementing stealth or detection in a game with multiple height levels. Why: Height difference changes how easily a creature can be seen.
- [procedure] 1) Hide the enemy model fully when it qualifies as hidden. 2) Reveal it through a separate detection mechanic, such as removing the conditions that permit the cloak. 3) Optionally show a partial model, or let detector abilities reveal it fully or partly. When: Designing stealth or cloaked enemies.
- [tradeoff] Full hiding prevents any betrayal of the enemy, while partial models, such as transparent versions with water or blood effects, reveal signs of presence. When: Choosing how to show hidden enemies. Why: Partial models betray the enemy, so use them only when the player should still see signs of presence.
- [pitfall] Stepping through a narrow gap can break line of sight to the goal, so steps should keep part of the line of sight open. When: Moving a monster through gaps while using direct steps toward a visible goal. Why: Losing sight of the goal forces the monster back into wall following.
- [pitfall] Monster behaviour the player cannot see or hear effectively did not happen, so unseen AI adds no value outside stealth games. Why: The player never registers the action.
- [pitfall] Realistic AI the player cannot perceive may just read as difficulty rather than as intelligent behaviour. When: when enemy behaviour is hidden from the player. Why: players cannot distinguish smart play from unfair play.

## emergent ai and simple rules
- [mechanism] A gambit system lets players set rules of the form 'if in this state, do this action', automating routine choices while keeping control of the rules.
- [behavior] Monsters that advance, cast or shoot at the player are perceived as intelligent, because threatening actions are visible and responsive. When: Players observe monsters attacking or approaching. Why: Visible pursuit and attack read as deliberate intent to the player.
- [heuristic] Prefer good-enough local decisions over global optimal search, leaving route finding to operations research methods. When: when designing monster decision making. Why: local decisions are cheaper and often sufficient for play.
- [heuristic] Start monster AI with simple rules, observe the emergent behaviour, and modify only where needed, because complex behaviour from simple rules is easier to debug. When: Designing monster behaviour in code. Why: Simple tests choosing among a range of actions are easier to debug than long chains of tests choosing between two outcomes.
- [fact] Stateless and state machine monster AI are called pseudo-intelligence because they involve no learning, though game designers still call any monster decision code AI. When: Describing the learning status of common game AI. Why: These approaches follow fixed rules rather than adapting from experience.
- [fact] Emergent AI behaviour has been proposed as a replacement for traditional AI techniques that have failed to deliver satisfying play.

## target selection and threat
- [rule] Give attacking top priority when the creature's most-hated foe lies inside reach of one of its attacks. When: A hated target is in attack range. Why: Attacks in range beat routine tasks.
- [rule] Pack monsters that are faster than their target should favour targets with many open neighbouring squares, counting targets with more than four free grids as closer during selection. When: when a monster is faster than its target and open ground decides the fight. Why: exposed targets are vulnerable to surrounding pack attacks.
- [heuristic] Closest-enemy target selection is a useful default and should be kept unless a specific reason justifies reweighting. When: when choosing a default target selection rule. Why: simple default rules are predictable and cheap.
- [heuristic] Reweight target selection toward enemies that can hurt a monster most, such as those that exploit its immunities. When: when a monster's weaknesses are known. Why: targeting threats matches the monster's real vulnerability.
- [procedure] Flag monsters that have successfully attacked the player so that others do not walk through them. When: Monsters pass through allies in a group. Why: Flagging attackers keeps the front from breaking through the engaged monster.
- [pitfall] Monsters immune to blunt and edged weapons under-perform when target selection ignores their immunities. When: when a monster has damage immunities. Why: targeting that ignores immunities wastes attacks.

## ai director and world simulation
- [mechanism] A world that keeps changing while the player is absent, such as monsters acting on their own, makes locations feel less static and produces consequences the player discovers later. When: the player leaves an area and returns later. Why: Ongoing simulation makes the world's state reflect earlier events, not only the player's current position.
- [pitfall] Monsters that leave their room break room-based placement, and some AI updates make this worse over time. When: when using themed rooms with monster types. Why: monsters roaming out of rooms break the room's theme.
- [fact] Each level holds eight monsters of a single type; a bump kills a monster, some need two bumps, and a monster that bumps the player deals one damage.
- [fact] Dynamic AI is a subset of procedural content generation and inherits the same testing difficulties.
- [fact] Developers increasingly simulate player wants and needs, as with AI directors that adjust pacing and content to the player.

## idle and patrol movement
- [definition] Patrol restricts random destinations to a polygon, rectangle or circle area, such as a nest. When: Monsters must stay within a region.
- [mechanism] Giving a tile occupied by an idle monster a path cost proportional to that monster's idle time makes stalled tiles expensive, so other pathfinders route around jams. When: Many monsters share a choke point and use A*. Why: Stopped monsters raise the cost of blocked routes, while moving monsters add none.
- [mechanism] When the player idles, monsters finish their current move, so fast tapping lets the player outrun them. When: when designing semi-realtime movement and speed differences. Why: monster movement completes on idle, rewarding quick input.
- [heuristic] Patrol areas can be drawn from generated room outlines so guards remain in their chamber. When: Generated levels with rooms.
- [fact] The proportionality constant for idle-time path cost is not given and must be tuned. When: Implementing idle-time jam avoidance.

## monster item interaction
- [model] Item-interacting monsters include item-eaters that destroy what they pick up, single-item carriers that drop their item on death, and item-collectors that drop hoards when killed. When: Designing monsters that affect items.
- [mechanism] Transmuting monsters change items, for example melting metal into a more valuable item or turning coins into nugget crumbs. When: Designing monsters that transform items.

## summoning mechanics
- [mechanism] When summoners can summon other summoners, growth is exponential, because a summoned monster is likely to be the same type when monster level ranges overlap. When: monsters can summon monsters that also summon. Why: Each summon adds another summoner that can summon again.
- [rule] Restrict summoners so they summon only weaker monster types, so the summoning chain runs in one direction. When: monsters can summon others that can also summon. Why: A one-way chain stops summoners from endlessly recreating themselves at the same strength.
- [procedure] 1) Cap the total summons allowed per level. 2) When the summon pool is exhausted, teleport distant qualifying monsters nearby instead of summoning new ones. When: a level-wide summon limit is reached. Why: Existing monsters keep the encounter alive without creating unbounded new summons.
- [tradeoff] Capping total population suits breeders poorly and must be rechecked after every death, whereas capping total summons per level keeps growth bounded without banning breeding. When: bounding monster summoning. Why: A per-level summon budget limits growth without affecting breeders' normal reproduction.
- [pitfall] Strict summon direction alone still allows exponential growth if summoning can happen out of sight, though at a lower rate. When: summoners can summon while unseen by the player. Why: Regeneration across many summoners keeps the population growing.

## dijkstra flow maps
- [definition] A Dijkstra map stores each walkable cell's step distance to the nearest goal, and one map can be shared by every actor that seeks the same goal. Why: Shared maps make goal-seeking cheap per monster.
- [mechanism] Storing full route distances to the player each time the player moves is very CPU-intensive. When: Monsters that compute routes to a moving player. Why: Recomputing distance fields on every player move multiplies computation.
- [mechanism] Covering every combination of terrain traits with separate flows can require up to eight flows. When: Implementing per-terrain pathfinding flows. Why: Each terrain trait combination needs its own flow, so the count grows quickly.
- [procedure] 1) Set goal cells to 0 and all other floor cells to a very high value. 2) Repeatedly set each floor cell to one more than its lowest cardinal neighbour. 3) Stop when no value changes. When: Building a goal-distance map on a grid.

## monster state and memory
- [heuristic] Remember the strongest desire direction after a monster loses sight of its target, and reinsert it when the calculated desire drops to zero. When: Monsters that leave sight of an objective. Why: Without memory, monsters forget objectives once out of view.
- [procedure] 1) Give each monster a target, minimum and best range. 2) Add flags for recent attacks. 3) Record learned player resistances. 4) Let monsters share the player's position and resistances with one another. When: when building monster AI that reacts to the player. Why: these fields let monsters act on local knowledge.
- [pitfall] Hard-coded global variables and string-typed time for each townsperson scale poorly; per-inhabitant structures are the better design. When: Storing schedule state for many characters. Why: Globals are shared and fragile, and strings are awkward to compare.
- [fact] Monster-to-monster interaction can leave a large level in an unusual state, so running a level for some turns before the player arrives can create interesting situations. When: Generating a level with many interacting monsters. Why: Interactions accumulate over time and produce emergent situations.

## monster traps and hazards
- [mechanism] Traps the player can avoid through a temporary state, such as flying over pits, being invisible past watchers, or staying in darkness past light shafts, let the player evade them by changing state. When: Designing traps whose danger depends on the player's current condition. Why: Avoidance by state gives the player a way to act on the trap rather than simply suffering it.
- [behavior] Indifferent monsters attack anything that crosses their path, so hostile monsters can be led into them while the player retreats. When: Designing hazards such as swarms that block a route. Why: Indifferent creatures attack any passing entity, not only the player.
- [behavior] Monsters with short attention spans drop their items when a shinier one appears, creating lure-and-swap puzzles. When: Designing item-based lure puzzles. Why: Distraction by a more attractive item makes the monster abandon its current one.
- [rule] Monsters should avoid traps under the same conditions as the player, which requires temporary abilities as well as permanent ones. When: Monsters move through an environment containing state-dependent traps. Why: Matching the player's avoidance rules keeps monster behavior fair and consistent with the world.
