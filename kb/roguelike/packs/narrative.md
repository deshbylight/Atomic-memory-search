# narrative

## quest and story structure
- [model] A roguelike story starts from a general goal such as rescuing someone, finding an object, killing a target, or escaping a maze.
- [model] A pitfall is a story obstacle, such as a capture that forces an escape. A reward is what the player gains after escaping it, such as experience, an exit, an item, help, or progress toward the goal.
- [mechanism] Narrative creates emotional investment, links otherwise unconnected places and events, and gives meaning to repeated actions.
- [behavior] Linear stories fail when repeated, whereas a roguelike's meta-narrative repeats by design, escalating failure against recurring kinds of challenge. When: when a game is replayed. Why: repetition of a fixed story feels stale, while repeated challenge types build a progression of learning.
- [rule] Express moral choices through persistent world consequences, not forced scripted dilemmas, which players can skip or dismiss and which carry less moral weight. When: designing moral or ethical choices in a game. Why: Choices embedded in the world create guilt and weight without interrupting play with a dilemma screen.
- [rule] Vary how players discover quests, such as hearing cries while travelling, rather than always receiving instructions from a quest-giver. Why: a fixed entry route makes the world feel scripted.
- [rule] Treat flavour as important but secondary to design, since an interesting narrative is played once while an interesting design is played repeatedly. When: Deciding how much effort to spend on story versus mechanics. Why: Replay value depends mainly on how the system plays, not on the fiction.
- [rule] Avoid anything that breaks suspension of disbelief, since it weakens the player's sense that an author stands behind the content. Why: Players judge generated content as authored only while immersion holds.
- [heuristic] Twist the ending so the target needs a special cure or artifact, or was never kidnapped at all, rather than resolving easily.
- [heuristic] Tie companion call-ups to gameplay so the character best suited to a monster's weakness asks for help. When: a companion's role matches an encounter need. Why: Tying dialogue to gameplay makes each character's role feel relevant.
- [heuristic] An unreliable narrator can change how the player should read a situation, so the player must reinterpret events once the narrator's bias is revealed. When: when designing a narrator who misleads.
- [heuristic] Use procedural content when replayability matters more than a fixed authored narrative. When: when choosing between replay variety and authored story.
- [heuristic] Fetch, kill and light puzzle quest content is acceptable, while fixed puzzles are the most disliked quest element.
- [procedure] 1) Choose the setting. 2) Set the main goal. 3) Add twists. 4) Add specific pitfalls with matching rewards. 5) Add roaming areas. When: Building a roguelike story.
- [pitfall] Reading hidden data or making speed-run edits removes the surprises the game holds for the player. When: Modding or cheating in a game with hidden content. Why: Knowing hidden outcomes in advance removes the surprise.
- [pitfall] Dialog backtracking exists only if scripted, and limited screen space and sentence length restrict options, which leads to repetitive dialog.
- [pitfall] Heavy lore can distract from how the system actually plays. When: Writing extensive world lore for a game with mechanical systems. Why: Players spend attention on the fiction rather than on the decisions the system asks for.
- [pitfall] Quests break when the game fails to recognise a quest item, stalling progress.
- [pitfall] Side quests can outweigh the main quest when they feel more important to the player.
- [fact] Roguelikes carry narrative without an authored story, using player-written after-action reports, shared day-in-the-life accounts, and a checklist of rules learned through play.

## horror and dread
- [model] Horror has three kinds: gore (visceral, easiest to make); fear (scenery and story); true horror (unseen menace beyond the scene, never fully shown, hardest to achieve convincingly in computer games).
- [mechanism] When a player cares for an NPC and then sees that NPC killed or transformed, the player feels they could have prevented it, which deepens the sense of helplessness. When: horror scene involving a companion or NPC. Why: Perceived lost agency after attachment makes the loss feel personal and avoidable.
- [behavior] Immersion in roguelikes comes from symbolic display plus player imagination rather than realistic graphics, with players not knowing everything and feeling their losses matter. Why: Symbolic presentation invites players to fill in the details themselves.
- [behavior] A player who stays in constant danger and is never sure what they face makes more careless mistakes. When: permadeath roguelike with constant threat and little information. Why: Constant threat and uncertainty raise anxiety, which degrades decision quality.
- [behavior] Players accept a fantasy only when its magic works consistently, even if the rules look arbitrary. When: A game presents magic or fantastical mechanics. Why: Consistency lets players trust the fiction, which keeps them engaged with the fantasy.
- [behavior] When a player finds random content, they may ignore it and restart, blame an outside agency, or build a story around it. When: when a player encounters unexplained randomly generated content.
- [behavior] Players dislike computer-generated content mainly when they perceive it as valued less than human-authored content. When: when players know or suspect content is machine-made.
- [rule] Describe monsters with evocative wording rather than naming them, such as calling a werewolf giant, horrible, bloodthirsty wolves. Why: Description evokes fear more than the monster's name.
- [heuristic] Dark humor can come from weapon jokes, dark sarcasm, and remarks by NPCs or the player.
- [heuristic] Gore alone, built from corpses, undead and blood, disgusts players rather than frightening them, and is often ineffective. When: Designing horror atmosphere. Why: Gore relies on visceral reaction, which does not create dread.
- [heuristic] Fear works best on objects players already associate with dread, such as a lone tower in marshes or a rising full moon. Why: Existing associations carry dread without explanation.
- [procedure] 1) Give the plot a disgusting archenemy and a supernatural or weird cause. 2) Split the plot into visible episodes so the player feels progress. 3) End with a hint that a threat survives, leaving room for a sequel. When: Plotting a gore roguelike. Why: Visible episodes give a sense of accomplishment; surviving threats enable sequels.
- [procedure] 1) Start with a distant sound. 2) Add an omen. 3) Show frightened villagers. 4) Stage the first attack. When: Building fear in a roguelike. Why: Fear builds gradually, like writing a book.
- [pitfall] Horror that is too extreme can cause some players to quit.

## world setting and lore
- [model] A hidden AI-run strategy game generates world events, and individual human players experience it only through roles such as warrior, spy or peasant, giving a consistent backdrop. When: Designing layered games where a higher-level system supplies story. Why: Individuals perceive the higher layer only through its indirect effects, so the backdrop stays consistent.
- [behavior] With no familiarity and no renown, citizens leave each other alone, while dungeon monsters start with low honor toward the player and attack until the player earns respect. When: Setting initial NPC attitudes. Why: Lack of familiarity and renown produces indifference; low starting honor produces hostility.
- [rule] Role-play needs a setting, background, descriptions, and rules similar to the real world, with exaggerated important details and room for imagination. When: Designing the world of a role-playing game. Why: Exaggerated key details make the world memorable while leaving room for the player's imagination.
- [heuristic] Narrative from a hidden system stays readable only when the system follows consistent rules the player can learn to predict. When: Using a simulated higher layer to generate story events. Why: Unpredictable events cannot form a coherent narrative for the player.
- [pitfall] Iron replaced bronze because a cheaper metal-recovery method spread, not because it was superior; name historical eras after the driving change, such as cheaper production.
- [pitfall] Speculative fictional visions of procedural plots are not demonstrated designs and should not be treated as proven. When: when evaluating proposed procedural narrative ideas.
- [fact] Magic shapes cultures, histories, myths, schools and attitudes, and whether it is rare or common, trusted or feared, affects the world. When: Designing a world around magic. Why: Magic's prevalence and reputation determine how societies form around it.
- [fact] Science fiction is defined by the scientific method: state the simplest explanation, test it, and revise it when a contradiction appears, rather than by genre conventions such as aliens or starships. Why: Genre conventions appeal to the gut but do not make a work scientifically rigorous.
- [fact] Near-future, non-apocalyptic single-player settings are rare, and near-future stories require extra rigour because real-world events can overtake them and weapons must stay close to real standards. When: Writing or designing near-future settings. Why: Real-world developments can quickly contradict a near-future setting, so accuracy matters more.

## companion character design
- [model] Each NPC holds favor, respect and honor toward others, each ranging -100 to 100; favor is liking, respect is recognition of abilities, and honor is obligation to help. When: Modeling relations between NPCs.
- [heuristic] Build companion personality through recurring characteristic beats, such as sarcastic comments on reloading, healing or a squadmate's rescue; give each member enough beats before fighting starts so players like them. When: writing companion personality or introducing a group of companions. Why: Recurring characteristic reactions make characters memorable; early characterisation builds attachment before the group is tested.
- [heuristic] Give the hero weak starting traits, such as low experience, poor equipment, and weak attributes, to form the first pitfall. When: Roguelike hero setup. Why: Weakness creates an initial challenge.
- [heuristic] Match companion roles to needs, such as a healer for healing and a logistics character for ammunition. When: assigning companion roles. Why: Role-appropriate characters make the group feel useful and coherent.
- [procedure] 1) Give each companion several lines per common situation, such as reloading or running out of ammunition. 2) Vary lines so the same line does not repeat. When: writing barks for companion characters. Why: Repetition breaks immersion and makes companions feel mechanical.
- [pitfall] Companion interactions that come across as arrogant weaken the group's appeal and need rewriting. When: writing companion banter. Why: Arrogance from companions makes players dislike them.

## dialogue writing craft
- [mechanism] Speech can be garbled by a language skill percentage: higher skill keeps more words intact, while lower skill drops letters and words. When: Simulating foreign or unknown languages.
- [rule] Branching dialogue works when choices are real and not obvious; conventional trees with few options that signal the best choice leave players guessing.
- [rule] Use fixed text for scripted speech, such as a dying NPC's request, so translators can handle it.
- [heuristic] Compose combat and event messages from tagged words rather than fixed strings, because fixed subject-verb-object sentences repeat and feel dull. When: Roguelikes needing many varied messages.
- [pitfall] Too much detail in role-play breaks suspension of disbelief and destroys the feeling of a world. When: Writing world descriptions and rules for role-play. Why: Overly precise detail draws attention to the artificial nature of the world.
- [fact] Good, ambiguous writing in deep dialog trees can make conversation interesting, so writing quality strongly affects the result.

## familiarity as renown bonus
- [heuristic] A familiarity stat that grows with each interaction can act as a bonus to renown. When: Designing how relationships build over time.

## npc attributes and renown
- [definition] NPC attributes are morals, skill, strength, reputation and charisma, each 0-100; renown is the percentage of each attribute visible to others. When: Modeling NPC traits and what others perceive.

## similarity favor formula
- [pitfall] Exact formulas for how similarity raises favor are not given, so the implementer must design them. When: Implementing favor growth from shared attributes.

## social action from averages
- [rule] Average favor, respect and honor toward another NPC: negative gives a chance of spontaneous harm, positive a chance of help, and zero means neither. When: Deciding how an NPC treats another.

## visible attribute scaling
- [mechanism] Visible attribute equals the attribute times renown percentage; a strength of 60 at renown 25 appears as 15. When: Computing how an NPC's traits look to others. Why: Renown hides part of an NPC's true traits.
