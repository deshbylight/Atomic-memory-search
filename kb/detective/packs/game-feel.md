# game-feel

## audio cues and reveals
- [mechanism] A light swung briefly across a figure can illuminate a face for a moment, revealing who is there. When: When a light source moves past a character in darkness. Why: Brief illumination exposes features that stay hidden in darkness.
- [mechanism] A small concealed device producing a sharp sound on cue can create an effect that observers attribute to a supernatural cause. When: Staging an effect that observers will misread. Why: A hidden, timed sound is convincing when it matches what observers expect.
- [mechanism] Ambient sounds such as passing traffic can give rough timestamps when no clock is visible, while observers hold position. When: when a timeline needs rough reference points. Why: Recurring sounds mark elapsed time without requiring an exact measure.
- [rule] Cut ambient sound and pause before exposing a hidden antagonist, so the silence heightens the reveal. When: Scripting a dramatic identity reveal. Why: A sudden drop in sound draws attention to the moment.
- [rule] Give injuries a direction and location in the game, so a wound read from one side can imply the attacker's stance or handedness. When: when designing injury feedback or forensic clues. Why: Directional wounds let players infer attacker positioning.
- [rule] Make enemies seen only as silhouettes against a bright exit require the player to move closer or test a detail before identifying them. When: When enemies are initially backlit or distant. Why: Forcing closer inspection turns identification into an active choice.
- [rule] Animate small reaction cues, such as a paling face or a hand to the chest, so players can read a suspect's reaction without a text prompt.
- [heuristic] Use audio cues like a striking clock, footsteps, and a ringing bell as countdown signals in pursuit or timed evasion, building tension and rewarding players who read them. When: Designing suspense in a stealth, pursuit, or timed evasion scene. Why: Sound cues signal approaching danger and shorten perceived time; audio gives players readable information about time and danger.
- [heuristic] A sudden loud noise after a quiet passage restarts momentum and redirects attention. When: A scene has gone quiet and needs renewed pull. Why: Abrupt sound breaks the calm and forces the player to react.

## stealth and tension design
- [mechanism] Wind, storm, darkness and drifting debris make a player flinch and raise tension, amplifying the emotional weight of an approaching reveal. When: Staging a tense scene before a discovery. Why: Environmental pressure heightens the sense of threat without new information.
- [mechanism] Surfaces that creak give away a hidden player while silent surfaces do not, so surface material becomes a stealth mechanic. When: Designing stealth movement across floors or stairs. Why: Surface sound determines whether movement is detected by nearby characters.
- [rule] Let a shadow or cloth fold read as a threat in a dark room and then reveal it as harmless, but require a visual check so sound alone never settles whether a threat exists. When: Designing horror or tension in a dark environment. Why: Fear is built by ambiguity, and a check prevents sound-only cues from being unfair.
- [heuristic] Alternate long quiet passages with sudden interruptions, releasing tension when the threat is left behind and rebuilding it in a new space; contrast makes each break land harder. When: Shaping a sequence of encounters or building dread before a reveal. Why: Contrast with quiet sharpens the impact of a sudden event; constant intensity flattens the effect. (x2)
- [heuristic] Use stealth or ambush sequences when they depend on patience and sensory cues rather than constant action.
- [heuristic] Place each pause just before new information, since a pause with no payoff turns suspense into boredom. When: pacing a suspense sequence. Why: pauses gain value when they lead to a reveal.
- [tradeoff] Constant action keeps energy high but dulls the impact of each moment; quiet stretches sharpen interruptions but risk boredom if too long. When: Pacing a chase or encounter sequence. Why: Contrast gives impact, while sustained intensity loses its effect.

## traces and tactile evidence
- [mechanism] Environmental disruptions such as a fire or light change can break a trick, creating a timed mechanic. When: Designing a trick that depends on lighting or fire. Why: Changing conditions reveal or defeat the trick and can be used as a timed challenge.
- [rule] Attach a timestamp to each trace so stale evidence does not mislead the player, since a stain's inference about timing fails once it is no longer fresh. When: Designing visible physical evidence such as stains or wear. Why: Freshness determines what a trace can tell, so stale traces must not imply recent events.
- [rule] Convey speed through bodily sensation such as sway, hill climbs and a braking stop, so the player feels motion when the view is restricted. When: the player cannot see the route.
- [heuristic] Touch-like cues such as a draught or contact can signal a nearby presence before it is seen. When: visibility is poor.
- [fact] Footprints stay sharp enough to read when there has been no new snowfall since the event and the surface is frozen, while heavy traffic, thaw or fresh snow erases them. When: Judging whether outdoor traces can still be read. Why: Snowfall, thaw and foot traffic overwrite impressions and remove the evidence.

## fog and lamp visibility
- [behavior] Fog that hides and reveals in patches, with lamps struggling against it, makes the surroundings feel threatening and leaves the watcher uncertain. When: when fog or partial lighting covers a space. Why: Partial visibility keeps the observer unsure what lies beyond the reach of light.

## lost time gaps
- [mechanism] A character who dozes and loses time creates a gap in the timeline that the player must explain. When: A mechanic lets a character lose time unnoticed. Why: The unexplained interval forces the player to reconcile the sequence.

## moving light dread
- [mechanism] Moving light that flashes objects into view and removes them again makes ordinary objects seem to appear, vanish and watch, so dread comes from lighting and motion rather than the objects. When: Static objects should feel threatening without adding enemies. Why: Shifting light and shadow give still shapes apparent motion and intent.
