# process

## scene inspection
- [behavior] Expectation focuses search on likely locations, so searchers sweep open ground and overlook containers holding decisive evidence; hunting hidden compartments makes plain containers missed. When: Searchers know which objects or hiding places to expect. Why: Prior expectation directs effort toward likely spots and away from ordinary containers.
- [behavior] Long fruitless searches erode motivation and method: searchers tire, lose ordered inference, and rely on chance effort. When: A long pursuit or bounded search yields repeated failed leads or empty zones. Why: Repeated negative results weaken both hope and systematic reasoning.
- [behavior] Players often overlook a key they saw early, because it looks like an ordinary item rather than a puzzle tool. When: A hidden object is opened by an item seen earlier in the game. Why: Familiar, mundane-looking items fail to draw attention as solutions.
- [behavior] A player or investigator overlooks an object already in view when an earlier assumption makes it seem irrelevant to the case. When: a working theory conflicts with the object's meaning. Why: Wrong assumptions suppress recognition of evidence.
- [behavior] Items hidden but never used reveal the hider's state of mind and intent, not only a theft.
- [behavior] After repeated searches come up empty, searchers lower their suspicion of that place. When: the hiding place looks ordinary. Why: A negative result reduces perceived risk.
- [behavior] A room that appears sealed from within leads players to assume no one could have left, which makes it an effective locked-room effect. When: A room is bolted or locked from the inside. Why: The apparent impossibility suppresses the search for alternative exits.
- [behavior] People hesitate to open sacred or ancestral resting places, so a hiding place protected by a social or ritual barrier is rarely searched. When: A hiding place is protected by a strong social or ritual taboo. Why: The taboo deters searching, which lets concealment succeed.
- [behavior] Players tend to overlook back routes that read as scenery, so routes shown as background may be missed. When: a route is visually plain or dull. Why: it resembles set dressing.
- [behavior] In a crowded scene, unexpected empty places stand out to observers and get filed as facts that need explaining. When: A crowd is present but certain positions are unexpectedly empty. Why: Conspicuous gaps stand out against a dense background.
- [behavior] Players treat an item revealed in a public search as self-proving, even when the holder never knew it was there. When: An object falls from a target's pocket during a search watched by a crowd. Why: Discovery in front of others makes the finding feel conclusive.
- [rule] When an alibi depends on a companion who cannot be found, test it through records that do exist, such as hotel registers and travel timetables. Why: An untraceable companion leaves the alibi unverified.
- [rule] Weigh several anomalies together rather than concluding staging from one oddity, since a single unusual choice can have ordinary causes. When: A scene shows one or more unusual features. Why: An isolated oddity may have an innocent explanation.
- [heuristic] Record apparently unimportant facts immediately, since trivial-seeming details can gain meaning later; noting only significant ones leaves nothing to reconsider when the picture changes. When: Many small facts must be compared later, or evidence is gathered before its significance is known. Why: Memory of minor details fades before they become relevant, and facts that seem trivial can gain meaning as the question clarifies. (x2)
- [heuristic] Treat small anomalies seriously and follow them up rather than dismissing them; the first explanation may be only half right, and dismissing an odd light can nearly close a lead. When: A minor odd observation seems explainable away. Why: Dismissed anomalies can hide the real lead.
- [heuristic] An object left in an unusually disordered state for its owner suggests someone may have deliberately altered it.
- [heuristic] A dry item left where it was set down shows an action was prepared for but never completed.
- [heuristic] A party who takes a document to an unfamiliar firm, with an unexplained reason, is a detail to scrutinize rather than accept. When: A claimant changes the usual professional channel for a document. Why: Unusual routing can signal an attempt to avoid scrutiny.
- [heuristic] Untidy details, such as unturned cuffs, loosely tied laces or an item in the wrong pocket, signal that a change of clothes was rushed.
- [heuristic] Examine correspondence closely for signs of tampering, paying most attention to official or franked envelopes. When: private correspondence could have been read by others.
- [heuristic] Too much scrutiny of one object narrows attention so that faint clues vanish; stepping back to survey the whole scene helps. When: a player fixes on one object and finds nothing. Why: intense focus excludes the surrounding details where clues sit.
- [heuristic] Ask why a perpetrator would choose a harder method when an easier one was available; an elaborate method can hint that the scene was staged. When: A break-in looks unusually elaborate. Why: Staged scenes often use methods that a real intruder would not pick.
- [procedure] 1) State the suspicion as a physical claim about distance, access or structure. 2) Inspect the site directly. 3) Confirm or reject the claim before acting on it. When: when a theory depends on physical facts. Why: a suspicion becomes testable only once it names a checkable property.
- [procedure] 1) List every object on the floor, including damaged ones. 2) Count containers and their marks. 3) Test residues. 4) Look for mismatches with the working theory. 5) Check for signs the scene was altered after people left. 6) Search corners. 7) Measure and draw a plan. When: Several people were present and objects have been moved, broken or left. Why: A systematic survey exposes details that a first impression misses.
- [procedure] 1) List each incriminating fact as a numbered item. 2) Beside each, write its most plausible innocent or alternative explanation. 3) Note which items no explanation covers and mark them as unresolved. When: Mixed evidence has accumulated and a clear overall view is needed. Why: Pairing each fact with a counter-explanation exposes which suspicions remain after ordinary explanations are spent.
- [procedure] 1) Inspect the exterior first for soiling, smells, re-sealing, altered dates and cover handwriting. 2) Examine the sides and backing. 3) Check fastenings. 4) Open and examine the contents. 5) Record each finding in writing as you go. When: Examining a sealed or wrapped object that may have been handled by others. Why: Staged inspection exposes tampering in order, and written notes prevent loss of detail before inference.
- [procedure] 1) Inspect how each restraint was made. 2) Determine whether the bound person could have tied it themselves. 3) Test whether the restraint can be loosened. When: When a bound person's restraints look like evidence of violence. Why: Restraint craft can reveal self-binding or a staged arrangement.
- [procedure] 1) Inspect ordinary furnishings as well as obvious evidence. 2) Note objects that do not fit the owner's public image. 3) Check the state of room controls such as shades and lights. 4) Ask what normal use the room shows and what has been disturbed. When: Investigating a room early, before it is disturbed. Why: Routine objects and odd details reveal habits, visitors and recent events that main evidence can miss.
- [procedure] 1) Check each shelf row by row, from the highest down. 2) Note books pulled out and returned out of alignment. 3) Compare each disturbed book's dust against its neighbours. When: inspecting a shelf for recent handling. Why: Recently handled items are clean and misaligned, distinguishing them from long-undisturbed ones.
- [procedure] 1) Begin the inquiry immediately after being summoned. 2) Examine the scene while traces are fresh. 3) Question everyone present, including guests and servants, early. 4) Record evidence before it decays. When: when running an investigation of a crime scene. Why: Delay and exposure destroy evidence and fade witness memory.
- [procedure] 1) Confirm that damage sits where the claimed method would place it. 2) Count the marks and check they match that method. 3) Check whether a dropped object shows signs of its fall. 4) Check any denial against the physical evidence. When: A scene looks like a break-in but the damage or placement seems arranged. Why: Mismatched damage, marks or placement reveal that the scene was arranged.
- [procedure] 1) Set the frame and who is present. 2) Collect every raw impression, including passing fancies. 3) Only then interpret the observations. When: Gathering reports about an event where interpretation could contaminate recall. Why: Impressions recorded before a theory fills the gap stay uncontaminated and can be checked later.
- [procedure] 1) Look for a physical signature, such as discharges or interference that starts and stops with an event. 2) Check which variable changes the effect. 3) Propose a physical and human explanation. 4) Confirm by tying the phenomenon to a person. When: An effect seems beyond explanation but leaves measurable traces. Why: A signature and a controllable variable point to a physical source, and a person confirms the cause.
- [procedure] 1) Record the same place under two different conditions. 2) Compare the records with measuring tools rather than general impression. 3) Note exactly what changed between records. 4) Trace the change to its cause. When: A scene seems to change between observations or a detail seems inconsistent. Why: A measurable difference between conditions exposes hidden structure that a casual look misses.
- [procedure] 1) Check whether your own movement could have caused the disturbance. 2) Rule out self-caused causes before concluding an outside agent acted. 3) Re-check each sudden impulse for its origin. When: A barrier or clue is found altered or a sudden disturbance occurs. Why: The first explanation may be wrong, and self-caused breaches are easy to mistake for hostile action.
- [procedure] 1) Start by assuming an ordinary cause. 2) Test each ordinary explanation. 3) Accept an unusual cause only after the ordinary ones fail several tests. When: Investigating an anomaly in a setting where both natural and unusual causes are possible. Why: Earned supernatural conclusions feel more credible than assumed ones.
- [procedure] 1) Survey walls, floor and ceiling with basic tools. 2) If the first pass finds nothing, repeat the search where events cluster. 3) Probe gaps and under fixtures with a tool. 4) Use a sound cue on contact to locate the object. 5) Retrieve the object and test a hypothesis about it. When: When a first survey fails and an event repeatedly centres on one spot. Why: Small objects in gaps are missed by a quick survey, and sound cues reveal contact out of sight.
- [tradeoff] Repeating an earlier pass's obvious places wastes effort, yet a quick first survey misses small objects in gaps, so re-search where events cluster. When: Re-searching a room or area after a first pass. Why: Obvious spots are already cleared, while gaps escape quick passes. (x2)
- [pitfall] Searching only for planned, obvious or theory-predicted evidence misses plain-view details and the real mechanism; investigators find what they expected. When: Reviewing evidence against a theory or searching a crime scene for clues. Why: A fixed hypothesis or expected evidence filters out ordinary but telling details. (x2)
- [pitfall] Relying on others' descriptions without direct inspection can carry a false account forward, so an object known only through reports should be treated as unverified. When: The only evidence for a scene or object is what others said or described. (x2)
- [pitfall] A partial or first search does not prove absence: missing traces may sit in unexamined parts or small items, so check the rest before concluding. When: Inspection covers only part of a site, or an early search comes up empty. Why: Unseen parts or small items can hold the fact that decides the question.
- [pitfall] A failed search may reflect the method used rather than the object's absence, since searchers without knowledge of habits look in the wrong places.
- [pitfall] Do not assume the better tool was actually in the actor's hands; a timing inference built on the fallback depends on that assumption.
- [pitfall] Collecting several traces without tying each to a location makes the set hard to interpret. When: When gathering multiple physical traces from a scene. Why: Without location links, traces cannot be interpreted together.
- [pitfall] Do not accept an explanation for disorder without checking when the space was last serviced, since an untidy room can act as a dated record.
- [pitfall] Missing documents can be read as concealment, but a gap alone does not prove intent. When: When a destroyed or absent record is noticed. Why: Gaps have innocent explanations as well as sinister ones.
- [pitfall] Anxious searchers look where they fear the target is rather than where it could plausibly be.
- [pitfall] Sending a field agent without a clear list produces unusable material, and missing a time limit lets the subject return or the scene change. When: Directing field work during an investigation. Why: Unfocused collection wastes effort, and delay lets conditions change.
- [pitfall] Rejecting one route after a single inspection can miss a second route, and a hypothesis rejected at once may still be correct under different conditions. When: Testing exits or hypotheses in a sealed-room investigation. Why: Each assumption must be checked explicitly rather than assumed excluded.
- [pitfall] A search radius that is too narrow can miss the place, and one that is too wide wastes time. When: Setting the scope of a location search. Why: Scope errors either exclude the target or bloat the search.
- [pitfall] A routine sweep of leads may produce no direction for several days; routine work alone does not guarantee a result. When: An inquiry relies only on standard searches and questioning. Why: Routine tasks cover known ground but do not themselves generate a new lead.
- [pitfall] Assigning a searcher unsuited to a space or person, such as a male searcher for a restroom, is an improper plan that needs correcting.
- [pitfall] Linking a found object to a suspect from memory and suspicion, without testing the object, produces a fragile inference.
- [pitfall] Clues recalled from a scene are unreliable unless the observer noticed the objects when first seen. When: Reconstructing a scene from memory after the fact. Why: Objects not attended to at the time are not accurately recorded.
- [fact] A safe opened without being picked implies the opener had its key, which shifts the question from how someone entered to who could reach the key. When: When a locked container opens without signs of forcing. Why: Absence of forced entry points to legitimate access, narrowing suspects to key holders.

## incomplete records
- [mechanism] Movements unseen by others leave holes in the shared record, and events inside those holes stay hidden until reconstructed. When: when a person's whereabouts were unknown to the household.
- [mechanism] A records check that finds no person matching a claimed identity removes the main support for that claim.
- [rule] Absence of a record entry does not prove an event did not occur; logs may be incomplete and record survival depends on chance. Check what a log records first. When: Checking a log, ledger, or expected record, or when an archive search finds nothing. Why: Records capture only some treated or logged events, so silence in the record does not disprove them. (x2)
- [rule] Do not fill missing evidence with assumed explanations or guessed text, such as assuming someone has left; state missing or damaged records and mark unknown durations as unknown. When: Records are missing, damaged, or incomplete, or a duration is unknown. Why: Assumed answers make an incomplete case feel complete, and guessed text can pass as evidence and distort the reconstruction. (x2)
- [rule] Record every fact, including those that seem irrelevant or were withheld, because significance and meaning may only emerge later. Why: Significance may appear later, and withheld evidence's meaning depends on who later learns its source.
- [rule] Verify a claimed identity against a reliable register, such as a directory of titled people, rather than trusting appearance, a card, or a title. When: A person claims a rank or identity that affects trust or access. Why: A reliable register exposes false claims that appearance cannot.
- [rule] Judge an explanation by whether it fits the independent record, not by how reasonable it sounds.
- [rule] Let a game's records system filter an archive by fixed fields, leaving a short candidate list that the player must visit to confirm. When: When designing archive or record mechanics in a game. Why: Fixed fields keep the search manageable and meaningful.
- [rule] Write in-world archives as footnoted documents with cited sources, some wrong or contradictory, so players must cross-check them.
- [rule] Document a minority conclusion carefully, so it can be defended when supporting evidence arrives. When: When a conclusion is held against the views of colleagues. Why: Careful records let a conclusion be defended later.
- [rule] Require every attendee to show a ticket, and log lost tickets and any whose printed seat differs from the seat they occupy.
- [rule] Record errors found in a published work and promise their correction in any reissue.
- [heuristic] Omit a key fact from in-world records as a hole that players fill from other documents or witnesses, such as an alibi revealed later. When: Designing investigation content or a case file with missing entries. Why: Discovery through comparison rewards attention. (x2)
- [heuristic] Reread records and cross-check all known facts against each new clue, since a decisive detail missed or left unused can resolve an unexplained clue. When: A case rests on written testimony or records, or a clue seems unexplained despite earlier information. Why: Recall drops details and facts already in hand go unused unless cross-checked; fresh reading reconnects them.
- [heuristic] Record deliberate gaps in a document as well as its contents, since an author's omissions can point to knowledge they expected to recall. When: reading a written account with unexplained absences. Why: gaps can signal knowledge the author did not write down.
- [heuristic] A record can exclude a suspect even when it cannot confirm one, so use records to rule candidates out as well as in. When: When checking candidates against records. Why: Negative results narrow the field even when no positive match is found.
- [heuristic] An incomplete cosmetic detail, such as paint covering only part of the mouth, is an observation the investigator must explain.
- [heuristic] Records of one dispute may be filed in different offices, each holding part of the terms, so search every office where related deeds or bonds may be filed. When: a land or ownership dispute involves several agreements. Why: The full terms can appear only when the scattered parts are compared.
- [heuristic] When records have a gap, check whether a known personal event, such as a lawsuit, falls in that period before linking the two.
- [heuristic] When repeating an earlier condition, check whether the original record likely underreported the amount, and adjust to match the real conditions. When: A reproduction must match an earlier dose, quantity, or input recorded in a log. Why: Records often miss part of what was actually applied, so the recorded value may be too low.
- [heuristic] A design log that records every room-layout change lets a playtest result be traced to the exact configuration that produced it. When: Layouts are changed between playtests. Why: Tracing results to configurations makes it clear which change caused which effect.
- [heuristic] Back a disputed document's authenticity with expert comparison and independent witness checks; a single recognition or signature match is not sufficient alone. When: A will, letter or receipt is contested. Why: Forgeries can pass one check but fail when corroborating witnesses are asked.
- [heuristic] Incidental records such as expense lists can reveal conditions and practices that no narrative states directly. When: When formal narratives are silent about conditions or circumstances. Why: Records made for accounting or other purposes often contain details the writer did not intend to preserve.
- [heuristic] Treat two independent gaps, such as missed payments and unclaimed belongings, together as stronger evidence than either alone. When: two separate routine trails stop around the same time. Why: Two unrelated lapses converging on one date make a shared cause more likely.
- [procedure] 1) Report observed facts without interpretation, marking any guess as a guess. 2) Name priority subjects such as relationships and new details. 3) Record which prior checks were negative. 4) Build a timeline of who was together and when they separated. When: One person observes in the field and another reasons about the observations. Why: Separating facts from theory keeps the reasoner unbiased and makes the timeline checkable.
- [procedure] 1) Gather older reference sources covering every way the event could have occurred. 2) Extract and classify their notes by method. 3) Review the classified notes before the investigative visit. When: a case has many possible causes. Why: Prepared knowledge covers every contingency, and preparation is rarely wasted even when the case proves simple.
- [procedure] 1) After a split result, record each decision-maker's stated reasons. 2) Identify which argument moved which person. 3) Adjust the next presentation to address those specific reasons. When: A case failed to reach agreement and will be presented again.
- [procedure] 1) Classify each marginal figure as a money amount or a time using context. 2) Check each time against a fixed event to confirm what it refers to. 3) Read earlier times as arrivals or glances at a watch, and later times as planned meetings. When: A victim left written numbers and their meaning is uncertain. Why: Context and fixed events disambiguate figures written without labels.
- [procedure] Keep an observation log that records the order in which facts were learned, so later reasoning can be traced to its source. When: Tracking evidence during an investigation. Why: Knowing the sequence of learning reveals which conclusions were influenced by earlier information.
- [procedure] 1) Watch the event without theorizing. 2) Write down the observations once the moment has passed. 3) Mark which details are uncertain. When: Recording a stressful or fast event. Why: Notes made soon after the event are more reliable than recollection later.
- [tradeoff] Gap suggests deliberate removal if the record should have existed and someone had access; otherwise it may reflect no action or non-recording. When: A record appears incomplete. Why: Gaps can reveal tampering, but a missing entry only means something if it was expected to be there. (x2)
- [pitfall] Elimination is valid only if the candidate list is complete; an omitted possibility, inherited from an incomplete source, makes a wrong answer look certain and yields a dead-end search. When: Excluding candidates one by one, such as hiding places, or key facts are still missing. Why: An unlisted place is never checked, so the search can end without finding the object.
- [pitfall] Condensed, recopied, or summarized notes can drop details or blend opinion with fact, distorting the sequence and making later checking harder.
- [pitfall] Records may stop at the last known point, so later stages depend on people's memories, which are less reliable. Why: Documents rarely cover the full trail.
- [pitfall] A quick explanation that ignores a pagination gap can miss the real story, so examine the object before accepting an explanation.
- [pitfall] A late confession can be staged to hide a worse fact, so a later admission is not automatically the complete account. When: A witness finally admits a detail after earlier denials. Why: Partial admissions can conceal the most damaging facts.
- [pitfall] A record written by an interested party is not neutral, and an official summary can smooth over or distort what happened. When: When a minister, official or participant produced the main surviving record. Why: Participants shape the record to their own position.
- [pitfall] A later marginal note can reflect hindsight rather than the moment of the event, so it should not be read as contemporary. When: When a note was added after the original entry. Why: Later writers know the outcome and may reinterpret the event.
- [pitfall] One misnumbered or unnumbered item in a transcription can shift all later references, misleading anyone who cross-checks them.
- [pitfall] Do not conclude a death from a missing record alone, since lapses, absence abroad and clerical failure can produce the same gap. When: a person's records stop without positive evidence of death. Why: A gap has several possible causes, and only positive evidence settles the fact.
- [pitfall] Trusting a key's history without checking whether it was returned, lent or copied lets a duplicate remain in circulation unnoticed. When: Access items pass between owners, services or returns. Why: A returned key may be a copy, so the chain of custody is broken silently.
- [pitfall] Keeping a record book of names, codes and signatures where many people can photograph it lets an attacker assemble the information needed to pass checks. When: Verification relies on written records of credentials. Why: Photographed records together supply identities, codes and signatures that defeat verification.
- [fact] Official files may be closed to private inquirers without a legal order, so an investigator may need another route to the facts.
- [fact] Records reveal transactions without anyone speaking, so they can confirm or contradict witness testimony. When: Testimony and documents may disagree. Why: Documents are a physical trace independent of what people say.
- [fact] Older specialist reference works still hold useful knowledge and should not be neglected in favour of recent sources.
- [fact] Surviving records of past events are often imperfect and scattered, so accounts must be assembled from fragments and stated as such. When: When reconstructing events from historical documents. Why: Few complete records survive, so reconstructions rest on partial evidence.
- [fact] Important facts can survive only in documents made for another purpose, such as an estate dispute, so such records can settle questions that official rolls leave open. When: When a primary record of a relationship or identity is lost. Why: Disputes force people to write down facts that would otherwise go unrecorded.

## witness interviews
- [behavior] A witness may turn pale or fall silent when another person points out a gap in their account, which may suggest the point is sensitive. When: an inference about their timing is put to them. Why: an inference touching their account can cause visible discomfort.
- [behavior] Evasive people often disclose more when questioned one-on-one than in front of others, since others' presence raises their guard. When: questioning someone who has reason to conceal information. Why: Being watched increases self-protection.
- [behavior] A witness given a real choice, such as deciding whether the questioner stays, becomes more willing to talk. When: A guarded witness is being questioned. Why: Feeling in control lowers defensiveness.
- [behavior] A witness who admits one lie may still be shielding other parts of the story. An admission of one lie does not verify the revised account. When: After a witness corrects an earlier statement.
- [behavior] A tentative witness can be overridden by a more confident one in group settings, so corrections must be checked for real observation. When: group interview. Why: Confidence is not the same as accuracy.
- [behavior] When the subject says little, a witness's fear and excitement can supply the characterization, so the account reflects the witness. When: when the subject gives few words. Why: the listener fills the gap with feeling.
- [rule] Have each observer sign a plain written account of what they saw, so their testimony is fixed before later discussion alters it. Why: written accounts fix testimony.
- [rule] Flag NPC lines repeated in identical wording and tone so the designer can see scripted rehearsal and vary the lines. When: when reviewing dialogue. Why: Repetition makes scripted behavior obvious to players.
- [heuristic] Give the witness the role of corrector rather than accuser, which lowers resistance and makes candid answers more likely. When: interviewing a witness who is ashamed, frightened or hostile. Why: A corrector role feels less threatening than being accused.
- [heuristic] Keep a second person close enough to restrain a suspect during a confrontation. When: Questioning a suspect who may become violent. Why: Physical proximity allows a fast response if the suspect turns dangerous.
- [heuristic] Choose sceptical witnesses for a test, since a result that convinces them cannot easily be disputed. When: A result must withstand challenge from others. Why: Doubt from a critical observer forces the evidence to be strong.
- [heuristic] Control a subject's unstable state, such as sleeplessness or nervous sensitivity, before relying on a reproduction. When: The subject's condition varies unpredictably between tests. Why: An uncontrolled variable makes the result of a reproduction unreliable.
- [heuristic] Expect a rambling interview to yield few usable facts, and plan to sift for them rather than judge the interview as pointless.
- [heuristic] Corroborate a timeline with a witness who saw the victim alive at a stated time. When: Establishing when a person was last alive. Why: An independent sighting supports a stated time.
- [heuristic] Ask whether anyone entered or left the area during a witness's stay; a clear answer can close off a route. When: Checking whether an unseen person could have passed through a location. Why: A witness who was present for the whole stay can rule out entry or exit.
- [heuristic] Keep a faithful, timed record of a witness's exact words, since it is the only reliable basis for settling later disputes about what was said. When: A statement may be contested later. Why: Memory of wording is unreliable and disputes are settled by the record.
- [heuristic] An identification resting on one witness is weak, so seek independent confirmation and check the witness for a motive that could shape it. When: Identifying a body from a single source. Why: A lone or interested witness may be mistaken or biased.
- [heuristic] Check a party's stated belief against its conduct, since actions that contradict the claim reveal the true position. When: Assessing a witness or interested party's testimony. Why: Conduct inconsistent with a stated belief is stronger evidence of their real view than the words.
- [procedure] 1) Keep other people out of earshot during one-on-one questioning. 2) Have a neutral second observer record statements so accounts can be compared afterward. When: Interviewing a witness, especially in sensitive one-on-one questioning. Why: Isolation reduces outside influence, and a second observer guards against misrecording. (x2)
- [procedure] 1) State who you are and why you are asking. 2) Ask the witness's opinion first and record it. 3) Ask what was actually seen or said. 4) Name any witness bias and weigh the account accordingly. 5) Trace third-hand information to its source. 6) Compare opinion with physical clues; revisit only if new evidence contradicts it. When: Interviewing a witness with a personal stake or close connection, or when early fixation on one suspect risks closing the investigation. Why: Stating your purpose lowers defences, and separating opinion from observation shows what the witness actually knows. Recording an unprompted judgment stops early fixation from shaping later answers. (x2)
- [procedure] 1) Ask whether the witness knows anything beyond what others told them. 2) Separate personal knowledge from secondhand report. 3) Ask a direct follow-up when a rumor is denied to see whether the denial holds. When: A witness has a personal stake or knowledge of others' actions and answers seem carefully limited. Why: Narrow answers can hide a key fact known through another person.
- [procedure] 1) Fix the last sighting in the witness's own words. 2) Raise a quarrel neutrally and let the witness volunteer the reason. 3) Probe unvolunteered points, such as whether a parting was friendly on both sides. 4) Test the account against independent records. When: When interviewing a witness about a sequence of times, claims and contacts. Why: Establishing fixed facts first and letting motives surface unprompted reduces the risk of answers shaped by the question.
- [procedure] 1) Ask one concrete question at a time: who, which way, whether they spoke. 2) Record the answer. 3) If answers stay empty, accept the limit and move on. When: Interviewing a minor witness. Why: Single focused questions extract usable detail without overwhelming the witness.
- [procedure] 1) Separate each claim into what was perceived and what was assumed later. 2) Note who was present and when. 3) Check each claim against physical evidence and other witnesses. When: when several people were awake or present at an event. Why: Mixing perception with later assumption hides the claims that need verification.
- [procedure] 1) Reread the account as if no one had told it. 2) List every unusual feature of the scene. 3) Check the state of physical objects against what the witness described. When: when a testimony is complete and consistent but the scene may disagree with it. Why: A coherent story can still be false, and objects often reveal small contradictions.
- [procedure] 1) Write notes promptly after the conversation, within about five minutes. 2) Review the notes together, separating reported facts from interpretation. 3) Record uncertain characterisations as tentative. When: after any informal interview whose account may be needed later. Why: Recollection drifts within minutes, so delayed notes lose accuracy.
- [procedure] 1) Record each version of a witness account as it is given. 2) Compare versions against each other. 3) Set contradictions against one another to find what changed and why. When: A witness's account changes across interviews. Why: Tracking versions exposes shifts that a final account hides.
- [procedure] 1) Collect each party's account. 2) Test contradictions against physical facts such as wounds, weapons and sounds. 3) Identify which accounts cancel out. 4) Check whether any witness will formally testify. When: Several accounts of one event conflict. Why: Physical facts break ties that testimony alone cannot resolve.
- [procedure] 1) Record distance, light, obstruction and the witness's emotional state. 2) Check the account against other witnesses. 3) Accept an identification only after these conditions are weighed. 4) Do not treat a description matching a suspect as proof of identity. When: Testimony is the only evidence that a figure was present or who it was. Why: Observation conditions and witness state determine how reliable a sighting is.
- [procedure] 1) Write down the exact observed detail at the moment of sighting. 2) Keep competing readings open. 3) Check the detail against an independent record. 4) Treat a match as a verified link, not proof of motive. When: When a precise sighting may connect a person to a place or fact. Why: A recorded, independently checked detail separates a real link from chance overlap.
- [procedure] 1) Record the witness's words as they are given. 2) Have the witness retell the events in order, including details already mentioned. 3) After each stage, summarise it and get confirmation before continuing. 4) Pair statements with the witness's reactions. 5) Hold conclusions until the full sequence is confirmed. When: A witness has given an informal account that needs to be fixed before testing against other evidence. Why: Recording and confirming step by step prevents the account from shifting and exposes gaps.
- [procedure] 1) Read the complete case record before acting. 2) Form a working theory of who committed the act, kept separate from the official charge. 3) List every witness who can speak for the accused. 4) Visit each witness, state who you are and what you want, and ask direct questions. 5) Let each answer choose the next step rather than following a fixed script. When: An investigation must reach beyond formal channels and needs a stepwise method. Why: Evidence-first reasoning and adaptive questioning reach information formal routes cannot.
- [procedure] 1) Take notes in shorthand during the exchange. 2) Transcribe them into full text. 3) Make fair copies and check them against the original. When: Recording a witness's statement for later use. Why: Shorthand is useless until transcribed and verified.
- [procedure] 1) Ask a pointed question the witness has no easy reason to answer. 2) Note the stated reason for presence. 3) Note any deflection or redirected question. 4) Note whether the response shows any expressive reaction. When: Watching a witness for evasion during questioning. Why: Evasion and deflection reveal more than a plausible answer, and a lack of reaction is a weak signal either way.
- [procedure] 1) Choose a routine request that looks ordinary. 2) Let the other party's own procedure produce the record. 3) Accept the answer without stating the true purpose. When: the true purpose would be refused if stated openly. Why: routine requests draw less scrutiny than direct questions.
- [pitfall] Relayed or remote reports stay unverified until checked on the ground or against independent records, since each relay can alter times, wording or order. When: Findings come from a distant source or a timeline relies on accounts relayed by other people. (x2)
- [pitfall] A sympathetic listener may accept a weak theory too quickly, so their agreement is not proof that the reasoning holds. When: when a friendly reviewer hears a theory. Why: Goodwill can override scrutiny.
- [pitfall] Answering an objection by repeating the original claim rather than giving a new reason fails to address the objection. When: Defending a theory against a challenge. Why: Restatement adds no evidence to the argument.
- [pitfall] Vague witness answers leave gaps that can be misread as confirmation. When: Accepting a witness's non-specific answers. Why: Silence or vagueness is not corroboration.
- [pitfall] An interviewer's private theory forming before the account is complete can steer which answers get recorded and which are overlooked. When: Interviewing while the account is still incomplete. Why: A premature conclusion biases what is noticed and recorded.
- [pitfall] A witness who has already been alarmed may give a different account on a second telling, so repeated accounts should be compared for shifts. When: Re-interviewing a witness after an initial alarming questioning. Why: Alarm can alter recall, so differences between tellings are signals to examine.
- [pitfall] A date misremembered by a witness can break a chain of reasoning, so the date should be checked against a record rather than memory. When: A timeline relies on a witness's recollection. Why: Memory is unreliable for specific dates and times.
- [pitfall] Accepting a plausible, on-topic reason for a witness's presence at face value can hide a real motive. When: Evaluating a witness's stated reason for being present. Why: Innocent-looking reasons are easy to give and hard to verify without checking.
- [pitfall] A physiological spike can reflect nervousness rather than guilt, especially when the questioner already suspects the secret. When: Interpreting a stress reading during questioning. Why: Anxiety from suspicion produces the same response as guilt.

## mystery investigation reasoning
- [model] When one observation could have several causes, list every plausible source, compare each against the other known facts, and keep the conclusion open until one candidate clearly fits best. When: A single sighting or clue has multiple possible explanations. Why: Accepting the first explanation risks error, especially when fear or emotion favors the nearest answer.
- [rule] Investigate improbable possibilities and long-shot leads before excluding them; dismissing them on assumption leaves an unclosed branch that can later undermine the conclusion. When: A conclusion depends on a fact that is probably false but unproved, or when deciding whether to continue a long-shot lead. Why: An unlikely claim can still be true, and only inquiry shows whether it is; a hopeless-seeming lead may still need closing to make the conclusion defensible.
- [rule] Hold every competing explanation to the same test: it must account for all facts and physical marks, not only the dramatic ones. When: Choosing between or evaluating rival explanations. Why: A theory explaining only striking details can mislead, and applying one standard prevents any theory from escaping scrutiny.
- [rule] Start from the assumption that an error may exist and look for it in the record, rather than accepting a verdict as final. When: Reviewing a case that was closed by a verdict. Why: Even expert work can contain mistakes.
- [rule] Prevent parties from exchanging open accusations early, since public accusation can escalate a private dispute into a scandal or criminal matter. When: Investigating a dispute between people who may distrust each other. Why: Once accusations are public, witnesses become guarded and the investigators lose control of the situation.
- [heuristic] Before treating a motive as proof, check whether other parties had the same motive; a shared motive can point to a rival as readily as the accused. When: a real motive is found for one suspect. Why: A motive that applies to several parties does not single out one of them.
- [heuristic] When a wider party with power and reason to act emerges, reopen the question of who benefited from the victim's death. When: new testimony names an enemy with power and motive. Why: A new candidate with means and motive can overturn an investigation built on a single suspect.
- [heuristic] Reason from physical properties: if a material's bulk moisture would have to be overcome first, a theory that depends on intense heat is unlikely. When: Evaluating a theory that requires extreme heat to produce an effect. Why: Water content acts as a barrier that absorbs energy before the object can burn.
- [procedure] 1) Settle the foundational fact of the case, such as the identity of remains. 2) Only then examine who acted and why, avoiding motive speculation while basic facts remain uncertain. When: An investigation has a basic fact question and a later question about motive or culpability that depends on it. Why: Motive built on an unsettled fact tends to fit evidence to a preferred story, and culpability cannot be assessed reliably until the fact is established.
- [procedure] 1) List every rival explanation early, including human, medical and psychological ones. 2) Find a test that separates them. 3) Check whether the phenomenon depends on place, routine or particular people. 4) Test each rival against the same facts. When: A phenomenon is reported and could have a supernatural or human cause. Why: Each rival predicts different results, so a decisive test can discriminate between them.
- [procedure] 1) List every possible outcome that covers the case. 2) Test each against the facts. 3) Discard options that conflict with evidence. 4) Check whether the remaining option is the only one left standing. When: A case has a small set of alternatives that can be exhaustively enumerated. Why: Eliminating alternatives against facts reaches a definite answer, but only if the list truly covers all possibilities.
- [procedure] 1) State every candidate explanation up front and number it. 2) For each, list what it would require to be true. 3) Dismiss candidates on evidence, keeping physically possible but remote ones labelled as remote. 4) Stop once one explanation is confirmed by direct proof. When: Several explanations fit the facts and a wrong choice is costly. Why: Listing all candidates prevents premature dismissal and ensures each rejection rests on evidence.
- [procedure] 1) Restate the conclusion. 2) List the premises it is drawn from. 3) Ask whether it follows from them or depends on an unstated link. 4) Ask for the specific circumstances behind the link. When: Testing whether a claimed anomaly or conclusion is logically sound. Why: A conclusion may seem valid while resting on a premise nobody stated, which specific questions expose.
- [procedure] 1) List every explanation the facts allow. 2) Sort them into outside and inside groups by whether they involve the named parties. 3) Dismiss outside explanations with a conclusive item. 4) Test each remaining suspect in turn. 5) Set motive aside until the list is formed. When: Starting an inquiry where facts are known but motives are not. Why: Setting motive aside first keeps the probabilities balanced rather than fitting facts to a favoured suspect.
- [procedure] 1) Use idle time to build explanations from partial reports. 2) Keep each theory private until new facts arrive. 3) Test the theory against those facts before committing to it. When: when an investigator has partial reports and must weigh facts. Why: withholding a theory avoids bending later facts to fit an earlier public claim.
- [procedure] 1) Separate established facts from asserted claims. 2) List each competing explanation. 3) Test each against measurable traces, using controlled experiments where needed. 4) Conclude only after tests are complete and the evidence chain holds. When: Several explanations fit the same facts and each has a credible expert behind it. Why: Expert opinions can conflict, and only measured traces can separate them.
- [procedure] 1) Check the physical facts first, such as fire damage, soot pattern and undamaged surfaces. 2) Test each candidate cause, exotic or mundane, against those facts. 3) Accept only the cause that fits every fact. When: Evaluating competing causes of an unusual death or event. Why: Physical facts constrain every candidate, so a cause must match all of them.
- [procedure] 1) Test the theory alone or with a second visit before informing others. 2) Confirm the mechanism is present before acting on it. When: An investigator has a theory that could lead to a false accusation. Why: A verifying return prevents error and protects against acting on a single frame.
- [pitfall] Claiming to know the answer before reading the evidence can blind an investigator to contrary facts. When: Starting an investigation with a strong prior belief. Why: Confidence without evidence filters out information that does not fit the belief.
- [pitfall] A document can seem to confirm what the reader already believes, so strengthened conviction should prompt a check of the reasoning. When: Reading a document that matches a prior theory. Why: Readers accept evidence that fits their belief more readily than contrary evidence.
- [pitfall] Arresting suspects who do not match the trace profile wastes time and misleads the investigation. When: Suspects are detained based on general suspicion rather than the evidence. Why: Unmatched arrests are released, and the actual culprit stays free.
- [pitfall] Do not assume an odd absence means foul play before ordinary explanations are checked. When: a physical absence appears suspicious. Why: Accidental, natural or routine causes are more common and must be ruled out first.
- [pitfall] Do not accept a common motive without checking whether it fits all the traces of the act. When: a familiar motive explains only part of the evidence. Why: A motive that leaves some traces unexplained is probably incomplete.
- [pitfall] Eliminating alternatives only works when the list covers every possibility; an incomplete list can lead to a confident but wrong conclusion. When: Building the set of alternatives to test. Why: A missing option can survive elimination unnoticed, so the remaining answer may be false.
- [pitfall] Ruling out an alternative using assumptions about a person's character or habits, instead of evidence, weakens the elimination. When: Testing an option during elimination. Why: Unverified assumptions about character can dismiss a possibility that the facts do not actually exclude.
- [pitfall] Rejecting a hypothesis because it is unpleasant rather than improbable biases the analysis. When: Weighing candidate explanations. Why: Emotional aversion removes viable candidates without evidence.
- [pitfall] Alerting a person whose interests are affected while checks are still running can destroy evidence or let them prepare an account. When: Investigating a case where an interested party is involved. Why: Early disclosure gives the interested party time to influence what the checks find.
- [pitfall] A certain but uninformative statement, such as a trivially true disjunction, does not advance an investigation. When: Evaluating a conclusion that is certainly true. Why: Certainty alone carries no information about which explanation holds.
- [pitfall] When the one feature that would confirm a match cannot be examined, the case stays ambiguous and cannot be closed. When: The key distinguishing evidence is missing or unexaminable. Why: Without the decisive feature, general agreement cannot resolve identity either way.
- [pitfall] Guessing the cause of an unrecorded mark and presenting the guess as fact. When: Writing up identifying marks from a report. Why: An unrecorded cause is speculation, not a recorded fact.
- [pitfall] A single outcome or a long run of results is not proof of deliberate control on its own. Why: Unlikely events still occur by chance.
- [fact] The more general a proposition, the more certain it tends to be, and the less it tells about any single case. When: Reasoning from a broad rule to a specific individual or event. Why: Broad statements average over many cases, so they lose predictive force for one.
- [fact] An accepted explanation can be used to dismiss evidence, and it may persist for generations without testing. When: Evaluating an explanation that closes a case. Why: A plausible story that fits one striking fact can stop further examination.

## reproduction and experimental testing
- [mechanism] A surprising result that contradicts the expected outcome can force a revision of theory, but an incidental action can destroy the subject before the result is confirmed. When: A controlled test is interrupted before it is repeated or verified. Why: A single unconfirmed surprising result remains weak evidence, and losing its source removes any chance to check it.
- [mechanism] A controlled experiment reproduces one suspected cause in isolation, showing whether that cause alone can produce the effect. When: Several causes could explain one outcome. Why: Isolating a single variable reveals whether it is sufficient on its own.
- [rule] Prepare and test equipment, such as a lock, and check the wiring and placement of any tripwire or alarm before relying on it during an investigation. When: Before entering a space with equipment, or when using a tripwire or alarm to fix timing. Why: Untested equipment can fail at the critical moment, and an unverified signal can fail silently and mislead the timeline.
- [rule] Playtest route durations against the timings given to players, by walking the routes yourself, before relying on them in a mystery. When: during level and schedule validation. Why: Self-testing catches impossible timings.
- [rule] Check the identity and purity of test reagents and samples yourself rather than trusting their labels, and confirm that samples cannot be swapped. When: When a test result depends on materials and one operator. Why: Labels and operator trust can hide errors or tampering in the procedure.
- [heuristic] Test a physical assumption directly, such as whether a sound carries, instead of assuming it, then check whether anyone reacts. When: when a theory depends on sound or sight reaching a person. Why: perception depends on physical conditions that are easy to misjudge.
- [heuristic] Do not treat one successful test as proof, because a single unnoticed breach in the setup can undo the result. When: Interpreting results of an isolation or control test. Why: Controlled setups depend on the enclosure being complete.
- [heuristic] Record control samples under the same conditions as the event, including the investigator's own movement, since any nearby movement contaminates the record. When: Collecting reference data for a sensitive measurement. Why: Uncontrolled activity can disturb the instrument more than the signal being measured.
- [heuristic] Comparing samples side by side under identical conditions can rule out a cause quickly. When: Two samples may differ in origin and conditions are easy to match. Why: Identical conditions remove lighting or setup as explanations for the difference.
- [procedure] 1) Run the test with the subject aware of the trigger. 2) Repeat it with the trigger hidden. 3) Compare results; a reaction that disappears when hidden came from knowledge.
- [procedure] 1) Hide the claimed source from the subject. 2) Have a substitute perform the same action. 3) Compare the reactions; equal reactions mean the source is not causing the effect. When: when testing whether a claimed effect is real. Why: separates the real effect from the subject's expectations.
- [procedure] 1) State the claimed action, such as a throw or a drop. 2) Repeat that action under matching conditions. 3) Compare the resulting mark or position with the evidence found on site. When: Checking whether an object could have moved as a theory claims. Why: A reproduction shows whether the claim is physically possible.
- [procedure] 1) Recreate the suspect's likely actions in the real location. 2) Time the operation. 3) Predict the physical marks it would leave. 4) Search for those marks. 5) Judge whether the sequence was possible. When: A sequence of actions is claimed impossible or implausible and the location can still be recreated. Why: A physical trial shows feasibility and tells investigators where to look for traces.
- [procedure] 1) Separate whether a person believes an act happened from whether the effect could have occurred at the claimed time. 2) Check the substance's timing against the claimed hour. 3) Test by analysis when possible. When: A dosing or death time is disputed. Why: Physical facts settle claims that testimony cannot.
- [procedure] 1) Name the claimed cause and the action that should end the effect. 2) Perform that action once as a controlled trial. 3) Watch whether the state changes. 4) Collect other reports of the same action and check for a pattern. When: A state is claimed to respond to a specific action that can be repeated. Why: A deliberate intervention helps separate a real cause from coincidence.
- [procedure] 1) Once a theory explains the evidence, arrange conditions where the subject performs the act under observation. 2) Remove interfering observers. 3) Watch each stage and match it to the original incidents. When: A theory explains the evidence but no direct witness can confirm it. Why: A reproduced act confirms the theory more strongly than argument alone.
- [procedure] Run a calm, fixed pre-event routine so the game can log a baseline that later deviations are measured against. When: Setting up a scene where a change must be detected. Why: A stable baseline makes later changes measurable.
- [procedure] 1) State the candidate environmental cause, such as softening, heat or moisture. 2) Check known cases where the material behaved that way. 3) Treat the result as plausible until it is confirmed. When: an alteration might come from a material change rather than a human act. Why: Testing a natural mechanism against known cases separates it from a human explanation.
- [procedure] 1) Identify the mechanism the explanation relies on. 2) Recreate that mechanism under controlled conditions. 3) Observe whether the expected result occurs. 4) Reject the explanation if it fails. When: Testing a theory about how an event happened. Why: Demonstrating the mechanism directly checks whether it could produce the observed result.
- [procedure] 1) Measure force with a pressure gauge while applying a tool to a test surface. 2) Reduce force in repeated trials until marks match the original. 3) Use the lowest force that reproduces the marks as the estimate. When: Tool marks are present and the tool can be reproduced in trials. Why: The force needed to make the marks reveals how strong an intruder must have been, and which defence was weak.
- [procedure] 1) Rebuild the suspected setting, matching layout, materials and key positions. 2) Run each candidate action in the same fixed sequence. 3) Compare outcomes trial by trial. 4) Restore the scene so the test leaves no trace. When: An event was heard but not seen, and its source depends on location. Why: A faithful replica with fixed actions makes trials comparable and the location variable testable.
- [procedure] 1) List each anomaly as its own question, including small ones. 2) Propose an ordinary mechanical or sensory cause for each. 3) Check that the proposed mechanism physically works by rebuilding and testing it. 4) Label any event that stays unresolved. When: Before accepting a supernatural explanation for uncanny events. Why: Each strange event may have a traceable cause, and testing separates real causes from guesses.
- [procedure] 1) Identify the physical mechanism that produces the evidence. 2) Reproduce that mechanism with the tools a forger would use. 3) Compare the result with the disputed evidence to test whether it was genuine. When: when a piece of evidence may be forged. Why: Reproducing the method lets the player demonstrate forgery rather than assert it.
- [pitfall] Changing several variables at once, such as travel, company, routine and treatment, makes it impossible to tell which change caused the difference in outcome. When: Re-running a test after a failure. Why: Attribution of cause requires isolating one variable. (x2)
- [pitfall] The operator's manner and expectations shape the subject's response but are often unrecorded, so results cannot be fully checked afterward. When: running an interrogation or measurement test. Why: the operator is part of the measurement.
- [pitfall] A wager or prior claim by the tester can bias how a test is set up and read, so keep the expected result out of the setup.
- [pitfall] Outside people with their own plans can disturb a test, so their interference should be treated as a threat to the setup. When: Others outside the test team can affect the subject or setting. Why: Unplanned contact changes the conditions the test depends on.
- [pitfall] A reproduction that misses a key condition makes a failed result meaningless, so it cannot refute the theory.
- [pitfall] A reproduction by a different person can give a different result, because strength and angle change where the object lands. When: Repeating an action with someone other than the original actor. Why: The physical result depends on the actor's force and angle.
- [pitfall] Do not treat a material explanation as proven just because it could happen in principle. When: a natural cause is proposed for an observed change. Why: Possibility is not confirmation, and the explanation needs a test.
- [pitfall] Repeating an experiment until it yields the result a theory needs corrupts the test.
- [pitfall] A replica that differs from the original in position or detail can change the result, and without a standard fixed action comparing trials is meaningless.

## search and surveillance
- [definition] Concealed observation places silent witnesses where they see a subject unseen, so their reports stay reliable and the subject's natural behaviour is undisturbed.
- [behavior] A confined person can infer hidden observation from others' behaviour, such as a helper avoiding eye contact or dimming a light, before the watching is confirmed. When: A person is held in a room that may be overlooked. Why: Unusual conduct by companions signals that they are being watched.
- [rule] Keep each NPC's current room or position in the game state, and update records whenever someone moves, so answers to player questions stay accurate. When: A room or sleeping arrangement changes during play. Why: Unreported changes send investigators and players to the wrong place.
- [rule] Search a small area around a paced mark rather than a single point, because paced distances are approximate. When: Locating a site from measured distances. Why: Pacing introduces error, so the true spot may lie nearby.
- [heuristic] Broad coverage of peripheral locations or searches of all household members often yields nothing, so aim effort at the likely source. When: When investigators consider broad surveillance of peripheral locations or searching all household members. Why: Wide coverage not aimed at the likely source is unproductive; staff rarely keep stolen property on the premises, and broad searches produce protest that costs goodwill.
- [heuristic] A reflective surface can reveal that a character is watching someone, allowing observation without confrontation.
- [heuristic] Test indirect observation, such as a reflection, by asking how it showed each movement, since indirect views can miss details. When: A witness saw an event only through a mirror, glass or similar reflection. Why: Reflected views are partial and can distort what is visible.
- [heuristic] Once a target is lost in a crowd, infer where they went from what is already known about their goals. Why: known intent narrows likely destinations.
- [heuristic] A hidden item's likely location can be inferred from its owner's character, such as a tidy person keeping papers in a particular cubby. When: The owner's habits are known and the target is likely hidden by routine.
- [heuristic] Avoid a costly search built on a weak premise, because a long empty search damages credibility. When: When deciding whether to commit resources to a physical search. Why: Failed searches cost time and erode trust in the investigator.
- [heuristic] Examine concealment spaces such as linings and closets by hand when an object may hide something. When: A suspect's belongings may conceal a small item. Why: Hidden compartments are common places to conceal items.
- [procedure] 1) Build rapport with a helpful side character. 2) Ask about habits and routine during ordinary conversation. 3) Record routine facts without it looking like an interrogation.
- [procedure] 1) Return to the scene and record any changes. 2) Compare repeated marks with earlier copies. 3) Ask others discreetly about unfamiliar people. 4) Combine these clues to infer what the witness will not say. When: a witness will not disclose the decisive fact.
- [procedure] 1) Post someone to guard the body or scene. 2) Request fingerprints and physical evidence. 3) Question staff only in a general way. 4) Send investigators to search the rest of the premises, such as basement and yard. When: at the start of an investigation.
- [procedure] 1) List every exit and what lies beyond it. 2) Check whether a route out exists. 3) Post watchers at the points a person's route must pass. When: Verifying whether a person could leave a building unseen. Why: Known exits and watched chokepoints determine which routes are possible.
- [procedure] 1) Compare a specific written plan or map against the actual layout. 2) Pace out the measured distances and mark the indicated spot. 3) Clear any growth or cover from the candidate surface. 4) Tap and scrape the surface, marking hollow or changed sound or texture to dig. 5) Bring in a helper with tools once the search narrows. When: A concealed feature is documented but its exact position is lost. Why: Combining documented measurements with direct physical testing narrows the search to a precise spot.
- [procedure] 1) Place a marker where a hidden intruder would have to disturb it. 2) Test the marker by opening the door yourself to confirm it responds. 3) Check the marker's state after each event to learn what happened unseen. When: A space must be watched when no witness can stay there. Why: Tested indicators provide a trustworthy physical record of passage.
- [procedure] 1) Sound out the floor, ceiling and walls of the space. 2) Check each surface for marks. 3) Record findings before sealing or leaving the space. When: Searching a room for a hidden mechanism or object. Why: Covering every surface avoids missing marks left by a device.
- [procedure] 1) Photograph the scene by day from a fixed position. 2) Return at night from the same position and take a second picture. 3) Compare the two images for changes. When: Checking whether a scene changed overnight. Why: A fixed viewpoint makes differences attributable to events, not framing.
- [procedure] 1) Rig a camera and light to the point of interest. 2) Record any opening or movement. 3) Use the recording to separate a hoax from the event. When: Testing whether a reported event is real. Why: A recording captures what witnesses cannot reliably report.
- [pitfall] A search that looks only in expected places, or feels thorough, can miss items, so a negative result does not prove absence; check methodically. When: When searching a scene or area for evidence or a hidden object, especially after finding nothing. Why: Searchers can miss hiding places they did not consider; confidence in a search does not guarantee every item was found. (x2)
- [pitfall] Concluding a missing item such as tickets left the building is unreliable if its search, or a paired object's search, was not exhaustive. When: A search for missing items was not exhaustive. Why: Small items can be concealed on a person and missed by a limited search.
- [pitfall] An indirect search from outside the room, such as probing with a tool through a window, can miss small traces that close examination would reveal. When: When an investigator cannot enter the scene. Why: Indirect tools reach only surfaces they touch, not fine details.
- [pitfall] A search run without clear procedure can waste time and find nothing, even when the target is nearby. When: When a search area is covered without a defined order or method. Why: Unstructured searching misses ground and time runs out.
- [pitfall] Relying on one channel, such as newspapers, misses people who could identify the target by description. Why: Each channel reaches a different audience.
- [pitfall] Searching a large collection of records is slow, and effort can be wasted on the wrong samples. When: When comparing a trace against many records by hand. Why: Manual comparison scales poorly with collection size.
- [pitfall] A recording may confirm that an event happened without identifying its cause, such as a door half open in a photo.
- [fact] An investigator can establish that a person is present, and roughly who, from recent orderly traces, before ever seeing that person.
- [fact] Long surveillance on foot can be tedious and may last weeks, so it demands sustained patience from the watcher. When: Planning a long surveillance task. Why: Covert following on foot is slow and repetitive.

## timeline and event sequencing
- [rule] Test a clock's error against a second source before using it; when a witness's time conflicts with other sources, trust whichever independent clock is more reliable. When: A timeline depends on a clock or watch, or a witness's time conflicts with other sources. Why: Unknown error can shift a whole window; independent fixed references anchor a timeline better than one person's memory. (x2)
- [heuristic] Act on physical traces as soon as possible, because some clues degrade or disappear within hours if left unattended or handled. When: Arriving at a scene where traces may decay or be cleaned. Why: Delay lets fragile traces evaporate or be lost.
- [heuristic] Physical evidence such as mismatched torn stub halves can show that two people entered at different moments. When: Paired physical tokens are used to date entry. Why: Tokens torn at different times fail to line up.
- [heuristic] A killer who expects a later discovery plans a wider window than the actual one, so the real window is often narrower than intended. When: Reasoning about when a culprit planned an act to be discovered. Why: Earlier discovery shrinks the safe window the culprit assumed.
- [heuristic] Estimate the start of a long-running dispute by subtracting each witness's stated age from the event's date, then compare the results across witnesses. When: When a witness gives an age and the dispute's duration is unknown. Why: Ages stated at a known event fix an approximate date for earlier events.
- [heuristic] Reconstruct events from a range of source types, including testimony, petitions, diaries, expense records and earlier histories, and weigh each against the others. When: When key events are known only from scattered papers. Why: Different source types reveal different facts, and cross-checking reduces the risk of relying on one biased account.
- [heuristic] Confirm an individual's stated dates and recurring intervals against other observers, since a precise count from one witness may still be wrong. When: A recurring event must be checked against a timeline. Why: Precision signals care but does not guarantee accuracy.
- [procedure] 1) Group marks by likely source and label each group. 2) Check which mark lies on top of which; the mark on top was made later. 3) Read where each mark sits. 4) Chain the layers into a sequence. 5) Present the result as checkable inferences. When: Several traces touch the same object and their order is uncertain. Why: Layering reveals which events came first, since physical marks record the order in which they were made.
- [procedure] 1) Gather every report about the event. 2) Arrange all reports by date and time. 3) Compare adjacent entries to find gaps where the sequence is unexplained. When: several accounts each cover part of an event. Why: ordering exposes missing intervals.
- [procedure] 1) Start with the earliest dated record. 2) Add each later record in order, such as grants, purchases, petitions and debt discharges. 3) Note when each debt was cleared. 4) Cross-check against neighbours' records. 5) Anchor entries to public events. When: reconstructing a family's or party's history from scattered paperwork. Why: Ordered dated entries reveal turning points and gaps that others sources can check.
- [procedure] 1) Anchor the timeline to an independent forecast of when the event will occur. 2) Log each change with a clock time or interval. 3) Log unchanged intervals too. 4) Have a second person keep notes. When: a sequence of states must be reconstructed and its timing will be disputed. Why: Independent anchors and two records make the timeline checkable.
- [procedure] 1) Establish the household's normal routine as a baseline. 2) Record each witness claim with its time. 3) Flag claims that contradict each other. 4) Note which events were seen and which only heard. When: Building a timeline from staff who were awake but did not watch events. Why: A baseline makes deviations visible and contradictions and weak sources easier to mark.
- [procedure] 1) Order the events by time. 2) Follow one actor's sequence at a time. 3) Anchor each stage in physical traces or witness reports. 4) Mark which steps are supported and which are inferred. When: Reconstructing a death or incident from overlapping events. Why: Separating sequences by actor and anchoring them in evidence prevents overlap from misleading.
- [procedure] 1) Date the warrants or orders. 2) Note the place of each examination. 3) Record the names of officers involved. 4) Use these anchors to order the events. When: When ordering events from scattered official documents. Why: Dates, places and names are fixed points that can be matched across records.
- [procedure] 1) Find each undated record's date by linking it to an independent event that is unrelated to the others. 2) Confirm each date with a second unrelated anchor. 3) List dated entries side by side to reveal recurring intervals. When: When a witness's memory of a date is doubtful or a sequence must be rebuilt from several records. Why: A single date with no independent check can be wrong, and recalled dates drift, while patterns only show when entries are compared.
- [procedure] 1) Start with the earliest event. 2) Record each fact in its own brief numbered line. 3) Re-read the log, mark doubtful entries, and correct errors by erasure or annotation. 4) File it apart from routine records. When: events happened across several places and their order is uncertain. Why: numbered, ordered entries turn scattered recollections into a reviewable timeline.
- [procedure] 1) Fix a dated starting point and a dated earlier point. 2) Define the window between them. 3) Request every record within the window. 4) Mark each stage as known or unknown, never filling gaps by guess. 5) Use each stage to name the next source to ask. When: Reconstructing a person's history against a fixed period. Why: A complete, bounded window prevents gaps from being missed and keeps assumptions out of the record.

## objections and escalation
- [rule] Warn your superior before acting alone on a doubted conclusion, so that later success is credited to your earlier judgment rather than to ambition. When: A lead has closed a case and a subordinate disagrees with it. Why: Early warning protects standing and makes independent work look principled.
- [rule] Answer a strong opposing view with a specific reason tied to the case, not a general appeal to how things usually go. When: Defending a conclusion against a counterargument. Why: General appeals to base rates do not address why this particular case differs.
- [heuristic] Weight each objection by its actual strength: overstating a weak one wastes effort, and ignoring a strong one misleads. Why: objections carry unequal weight.
- [heuristic] A theory survives objections about detail, but objections to its overall structure require rebuilding the chain of reasoning. When: Evaluating which objections to a theory matter. Why: Detail errors can be patched while structural errors undermine the whole argument.
- [procedure] 1) Note the ambiguous pattern without acting. 2) Set a clear trigger for escalation, such as a suspect trying to leave the area. 3) Act only when the trigger fires. When: When a suspect's behavior is suspicious but not conclusive.
- [pitfall] A repeated or odd request, or a cover that a third party can check, can draw suspicion and cause the pretext to collapse. When: a pretext is repeated or can be verified. Why: repetition and checkable details give observers grounds to doubt the cover.

## accepting printed drama
- [pitfall] Accepting the most dramatic account because it appears in print, and overlooking that a mundane cause must also fit every fact, leads to wrong conclusions.

## approaching reluctant helpers
- [procedure] 1) Identify the person most useful to the inquiry who is least willing to accept help. 2) Offer help informally, without pressure or an obligation to pay. 3) Assign each investigator to the person they can best approach. When: The most useful witness refuses direct assistance. Why: Pressure breaks the trust later needed, and matched roles make the approach feel natural.

## blind sample labelling
- [procedure] 1) Collect each item and place it in a numbered container. 2) Record the owner against the number in a separate key. 3) Keep the key away from the person doing the testing. When: Testing samples whose source must stay hidden from the examiner. Why: A blind label prevents the examiner's expectations from influencing results.

## checking expert claims
- [heuristic] Do not dismiss an expert outright; verify the specific source and test the conclusion before accepting or rejecting it. When: An expert's claim conflicts with another conclusion. Why: Experts can be refuted by a more careful reading of details, not by dismissal.

## chemical precipitation confirmation
- [fact] Adding a solvent to a sample can reveal a chemical identity through whether a precipitate forms. When: Confirming a chemical identity in a sample. Why: Precipitate formation is a checkable reaction signature.

## climax staging plan
- [procedure] 1) Set a deadline and plan backward from the gathering time. 2) Assemble all suspects and expert witnesses in one room, with an observing official. 3) Present evidence in sequence, narrowing the group at each step. 4) Test each suspect physically. 5) Switch to full light at the decisive moment to reveal the result. When: A climax where all suspects can be assembled and the evidence is ready. Why: Scheduled, sequenced staging builds suspense and a clear reveal.

## control baseline in testing
- [rule] Keep a control subject beside a test subject, with only one receiving the treatment, to establish a baseline. When: Running a controlled experiment on test subjects. Why: Without a control, an observed effect cannot be separated from ordinary variation.

## controlled sample comparison
- [procedure] 1) Test each source on the same subject. 2) Compare the resulting effects. 3) Link sources that produce the same effect. Shared effect on one test subject can connect two samples. When: Physical samples must be tied to a common origin. Why: Identical responses on the same test subject give a traceable link between samples.

## controlled variable testing
- [procedure] 1) State a hypothesis and predict the result. 2) Change one factor while holding all others fixed. 3) Compare the outcome with the earlier baseline. 4) Restore the variable and retest to confirm. When: Testing a suspected cause in a system with several interacting parts. Why: Isolating one variable shows whether it is the true cause.

## covert evidence collection
- [tradeoff] Covert collection avoids alerting suspects but carries ethical and personal cost, and is justified only when suspicion has a real basis. When: Deciding whether to gather evidence secretly. Why: Secrecy protects the investigation but can harm trust if no cause for suspicion exists.

## covert word-association probe
- [procedure] 1) Open with neutral prompts to set a quick, automatic rhythm. 2) Switch suddenly to charged prompts while the subject is off guard. 3) Keep the pace fast so there is no time to compose an answer. 4) Time each response and log it alongside a physiological reading. When: A subject hides one specific piece of knowledge and likely trigger words can be guessed. Why: Hesitation and physiological spikes on charged words can reveal the concealed knowledge.

## description-based identification
- [procedure] 1) Compare a body's observed features with a written description. 2) Count the matched features and separate common traits from distinctive ones. 3) Confirm only with distinctive traits that can actually be checked. When: Deciding whether a found body matches a missing-person description. Why: General agreement across common traits fits many people, so only a verifiable distinctive trait can confirm identity.

## emotional commitment in review
- [pitfall] An investigator's emotional commitment can lead them to assume an error before checking the record, distorting the review. When: Reopening a case the reviewer feels strongly about. Why: Conviction can precede evidence.

## estimate tolerance
- [heuristic] A small discrepancy in an estimated measurement, such as height, is better explained by the uncertainty of the estimate than treated as a contradiction. When: Comparing an estimated measurement to a recorded value. Why: Estimates carry error, so small gaps do not by themselves rule out a match.

## explaining missing body part
- [procedure] 1) Check whether the absence matches a reported missing person with that condition. 2) Establish whether removal happened before or after death. 3) Ask why the part was removed. 4) Test each candidate reason against personal detail. When: a body part is absent from remains. Why: Checking records, timing, motive and personal facts in order separates accidental, natural and deliberate causes.

## force-based identification
- [pitfall] Tool force shows what the tool required, not who used it; a single matching trial proves little, so repeated comparison is needed. Why: Similar marks can arise from different people and forces.

## hazardous test safety
- [rule] Do not run a test that risks lethal harm without protective measures and a witness. When: Designing a test that could cause serious injury. Why: Hazardous tests need protection and oversight to limit danger.

## identification by hidden marks
- [procedure] 1) Image the body with X-ray or scanner to reveal hidden structures. 2) Note each mark: dental work, surgical metal, old fractures, tattoos. 3) Match each mark against its own independent record. 4) Treat identity as conclusive only when several markers each match their own record. When: Body is unrecognizable or hidden and reliable records of teeth, surgery or injuries exist. Why: Each independent record confirms identity separately, and several confirmations together are far stronger than one.

## identifying remains from records
- [procedure] 1) Obtain a full written description of the missing person, including illnesses, injuries and names of treating doctors and dentists. 2) Prioritise dental records. 3) Compare each known injury with the matching part of the remains. When: Remains are found and a known person is the candidate. Why: Dental records can identify a skull found later, and injuries must be checked against the correct body side.

## incomplete witness descriptions
- [pitfall] Assuming a witness's description is complete when the witness did not closely observe the person's health. When: Relying on witness descriptions for identification. Why: Gaps in observation produce gaps in the description.

## independent inspection checklist
- [procedure] 1) Work from a fixed written checklist. 2) Count each bone or part against it. 3) Take your own measurements. 4) Compare with the official findings. 5) Record evidence at once and sketch positions on site. 6) Ask few questions, listen closely, and follow up leads the observations suggest. When: An investigator must confirm or challenge another party's findings on a limited visit. Why: Independent measurement and a fixed checklist expose gaps and errors in the official account.

## mark without explanation
- [mechanism] A recorded mark can identify a person even when its cause is not recorded, so identification does not require knowing how the mark arose. When: Matching a recorded scar or mark to remains. Why: Identity depends on the mark's presence and description, not its origin.

## matching signs not proof
- [heuristic] A body that matches a poison's appearance is not proof of poisoning, because a natural illness can produce similar signs. When: an investigation rests on physical appearance. Why: Similar symptoms can have different causes.

## measured dose escalation
- [rule] When testing a suspected toxin, increase the dose in measured steps, log the effect at each step, and set a ceiling for safety. When: Testing a toxic substance. Why: Stepwise dosing with a limit reveals the response curve while bounding harm.

## misread expert exceptions
- [pitfall] Quoting a rule without reading the case it cites can produce a false exception that seems to overturn the rule. Why: The omitted detail in the source case is what resolves the anomaly.

## natural versus poisoning test
- [procedure] 1) Compare symptom onset with the time since the suspected dose. 2) Check whether the dose and timing fit a poisoning pattern. 3) Test tissues for traces, since a long, small dose should leave them. 4) Weigh the result before deciding. When: physical signs look like poisoning but may have a natural cause. Why: Timing, dose and tissue evidence separate poisoning from illness that merely looks similar.

## negative results
- [heuristic] A negative finding, such as normal bones, still narrows the field of possibilities even though it names no one. When: An inspection finds nothing abnormal. Why: Eliminating options is progress toward identification.

## negatives mistaken for progress
- [pitfall] Confusing a long run of negative results with real progress, or volunteering conclusions beyond one's qualifications, such as an exact identity. When: Reporting on a limited inspection. Why: Negatives narrow options only slowly, and non-experts overreach when they state definite identities.

## observed test reporting
- [rule] Watch the test result directly and use a written instruction, rather than relying on a verbal report. When: Running a test that requires precise execution. Why: Direct observation and written instructions reduce error and misreporting.

## permanent identifying marks
- [fact] Old healed fractures, long scars and distinctive tattoos are lasting features that can identify a person when the body is later found. When: A body may never be found and identity must rest on unchanging features. Why: These features do not change over time, unlike appearance.

## press-driven haste
- [pitfall] Sensational press coverage can force action before the facts are settled.

## probe trigger timing
- [rule] The value of a word-association probe depends on the timing of the switch to charged prompts, not on the neutral opening. When: Designing a word-association probe. Why: The neutral opening sets up the rhythm, and the switch produces the signal.

## probing ambiguous symptoms
- [rule] Ask what else could cause a reported symptom before treating it as evidence of one body part or condition. When: Interpreting a witness's account of a symptom such as a limp or pain. Why: A single symptom often has more than one cause, so it does not point clearly to one site.

## protecting unchanged components
- [rule] Shield the parts that are not under test so that the only changed variable is the one being examined. When: Running a controlled test on a machine or system. Why: Stray changes in other parts can confound the result.

## record keeping
- [rule] Read back notes only with a clear record of when they were taken, because undated notes are open to challenge. When: Presenting written or recorded statements as evidence. Why: Missing timestamps let opposing parties dispute reliability.

## record on-site placement
- [rule] Record the position and orientation of finds with sketches on site, since placement may matter for later analysis. When: Recovering physical evidence at a site. Why: Positional context can be lost once objects are moved.

## record-dependent identification
- [heuristic] A physical marker is only as strong as the document that matches it; without a trustworthy record, the marker cannot establish identity. When: Using hidden marks to identify a body. Why: The match depends on the reliability of the comparison record.

## reference chain check
- [procedure] 1) Identify the referee named in the application. 2) Verify the referee through an independent source such as a directory. 3) Request a written reply. 4) Judge the reference against the claims made. When: Verifying a person's background through the people who vouched for them. Why: A reference is only as reliable as its writer, and independent checks reduce the risk of error.

## review without key knowledge
- [pitfall] Rereading a record without the person who knows its context can miss background that only that person can provide. When: Reviewing documents without consulting people involved. Why: Some context exists only in people's memory.

## reviewing closed cases
- [procedure] 1) Reread the original record line by line. 2) Ask where the earlier process could have failed. 3) Check for missing evidence, uninvestigated circumstances, unwatched suspects, and oversights by the defense. When: A closed case still has an unresolved question and the original documents are available. Why: Systematic rereading can expose errors that the verdict hid.

## sample contamination
- [pitfall] Cross-contaminating samples or using unlabelled containers makes results untraceable to their source. When: Handling and storing multiple samples. Why: Mixed or unlabelled items cannot be reliably linked to an owner or origin.

## self-testing danger
- [fact] Testing a suspected poison on oneself can be dangerous and nearly fatal, so it should not be imitated. When: Considering testing a toxic substance on a person. Why: Human exposure to toxins can cause severe or lethal harm.

## sensational claim testing
- [heuristic] Treat a dramatic or exotic explanation as a hypothesis to test like any other, rather than dismissing or accepting it on its presentation. When: A death is framed as strange or unexplained by the public. Why: Presentation can make an unlikely cause seem more credible than the evidence supports.

## single-study conclusions
- [pitfall] A conclusion resting on a single study or experiment is weak evidence. Treat it as provisional until other trials confirm it. Why: One result can reflect chance or unusual conditions, so acting on it as settled can mislead.

## single-variable change logging
- [rule] Change only one variable at a time and record each change, since changing two at once hides which one mattered. When: Running a test to identify a cause. Why: Simultaneous changes make the result impossible to attribute.

## social setting for approach
- [heuristic] A relaxed social setting, such as a meal, can make a reluctant person more open to help than a formal meeting. When: Choosing where to approach a proud or guarded person. Why: Informal settings lower defensiveness, though they must not be used to press a point.

## statistical rule misapplied
- [pitfall] Applying a statistical generality to predict one individual, such as a single suspect or victim, confuses group tendency with individual outcome. When: Drawing a conclusion about one person from a population-level rule. Why: Group statistics describe a population well but say little about any one member.

## structured inquiry sequence
- [model] A structured inquiry establishes the victim's identity first, then addresses how, when and by what means death occurred, reporting only what is actually established. When: When investigating a death or similar case with several open questions. Why: Identity anchors every later inference; skipping it leaves conclusions without a foundation.

## testing an ambiguous sighting
- [procedure] 1) Attempt a physical probe that would be blocked if the object were solid. 2) Note whether it passes through with no resistance. 3) Try a rational or natural explanation before accepting the anomaly. When: A witness must check an ambiguous or impossible object. Why: A physical test separates real objects from illusions, and a rational check should be attempted before concluding the sighting is supernatural.

## trust versus verification
- [rule] Do not treat a trusted expert's word or reputation as proof that the process was clean; test the process itself. When: When a result depends on one operator's work. Why: An argument that someone would not do something is not a check that they did not.

## unclear source context
- [pitfall] Interpreting a scan or image without knowing its context can lead to a wrong conclusion, such as taking old remains for recent ones. When: Reading imaging or physical evidence. Why: An image has no meaning until the context that produced it is known.

## verify claim against records
- [procedure] 1) State the claimed fact. 2) Find a primary record such as a list, booking or log. 3) Check whether the record supports or contradicts the claim. When: When a witness or suspect asserts a fact that a record could confirm. Why: Primary records give checkable evidence independent of memory.

## verifying cited sources
- [rule] Check an authority's cited rule against the specific case it describes, since the source may contain an omitted fact that explains the apparent exception. When: A conclusion relies on a textbook rule, published case, or expert opinion. Why: A rule may appear overturned only because the cited case omits a relevant condition.

## weight-based identification
- [pitfall] Weight or gait narrows a suspect pool but does not single out one person; other evidence is required. Why: Many people share similar body weights and gaits.
