# player-psychology

## involuntary and emotional tells
- [mechanism] A dialogue system can store every NPC line verbatim; when a later statement contradicts it or shows hesitation, it can offer a contradiction action unlocking new questions. When: designing NPC dialogue the player can interrogate. Why: Stored statements let the game detect conflicts and reward the player for catching them.
- [mechanism] A dialogue system can track tells such as hesitation, blushing, word choice, repeated phrases or topic changes as flags, letting NPCs react or unlock targeted follow-up questions. When: NPC dialogue is generated from player-driven conversation.
- [mechanism] A corpse showing agitation suggests the victim foresaw death, while a sudden natural death usually leaves no such expression. When: Reading a body to infer how death occurred. Why: Expression reflects the victim's state at death, which differs by cause.
- [mechanism] An NPC's response changes with how much of the player's reconstruction is correct, so its dialogue can disclose its motive as the player's deductions improve. When: in a confrontation scene with a boss or NPC. Why: The NPC reacts to accurate points and revealing lines, giving the player feedback on their reasoning.
- [mechanism] Authored body-language cues such as hesitation or a tightened grip can point the player toward a line of questioning. When: Designing NPC reactions to sensitive questions. Why: Nonverbal signs of unease guide the player to the topics that matter.
- [mechanism] Mystery works by drawing thought away from a simple fact, not by hiding the fact itself.
- [mechanism] A long, consistent lie is hard to sustain, because each invented detail adds a new point where it can be contradicted. When: A witness or character gives a detailed story across several statements. Why: Every extra specific claim creates another checkable surface.
- [behavior] A hesitation before answering a precise detail or fetching something may reflect a rehearsed account or nerves; record it as a weak lead, not proof. When: a witness is asked a precise detail about a claimed event, or pauses when asked to fetch or check something. Why: Nerves and many innocent causes also produce hesitation, so it must be checked against other evidence.
- [behavior] Changed voice, shifting gaze or sudden starts under questioning can reflect stress as much as concealment, so they signal stress, not proof of a lie. When: a witness is questioned directly on a sensitive point or a suspect is under pressure. Why: Guilt and stress look alike from outside.
- [behavior] Visible discomfort, like lowered eyes, blushing, a changed manner, shifting posture or tone, or a repeated denial, often marks concealed feeling or a sensitive point worth pressing. When: a question touches a sensitive fact or subject and the witness's manner changes. Why: Concealed knowledge or feeling produces visible discomfort, which often marks evasion.
- [behavior] Under incriminating evidence or unwelcome news, the body may show pallor, sweat, trembling, fidgeting, flushing, a tight grip, collapse or fainting while speech stays controlled, signalling a near break. When: a suspect is shown incriminating evidence they fear, or a listener hears unwelcome news. Why: Physical reactions are harder to control than speech.
- [behavior] A name raised in questioning can trigger a visible flush, caught breath, stiffening or scream before composure returns, exposing a link a denial hides. When: a person is named in questioning, especially if the witness denies or avoids them. Why: Emotion leaks through the body when a person is surprised by a name, despite verbal control.
- [behavior] A suspect who fears being blamed may show pallor, hesitation or panic and look guilty even when the facts point elsewhere, since fear mimics concealment. When: a suspect is at risk of being blamed or shows visible panic. Why: Fear of suspicion produces the same physical strain as concealed knowledge, and anxiety is easy to read as guilt.
- [behavior] Haste leaves visible traces, such as half-finished clothing or tasks, snatched remains, emptied drawers, forced locks or overturned furniture, revealing urgency or a targeted search. When: a character is interrupted by an urgent call, invents a reason for being present, or a ransacked room is examined. Why: Attention shifts to the urgent event before routine finishing; an excuse made in a hurry is performed roughly; urgent searching is disorderly and targets a goal.
- [behavior] Haste under alarm makes careful actors forget precautions, leaving a single slip, such as a light left burning, that undoes the plan; check hiding steps before leaving. When: an actor must leave quickly after an unexpected interruption or while departing under pressure. Why: Haste reduces attention to small details, and urgency crowds out planned precautions.
- [behavior] An unplanned first reaction to a shown object, such as a stammer or pause before denying, reveals a connection to probe, more than a prepared answer. When: an object is shown unexpectedly and the suspect is questioned about it. Why: Immediate reactions come before a denial is constructed; hesitation signals knowledge the character would rather not share.
- [behavior] A loaded phrase or planted lure touching a hidden fact provokes a visible involuntary reaction in someone holding a related secret, while an uninformed listener stays unmoved. When: a suspect hears a phrase tied to a hidden fact, or a guarded suspect is exposed to a provocative item. Why: Knowledge of the secret gives the phrase weight, and fear that the hidden fact will come out drives the reaction.
- [behavior] A calm or blank face is ambiguous and may signal innocence or concealment; unexpected calm under a humiliating stimulus may hide feeling, so the reaction can count as evidence.
- [behavior] A character hiding a secret leaks it through brief reactions, such as a glance, held breath, paleness, stopped gesture or hand moving toward pocket, even when words stay calm. When: the character is asked about or reminded of the concealed matter, or cannot be asked directly. Why: Involuntary movements can leak what speech withholds or conceals.
- [behavior] Observers trust involuntary bodily signs, such as flushing, fidgeting or averted gaze, over a person's composed words when judging temper or guilt. When: A character's spoken words are guarded or calm while physical signs show agitation. Why: Uncontrolled signals are harder to fake than speech.
- [behavior] A sudden change in a person's colour or manner at a new arrival can reveal a prior connection the observer does not yet understand. When: A particular figure arrives within the person's sight or hearing. Why: Emotion shows before the person chooses what to say, exposing a hidden relationship.
- [behavior] A character corrected mid-meeting can show alarm and reveal knowledge they meant to hide, once their expectation proves false. When: a wrong identity assumption is corrected in front of them. Why: being corrected exposes what they already knew.
- [behavior] A pause, pallor and a sudden specific answer after an aborted sentence can signal the person already knows the detail and is deciding whether to reveal it. When: a question touches a detail the witness is hiding. Why: the hesitation shows an internal choice to disclose.
- [behavior] A suspect may slip by using a name only an insider would know, then ask how it was known; such slips reveal knowledge the suspect denies having. When: questioning a suspect who denies knowing a person or object.
- [behavior] A witness who hesitates before confirming a known fact may have heard or seen more than they admit. When: when a known fact is put to a witness. Why: Hesitation can signal a withheld detail.
- [behavior] A speaker who revises a hypothetical toward a concern may be signalling a worry they do not voice. When: a speaker reshapes their wording mid-sentence. Why: Word changes can reveal what the speaker is holding back.
- [behavior] Pressed about a known link, people often admit only the true minimum and reveal nothing further, protecting what they hold back. When: someone is asked about a connection to a person tied to a hidden event. Why: a short, partly true reply closes the topic.
- [behavior] A body placed in a doorway communicates refusal more clearly than spoken words do. When: A gatekeeper blocks a visitor from a room.
- [behavior] Restless listening and shifting tone signal that a character is hiding expectation, and observers read these tells before any cause is explained. When: when a character conceals a motive. Why: people read behavioral signs as evidence of inner state.
- [behavior] An emotionally involved witness may volunteer detailed alibi information unprompted while complaining or boasting. When: a witness is close to the suspect and speaks freely. Why: Emotion lowers the guard over what is revealed.
- [behavior] A suspect's first reaction, such as stunned disbelief or a low admission, reveals his state of mind and can serve as evidence. When: immediately after an arrest or accusation. Why: unguarded reactions expose inner state.
- [behavior] Warm appeals work on emotionally exposed subjects but fail against self-controlled ones, who keep their composure under them. When: when a subject is interrogated for information. Why: emotional openings differ between subjects.
- [behavior] Involuntary reactions such as recoil, hiding, sudden departure or colour change can carry meaning, and each has both an innocent reading and a significant one.
- [behavior] Experienced observers read tells by pattern recognition built from many past cases, noticing early signs that a person is deteriorating. When: the observer has long experience with similar people. Why: past cases provide a basis for comparison.
- [behavior] A gesture that comes before a quick verbal reply can signal concealment, even when the face and words show no change.
- [behavior] Hesitation before a specific answer that changes when a particular name or topic arises can signal a stake, though grief or fear of blame can also cause it. When: Reading a witness's pauses during questioning. Why: Hesitation is ambiguous; it tracks emotional stakes as much as guilt.
- [behavior] Suspects under pressure may shiver, glance back, or turn suddenly angry when followed, and observers may read these signs as guilt. When: when being followed or questioned. Why: stress produces physical signs.
- [behavior] Frequent pauses between words can signal that a witness is holding something back. When: when interviewing a witness whose answers seem guarded. Why: hesitation suggests the speaker is choosing what to reveal.
- [behavior] A suspect's involuntary reaction to a name can be caught by a second observer who is watching them. When: A name is raised during questioning.
- [behavior] What a person grabs under alarm depends on their life situation, so a lure should match the target's circumstances. Why: different attachments, such as family or valuables, drive different reactions.
- [behavior] Excited or agitated suspects reveal more than calm ones, so emotional state is a cue for when to press. When: A suspect is emotionally engaged in the conversation. Why: Heightened emotion lowers guard.
- [behavior] A sharply personal question can produce a visible reaction such as sobbing, turning away or hiding the face. Such a tell is a reaction, not proof of guilt. When: when a pointed question touches a named relationship. Why: emotional reactions arise from the question's personal weight.
- [behavior] Pallor, a strained voice or a sudden flash of anger can indicate that a challenge has landed, and the suspect may then leave. When: when reading a suspect during an interrogation. Why: involuntary physical responses betray stress under a successful challenge.
- [behavior] A listener can notice when a speaker's energy shifts sharply toward a harmless subject as a sensitive one comes near. When: when a sensitive topic is close.
- [behavior] A person's unguarded reaction to a place or inscription can expose interests they deny aloud, so a strong reaction that contradicts stated indifference is a clue. When: when someone reacts strongly to an object or place while claiming not to care. Why: involuntary responses are harder to control than spoken words.
- [behavior] A calm, idle-seeming investigator leads observers to assume no lead exists, though the investigator may be tracking the real one. When: an investigator appears uninterested. Why: low visible urgency is read as low threat.
- [behavior] People accept odd behaviour from an injured or shocked person as a result of the injury, so a substitute's oddities go unquestioned. When: A substitute acts strangely after an apparent attack. Why: Injury provides a ready explanation that suppresses suspicion.
- [behavior] A suspect who receives a fact that matters to them may show a brief flicker of discomposure before changing the subject. When: A relevant fact is delivered to a suspect. Why: The fact touches something the suspect cares about hiding.
- [behavior] A witness who turns cold and gives routine answers when pressed on a sensitive person may be unpracticed at deception and say no more. When: A witness is asked about a sensitive past connection. Why: Deception is uncomfortable for people who do not practise it.
- [behavior] Small reactions after a tense exchange, such as a flush or a private smile, reveal the emotional cost to the people involved. When: A tense exchange has just ended. Why: Brief involuntary responses leak feelings that were suppressed during the exchange.
- [behavior] A suspect may know more than they are willing to admit, and concealing knowledge does not by itself make them the culprit.
- [behavior] Brief involuntary signs, such as a slight drooping of the eyelids, can reveal a flicker of disturbance when the rest of the face shows nothing.
- [behavior] A listener's visible interest in an offhand remark sets it apart from other remarks, so an attentive audience notices it. When: a casual remark is later needed as evidence. Why: visible interest signals importance.
- [behavior] Paleness combined with visibly weighing options reads as calculation rather than plain denial. When: A accused person who pauses to weigh a way out. Why: Observers take visible deliberation as a sign of strategic thinking.
- [behavior] A nervous target reads ambiguous gestures as threats, so indirect probing can unsettle innocent people as much as guilty ones.
- [behavior] A brief pause or sharp reaction to a recognised name or place, cut off before control, signals a hidden connection, since recognition is hard to suppress. When: A name or place is spoken aloud, or a suspect hears a trigger they recognise. Why: Sudden reactions read as concealment, and recognition causes a split-second change that is hard to suppress.
- [behavior] Under pressure, involuntary reactions such as a sudden start, fixed stare, eye or colour change, posture shift or dropped object can leak knowledge words hide. When: A suspect or witness is confronted with a difficult question, or a subject they have a stake in and try to conceal. Why: Involuntary reactions bypass deliberate control, so they leak what speech would suppress.
- [behavior] Pressing on a subject a guarded witness cares about can shift their expression between emotions; the flicker marks where pressure lies and prompts questions, not proof. When: A guarded witness is questioned face to face about a sensitive topic after a calm opening. Why: Pressure on a personal concern exposes emotions kept controlled, and a single flicker has many possible causes.
- [behavior] Words spoken in a delirious or disturbed state repeat a dominant preoccupation, so they reveal what a character fears more than what actually happened. When: A character speaks while feverish, drugged or otherwise impaired and the listener wonders whether the words are true. Why: Altered mental states bring the dominant preoccupation to the surface, so the content reflects inner fear rather than events.
- [behavior] Retelling can re-enact the experience, with voice and manner shifting. These changes are evidence of the witness's emotional state, separate from the events. When: A witness retells a distressing or embarrassing event. Why: Recalling an experience re-activates the emotions attached to it.
- [rule] Design brief reactions that read two ways and need pairing with another clue; a single reaction can mix anger, fear and uncertainty. When: Writing NPC reactions as clues or interpreting a single visible reaction. Why: A tell with one reading gives an answer instead of a deduction.
- [rule] Give NPC dialogue readable tells, such as changed stance, new option, pause or changed wording, that players learn to notice on key subjects or in false or evasive answers. When: Designing NPC dialogue that may conceal information. Why: A noticeable tell rewards attention and makes deception discoverable.
- [rule] Flag dialogue remarks that reveal knowledge the speaker should lack as designed tells, so players can notice the slip as a sign of concealment. When: Designing NPC dialogue with hidden knowledge. Why: Unexpected knowledge is a detectable signal of concealment.
- [rule] Flag hesitation before confirming a fact, or a hesitant or wrong identification, as a visible cue that an NPC is unsure or hiding something, prompting follow-up. When: Designing NPC responses to questions or witness scenes. Why: Hesitation gives players a visible cue to follow up.
- [rule] Let minor tells, such as fidgeting near an object, raise suspicion without settling it; combine them with physical facts, not nerves or a pale face. Why: Innocent people also become unsettled.
- [rule] Let an NPC who believes they are speaking to someone else give away information in dialogue that the player can use. When: an NPC is mistaken about who they are talking to.
- [rule] Plant a casual dialogue line that becomes a key only when combined with another clue. When: Designing dialogue that feeds later deduction. Why: Delayed significance rewards players who remember and connect details.
- [rule] Give dialogue a hidden reaction state, shown through text or animation, that the player must notice when a specific fact or item is mentioned. Why: lets players test an NPC's knowledge through dialogue options.
- [rule] Make hesitant non-player characters share only a fragment unless pressed, so persistence in questioning is rewarded. When: Designing reluctant NPCs. Why: Gating fuller answers behind pressing rewards inquiry.
- [rule] Separate evasive behavior and strong motive from proof of presence and action before judging guilt. When: deciding whether a suspect is guilty. Why: Suspicion and motive do not establish that someone was at the scene or acted.
- [heuristic] A pause, delayed or strained answer, or bitten-back half-finished reply often marks a sensitive point worth a focused follow-up, though not proof of guilt. When: a witness pauses, answers late, or starts and stops on a direct or easy question. Why: Hesitation marks where the account is weakest and can reveal what the witness meant to hide, but it has innocent causes too.
- [heuristic] A character who breaks off mid-name or nearly says a secret tells the listener more than a flat denial, so a cut-off can point toward hidden knowledge. When: A character has reason to hide knowledge but must still speak to others. Why: The listener completes the unfinished thought, so the implied secret feels more concrete than a denial.
- [heuristic] A hesitant or over-precise denial can be written as a dialogue signal that an NPC is hiding a link, without proving that the link exists. When: Writing NPC responses to a shown item. Why: Signalling concealment gives the player a lead while keeping the truth open.
- [heuristic] Dialogue can carry two meanings at once, the stated words and a hidden intent; judge a line by its tone and by what it leaves unsaid. When: reading a line that may hide a purpose.
- [heuristic] Shifts in routine across days, such as cancelled orders, unusual smoking, or burnt letters, mark a change in a person's state. When: when reading a timeline of a suspect.
- [heuristic] Use changed behaviour, such as drinking, restlessness or avoided eye contact, to build suspicion without a direct accusation. When: when an audience must suspect a character without being told outright.
- [heuristic] When a person acts against expectation, treat the reversal as a clue and make explaining it the focus of the investigation. When: a character breaks an established pattern. Why: the break often reveals hidden intent.
- [heuristic] A fair clue is usually one small slip within an otherwise careful plan, not a large or conspicuous mistake.
- [heuristic] Use mild teasing to test whether a witness shows irritation, since a sharp reaction can expose what they are protecting. When: a witness seems guarded but not openly hostile. Why: a small provocation can reveal a stronger reaction than direct questioning.
- [heuristic] A suspect's glances toward exits or windows, followed by a decision, can expose a plan of action. When: a suspect is under pressure or about to act. Why: Attention to escape routes often precedes movement, revealing intent before action.
- [heuristic] Show a character hesitating before a hard question, weighing reluctance against the need for truth, so the cost of asking is visible. Why: visible cost makes the question weigh on the audience.
- [heuristic] Treat a sudden departure as a lead, since haste suggests fear and fear suggests the matter is significant.
- [heuristic] A body arranged neatly rather than sprawled is unlike someone stabbed while standing or struggling, and may suggest the body was placed after death.
- [heuristic] A sudden recognition can redirect the whole line of questioning mid-conversation, so stay open to changing focus partway through. When: during an extended interview.
- [heuristic] A suspect who insists on a formal inquiry when offered a safe settlement is anomalous and must be explained, since guilt would normally favour the settlement. When: when a suspect's choice seems to work against their own interest. Why: Unexplained choices can reveal the true motive.
- [heuristic] Treat hesitant identifications as leads to pursue, not as proof of identity. When: a witness hesitates on identification. Why: hesitation means the match is uncertain.
- [heuristic] Deliver a final reveal as a monologue the player triggers, limiting the suspect's options to confess or refuse. When: designing a dialogue-based confrontation. Why: a limited option set focuses the scene on the reveal.
- [heuristic] Check whether a suspect's behaviour fits the crime window, since conspicuously open or noisy conduct right after the act argues against guilt. When: judging a suspect's behaviour near a crime time. Why: a recent killer would likely avoid drawing attention.
- [heuristic] A character's suspicion or cruelty makes sense once the reader knows what that character feared. When: Judging a character whose hostility seems extreme. Why: Fear of a specific threat shapes behavior that would otherwise be unexplained.
- [heuristic] In hidden-information games, opponents' handling of played cards, hesitation and facial expression reveal what they hold. When: reading opponents' concealed holdings. Why: habits of handling leak information.
- [heuristic] A desk or other barrier shields an examiner and lets him hide surprise by bending over papers, though giving up the barrier can read as weakness to a dangerous person. When: Choosing whether to stay behind a barrier during questioning. Why: Protection visibly used can signal either caution or vulnerability.
- [heuristic] Do not expect a disguised person to look awkward; an effective disguise works by avoiding awkward behaviour. When: when assessing whether someone is disguised. Why: awkwardness is the wrong signal for a well-built disguise.
- [heuristic] Infer from the reaction a person would normally give; a missing response can be as informative as an observed one. When: A silent scene is interpreted.
- [heuristic] A suspect can stage a result, so watch what the suspect does rather than what the suspect says. When: When a suspect's account conflicts with observed evidence. Why: Statements are easier to fabricate than sustained actions.
- [heuristic] A listener who avoids a subject or does not ask who benefits may be concealing knowledge of it. When: Questioning a suspect about a scenario.
- [heuristic] Physical traces such as warmth, arrangement or disorder can contradict a witness's account of recent activity. When: A witness describes an action that leaves a checkable physical state. Why: Recent activity leaves traces that cannot be reconciled with a claim of inactivity.
- [procedure] 1) Ask about a specific detail of a threat or act. 2) Watch for colour changes, twitches, stiffness, growing hesitation or shifting details. 3) Press with follow-up questions until the answer changes or firms up. When: A witness hesitates or changes answers under specific questioning. Why: Repeated specific questions expose shifting details and signal the need for a full account.
- [procedure] 1) Record what each NPC has told the player. 2) Compare new statements against that record. 3) Surface contradictions as clues. When: Running a mystery with many NPC conversations. Why: Logged statements let later contradictions become discoverable evidence.
- [procedure] 1) List the traits the crime required. 2) Test a suspect's behavior in a controlled situation against those traits. 3) Record how far the behavior matches. When: when physical facts seem to clear the suspect.
- [procedure] 1) Identify a rare, recognizable method the suspect has used. 2) Build a lure that imitates a credible threat. 3) Fix the time and place yourself. 4) Station a watcher with orders to intervene at once when the suspect acts. When: The investigator wants proof from the suspect's own act. Why: The suspect's action supplies proof that reasoning alone cannot.
- [procedure] 1) Give the NPC two motives that conflict with an item the player found. 2) Let the player detect the lie by comparing answers. 3) Ask a follow-up that forces one reason. 4) Record which reason the NPC avoids.
- [procedure] 1) List each sudden change in a person's behaviour or attitude with its date and place. 2) Attribute known changes to known causes. 3) Mark unexplained changes as the target. 4) Work backward from who could have triggered them. When: Motive is unclear and behaviour changes are recorded with dates. Why: Anchoring on dated changes gives deduction a fixed target.
- [pitfall] Faintness or strong emotion, including terror, can come from heat, fatigue, illness, grief or a different wrongdoing, and bodily arousal cannot tell fear from guilt. When: Interpreting physical or emotional reactions during questioning. Why: Many causes and emotions produce the same response, so reactions show feeling, not guilt. (x2)

- [pitfall] A slip of the tongue or unintended admission may be harmless; treat it as a lead needing corroboration, not proof of wrongdoing.
- [pitfall] Accurate tells invite plausible but wrong explanations: observers take concealment for surprise, dismiss threats, or explain clues away, so they go unused. When: a clue is easy to interpret incorrectly. Why: Observers tend to accept a plausible explanation for a reaction.
- [pitfall] Hesitating too long before acting can expose innocent people to harm, so reluctance carries its own cost.
- [pitfall] Reading mood or motive from behaviour, such as cheerfulness, new habits, smiles, a startle or a locked door, is a guess until checked, though witnesses present it as fact. When: A witness or investigator interprets mood, motive or an odd action without direct proof. Why: Behaviour is ambiguous and invites confident reading.
- [pitfall] Judging a speaker's honesty by appearance, manner or charm is unreliable and reflects the judge's generalizations, since a pleasant manner says nothing about truth.
- [pitfall] Reading silence or omissions as concealment relies on projected motives or assumed normal conduct, which may be wrong, and observers cannot tell deliberate from unconscious silence. When: Interpreting an ambiguous silence or omission from a suspect. Why: Projection supplies a sinister explanation for silence that may have no hidden purpose.
- [pitfall] Hesitation, glancing or eye movement can come from fear, loyalty or nervousness, so record it as a reason for further checking, not as proof of guilt or identification. When: When a witness pauses or glances during questioning. Why: Nervousness has many innocent causes, so such signs only justify further checking.
- [pitfall] Hesitating at the first touch can delay the check that matters most, so the most decisive test should not wait on reluctance. When: When an observer is reluctant to touch a person. Why: Reluctance postpones the decisive evidence.
- [pitfall] Excessive or insincere praise is easy to detect and undermines the approach.
- [pitfall] A witness caught in a minor lie may still conceal the central fact, so one exposed contradiction does not resolve the case.
- [pitfall] A lie that rests on one claim can be undone by a single document or source from outside the conversation. When: A speaker relies on an unverified falsehood. Why: An external record can contradict the one claim the lie depends on.
- [pitfall] A charitable excuse offered for a visible sign can conceal the truth rather than explain it, and the excuse itself may signal protection.
- [pitfall] Dismissing exits too quickly makes an impossibility feel like a cheat; listing each check carefully keeps it credible.
- [pitfall] Dramatic phrases are often assumed metaphorical, but literal statements can also be taken as figures of speech, so check each.
- [pitfall] Pressure to dispose of a body quickly can destroy evidence before the cause of death is settled.
- [pitfall] A hasty or confident first verdict, even from a local examiner, can be wrong; require careful examination before naming a cause.
- [pitfall] A first reading of silent gestures can be wrong when the missing sound would have explained the behavior. When: interpreting a scene without audio. Why: missing context leads to a wrong reading.
- [pitfall] A stranger's gaze can cause a guilty-looking action that has an innocent cause.
- [pitfall] Reading meaning into a single gesture can confirm a suspicion the observer already held.
- [pitfall] Readers and officials often hear a qualified denial as a full denial, or as a confession. When: A narrow statement is summarized or reported later. Why: People tend to simplify statements into the stronger version they expect.
- [pitfall] Strong anger can make a concealed real name slip out during a denial, exposing the identity.
- [pitfall] An invented excuse that is too neat or eager makes the target wary.
- [pitfall] Different stated reasons for skipping the same event can signal a concealed motive rather than a neutral choice.
- [fact] Involuntary signs, such as restless eyes, a brief flicker of recognition or guilt, or shifting gaze, brow or mouth, betray inner state more reliably than a composed face or words. When: words are guarded or composed. Why: Speech and looks are easier to control than involuntary reflexes.
- [fact] A protective secret can be exposed by a single careless disclosure from an intoxicated insider, so one slip can grant access that layered rules were meant to prevent. When: A secret protects a secured asset. Why: People under the influence reveal what they should keep quiet.

- [fact] A witness can speak only literal truths yet still create a false impression through what they leave out. When: When evaluating testimony or NPC dialogue that is accurate but incomplete. Why: Omission can mislead as effectively as falsehood.

## observer bias and presumption
- [definition] An investigator's assumption, or a sign pointing toward a suspect, is a presumption that justifies closer examination but not guilt; a deduction is a conclusion drawn from evidence. (x2)
- [mechanism] A mind primed for the strange sees ordinary details as strange and looks less for mundane causes, so an exotic explanation diverts attention from mundane suspects. When: A dramatic or exotic story is presented before facts are checked, or an observer strongly expects a supernatural event. Why: Expectation filters what is noticed and how it is interpreted.
- [mechanism] Suspicion from class, background, looks or reputation, not evidence, repeated through prejudiced framing passes for proof; it makes the investigator look foolish and must be handled carefully. When: a group forms a verdict about a suspect. Why: Prejudice carried by framing and noise is mistaken for proof.
- [mechanism] Model gaps in a witness's observation explicitly, so the player must work out what the witness missed. When: A witness was absent from part of an event. Why: Unobserved moments leave the player to reconstruct the missing information.
- [behavior] An observer drawn to someone by pity, anger, admiration or affection colours perception and report, may read a deliberate test as cruelty, so the account is partial. When: An observer is emotionally involved with, or drawn to, the person under questioning. Why: Emotional involvement shapes perception and report. (x2)
- [behavior] Outside pressure, from an outsider urging an investigator or from passion, fear and gossip in a stressed community, speeds decisions and accusations before evidence is weighed. When: an outsider presses an investigator to act, or fear or gossip circulates in a community under stress. Why: Pressure from others changes the pace of decisions; emotion and talk can outrun judgement. (x2)
- [behavior] A nickname or recent attention can lead observers to attach a familiar name to a stranger or shape what a witness expects to see, distorting identification. When: recent context fills the observer's attention, or a nickname is given to someone. Why: Priming makes familiar names and faces surface before observation, shaping perception. (x2)
- [behavior] Witnesses primed with a cause or legend before an event tend to report sightings matching it, so prior belief shapes what they perceive. When: people are primed with a preconceived explanation or told a story before an event. Why: Expectation shapes perception. (x2)
- [behavior] An investigator who states a suspicion as certainty reads each later detail as confirmation, ignoring details pointing elsewhere and keeping that suspect until a physical trace contradicts. When: a player or investigator commits to a suspect before proof arrives, or the obvious story points at one suspect. Why: A favoured theory filters new evidence toward the held conclusion. (x2)
- [behavior] An emotionally attached observer reads a suspect's behaviour in the most favourable light, so innocent acts look guilty or guilty acts innocent, favouring readings that match existing conclusions. When: The observer has affection or dislike for the suspect, or is emotionally invested. Why: Emotional attachment and confirmation bias shape which signs are noticed and how they are weighed.
- [behavior] Prejudice against a witness or claimant, or an advance warning, offers a ready explanation, so sound claims are dismissed as known delusion before being examined. When: A listener is warned about a claimant in advance or holds strong assumptions about them. Why: The warning or prejudice replaces scrutiny of the claim with a ready explanation.
- [behavior] An eerie mood or haunting setting, such as moaning wind, ghostly cues or a ghostly presence, primes players and investigators toward a supernatural explanation before evidence is weighed. When: a mystery or investigation uses eerie atmosphere. Why: Mood and atmosphere shape expectations and interpretation of ambiguous events.
- [behavior] Nerves (a startle, dropped item, shaking hand) and ordinary acts (yawning, unrelated lesson before an inquest) are read by observers as guilt or indifference, feeding suspicion without showing guilt. When: a character is addressed or questioned, or behaves ordinarily around an inquest. Why: Observers read involuntary tension or indifference as guilt.
- [behavior] A witness's prejudices and deference to rank shape how they describe people, so their descriptions reveal their attitudes. Why: Social attitudes colour observation.
- [behavior] A sudden bright light can blind an observer at the critical moment, leaving them unable to say what was happening. When: An event happens during a sudden disruption. Why: Vision and attention can fail exactly when they are most needed.

## attention and observation
- [definition] Observation means recording specific, countable facts that can be checked later, which is different from merely having seen something. When: Clues must be gathered and used in an investigation. Why: Only recorded details can support inference.
- [mechanism] Once a reliable observer covers a place from a given time, movement or action there after that time is ruled out, so the possible window narrows. - [mechanism] When a fortune-teller's checkable prediction fails under player testing, the town's regard for the legend should drop. When: Designing a prophecy NPC. Why: Predictions only shape belief if outcomes can contradict them.
- [mechanism] A constant companion becomes background to an observer, so its loss goes unnoticed until the loss is very close. When: when an observer is occupied with other matters. Why: habituated elements stop drawing attention.
- [mechanism] A shared belief in an explanation primes people to read ordinary events as confirming it, so the belief helps produce the reported phenomena. When: when a community strongly expects a certain kind of cause. Why: observers interpret events through the expected explanation, so each event seems to confirm it.
- [mechanism] Enemies who watch a meeting learn who the witness is, so a witness's visible contact with investigators can make them a target.

When: a witness must identify someone or judge a claim; observers already believe a suspect is guilty; witnesses in a haunted or eerie setting. Why: prior belief sets the baseline against which features are called unusual; expectation shapes perception and interpretation.

When: A witness gives two or more accounts touching the same facts, offers several explanations touching shared facts, or revises an earlier statement. - [pitfall] An impression of magical vanishing is not an explanation; the answer must be found in the door, the key or the route. When: a disappearance seems dramatic. Why: drama hides mundane mechanics.
- [pitfall] A rehearsed impression is still only an impression, not proof that the person was actually present.
- [pitfall] Trusting a witness who recalls only a vague impression can place an event at the wrong time. Why: impressions lack precise detail.
- [pitfall] Assuming a witness saw someone just because that person was present in the room leads to wrong conclusions about who was there.
- [pitfall] An observer whose attention is elsewhere, such as under a drug or stress, may miss signals from others in the room. When: Interrogating while the observer is impaired or distracted. Why: Limited attention filters out secondary cues.

## staged deception
- [model] A deception can be built so that each likely challenge has a prepared answer, steering audience attention toward effects rather than facts. When: When a character's apparent power depends on hiding how an effect is produced. Why: Prepared responses deflect scrutiny before it reaches the mechanism.
- [mechanism] A loud, absorbing spectacle, including ceremony, silence or dance around a trick, draws attention away from the quiet act or hidden channel producing the effect. When: A festival, performance or crowd event is underway, or a performance includes a spectacle around its trick. Why: Attention is consumed by the expected display and moves away from the mechanism. (x2)
- [mechanism] A staged setback that convinces opponents the investigation has failed can lure them into relaxing or revealing their hand.
- [mechanism] Skilled offenders are rarely caught and their failures are seldom seen, so the visible cases are mostly clumsy ones. Why: Detection shows the offenders who failed, not those who succeeded.
- [mechanism] A performer can seem to know private facts through a hidden intermediary who relays information from the questioner to the performer, so apparent insight may be a human channel. When: A supposed supernatural or omniscient source reveals private information. Why: A hidden relay can pass overheard words to the performer, producing apparent knowledge.
- [behavior] A deceptive statement is designed to sound small and reasonable, and a deceiver's apparent sympathy adds to its credibility.
- [behavior] People tell small lies to serve a larger purpose, such as hiding a deficiency or steering a visitor's choices. When: A character gives an account that seems to protect something or direct the listener. Why: A minor falsehood can conceal a bigger concern.
- [behavior] A suspect who drops the performance during a crisis reveals how much of the earlier manner was staged. When: when a suspect faces sudden pressure. Why: Under stress the performance gives way to natural composure.
- [behavior] Under accusation, suspects often answer with questions and counter-demands instead of volunteering information. When: when accused or pressed with claims. Why: deflection avoids giving away useful facts.
- [behavior] A target's reaction to a veiled accusation escalates in stages: calm, then agitation, then an attempt to rise, then near-faintness. When: when a suspect hears a hinted accusation.
- [behavior] A suspect who shows no alarm at a staged spectacle may already know it is staged, so the lack of reaction is itself informative. When: when a staged event is shown to a suspect. Why: a person who knows the staging does not react as a believer would.
- [behavior] Witnesses often reveal information in stages, first denying or minimising, then admitting more under pressure or after a direct question. When: A witness has a reason to withhold a detail. Why: Witnesses protect themselves or others and yield only as far as pressed.
- [behavior] A suspect who believes a message comes from a trusted source will act on it and attend the meeting, even when the message is a trap. When: When a message appears to come from someone the target trusts. Why: Trust in a familiar source overrides caution about the message's origin.
- [behavior] Only a rare observer with specific trick-spotting knowledge notices an inconsistency in a skilled performance, while most of the crowd notices nothing. When: A performance is watched by a mixed audience. Why: Detecting a trick requires knowing how the trick is done.
- [behavior] Manipulative lures are fitted to a target's current condition, so a character's present need reveals which temptation they are most likely to fall for. When: a character may be manipulated. Why: Manipulators exploit the specific circumstances of their target.
- [behavior] In a staged meeting, the witness may be shaken while the suspect remains calm, so their reactions can diverge. When: a witness and a suspect meet unexpectedly. Why: people differ in how well they control involuntary responses.
- [behavior] A strange, apparently pointless act can draw attention to its absurdity while the real purpose is achieved in the background. When: when an odd act seems irrational and its effects might serve someone. Why: Observers label the act as madness or quirk and stop looking for a purpose.
- [behavior] A target who expects a valuable exchange will act on a fake prize when the deal's cues, such as a code and a price, seem credible. When: a staged exchange trap. Why: Expected gain can outweigh suspicion of the package.
- [rule] Escalate a scare in stages, from an ambiguous noise to a cry to a visible figure, so tension builds before the visible event. When: when a sighting is the turning point of a scene.
- [heuristic] Study fabricated accounts to see how false reports persuade readers, but do not treat their claims as evidence of truth.
- [heuristic] A plausible account is only as strong as the checks it passes, and it cannot replace physical evidence.
- [heuristic] Design NPC dialogue in stages: a direct challenge, then a pause, then a reassurance option that unlocks a hidden clue. When: An NPC is meant to reveal a clue only after pressure. Why: Staged pressure with a face-saving option makes a reluctant NPC more likely to disclose.
- [heuristic] Plant a suspect before any crime by establishing a standing grudge, a visible watcher and rumors that supply a motive. When: the audience must hold a suspect with motive and opportunity before the incident. Why: the reader gets a reason to suspect early.
- [heuristic] Treat a visible quarrel between allies as possibly staged when their accusations follow a coordinated sequence. When: Allies appear to fall out. Why: Staged disputes can hide joint action.
- [heuristic] Before accepting that a source knows a private fact, check who else could have heard it, including intermediaries and prior arrangements. When: A source repeats a private statement exactly. Why: A leak or prior arrangement can explain apparent knowledge without any supernatural source.
- [heuristic] A missing reaction in a staged test is also information and may mean the setup failed. When: a planned reaction test produces no visible response. Why: absence of response can reflect a failed setup rather than innocence.
- [procedure] 1) Present the suspect as helpful and reasonable. 2) Frame requests as sympathy or duty. 3) Let the skeptic appear obstinate. 4) Keep the suspect's real motive separate from apparent motive. When: Designing a suspect who should be revealed only later. Why: Apparent helpfulness hides motive, and a discredited expert reinforces the misdirection.
- [procedure] 1) Rebuild the sequence of actions behind a convenient clue. 2) Check each action against the time available. 3) Check each action against the risk of being observed. When: When a convenient escape route or clue appears too easily. Why: Timing and observation constraints reveal whether a staged trace is physically possible.
- [procedure] 1) Arrange an unannounced meeting between a witness and a person she may fear or love. 2) Place a distant observer to record the first emotional reaction. 3) Use a signal and separate rooms or vehicles to avoid a public scene. When: identity is the open question and a reaction can narrow it. Why: an unwarned meeting exposes involuntary reactions that the parties cannot prepare for.
- [tradeoff] Supplying proof to authorities who want it before acting secures their cooperation, but gives up control over when the evidence is revealed. When: when authorities demand proof before acting.
- [pitfall] Treating a witness's theatrical distress, such as a faint, as proof of innocence or guilt is a mistake, since staging or questioners may arrange such reactions. When: An interview or confrontation scene where a reaction is treated as decisive evidence. Why: Staging and the setting of the scene can cause distress or fits that observers misread as confirmation. (x2)
- [pitfall] A sudden, convenient injury invites doubt, since a genuinely suffering person is watched and their health is asked about openly. When: an injury appears just when someone is needed elsewhere.
- [pitfall] A staged illness draws attention to the person faking it, who may then be watched more closely. Why: Sudden illness is noticed, and the one who feigns it becomes the focus.
- [pitfall] A questioner who stops at the general principle may miss decisive facts such as names or hiding places; probing further reveals what the abstract answer hides.
- [pitfall] A staged rebuke can mislead the very person it protects, since that person has no warning and may react as if the public scene were real. Why: the cover story is not shared with its own beneficiary.
- [pitfall] A suspect who recognises a manipulation tactic may call it out, which can end cooperation.
- [pitfall] Reading a deliberately ordered sequence of effects as random chance, or as proof of the supernatural, leads to wrong conclusions.
- [fact] Skilled performers can control facial expression, voice, breathing and colour, and produce fits or rigidity on cue, deceiving trained, skeptical observers such as judges, clergy and jurors. When: A witness performs a condition that cannot be checked directly. Why: Performers with fine control over their bodily signals can imitate symptoms convincingly.

## accuser motives and credibility
- [model] A community's suspicion of a person escalates through five stages: suspicion, spying, watching and searching, formal complaint, then formal charge.
- [mechanism] Accusations reverse when accusers facing defamation suits retract out of fear, or when reconsidered, former accusers sign statements for the accused and admit the wrong. When: When the accused sues the accusers or the case is later reconsidered. Why: Fear of consequences can outweigh the accusation.
- [mechanism] A victim's earlier accusations against others can make a later claim of poisoning look like an old fear, so it is dismissed. When: When a victim has repeatedly accused people of plotting against them. Why: Earlier accusations lower the credibility of later ones.
- [mechanism] Accusers can act as a collection point for local gossip, turning rumors such as irregular church attendance into formal charges before an authority.
- [mechanism] Letting accusers and onlookers speak against the accused during a hearing raises pressure and skews what the court hears.
- [mechanism] In some ordeal laws the accuser's gain or loss reverses with the outcome, so a false accuser risks losing life or property.
- [mechanism] In a cascade, being named is read as evidence of opposition, so each new accusation confirms the ones before it. When: A group where accusations trigger further accusations. Why: Accusers treat the naming itself as proof.
- [behavior] People who think accusing others first protects them, or find being an accuser safer than being accused, join accusations and name others, adding to the accused. Why: Participation offers protection from the accusation itself.
- [behavior] Authorities and examiners treat accusers' fits, outcries or visible reactions as proof, setting aside denials, since nothing independent stands between accusation and verdict. When: When the only evidence is accusers' distress or outcry, or a suspect denies charges made by distressed accusers. Why: Distress and dramatic reactions appear to confirm the charge.
- [behavior] Accusers name the most available, least protected people first, then those they barely know or who live far away, widening suspicion beyond grudges. Why: Easy targets are cheapest to name and hardest to defend.
- [behavior] A bitter witness can use a general hostility shared by the whole group to target one specific rival, often gaining from the accusation. When: when a witness accuses a rival with a motive everyone shares. Why: A common grievance gives cover for a personal attack on one person.
- [behavior] Authority figures may accept a named culprit immediately when the accusation touches their own emotional history, skipping verification. When: news of an accusation connects to a personal past grievance. Why: emotional stake shapes how the accusation is received.
- [behavior] Accumulated unresolved wrongs can leave people feeling the whole world is against them, which makes them easier to inflame by later provocation. When: when a community has many unresolved disputes. Why: cumulative grievance lowers trust and raises readiness to react.
- [behavior] Motives for joining a group accusation often start small, such as craving notoriety, mischief, or revenge on a few people who offended the accuser. When: early stage of a collective accusation.
- [behavior] Accusers are treated as oracles, so when a victim shows pain they name whoever they choose, often a new target or someone already jailed. When: accusers' word is accepted without checks.
- [behavior] Speakers join true facts such as debt, quarrels, and wealth into an accusation by supplying the connecting assumptions themselves. When: when a public theory forms from loosely related facts. Why: the links feel plausible to the speaker.
- [behavior] In a small group, each person who accuses another often serves their own interest, so mutual accusations carry little weight by themselves. When: Several people share a place and each has a reason to blame someone else. Why: Accusers protect themselves or redirect suspicion.
- [behavior] An accuser with a visible personal motive makes the audience doubt the accusation before any contrary evidence appears. When: a witness or accuser makes a claim against someone. Why: A personal stake suggests the claim may be self-serving.
- [rule] Tag each NPC accusation with the accuser's faction or property motive so the player must weigh who gains before acting on it. When: Designing NPC accusations in a game. Why: Motives tied to stakes make accusations strategic rather than random. (x2)
- [rule] Use an NPC accusation to create doubt, not as a finished answer; show the accuser's grudge as a visible flag, with credibility resting on support the player can find. When: When an NPC accuses a trusted or powerful figure. Why: Its author may be wrong or may want a particular reaction. (x2)
- [rule] Do not dismiss an accuser entirely. A grudge explains why a claim was made but does not prove it false.
- [heuristic] Treat an accuser's claims as unverified until their stake, grudge, family conflict, mental state and the source and timing are checked. When: When an accuser gains from the outcome or has a grudge, personal stake, or unstable state. Why: An angry or interested speaker may overstate, mislead, or pursue their own goal. (x2)
- [heuristic] Look beyond the accusation to material interests and the network of quarrels, property disputes and alliances around the suspect, where motive usually lies. When: When assessing who would gain from or be motivated to make an accusation. Why: Existing grievances give accusers a reason to act and shape who they blame.
- [heuristic] A witness who volunteers an accusation may be steering attention away from herself, so separate what she observed from what she inferred. When: A witness names another person as the culprit. Why: Accusations can serve to deflect suspicion from the speaker.
- [heuristic] Framing an accusation as a painful duty and citing the suspect's social standing and habits adds reputational pressure to the factual charge.
- [heuristic] Before accepting a verdict or death, list everyone who gains from the accused's ruin, including officials who collect fines or seize goods. When: When a suspect's death or ruin benefits identifiable people. Why: Gain gives people a reason to accuse, which shapes which claims are pushed and which are ignored.
- [procedure] 1) Examine the accusers strictly. 2) Keep the accusers apart for a time before hearing their statements. Why: Separation reduces coordination between accusers.
- [pitfall] A growing number of accusers is not a measure of truth; it may reflect spreading fear, and each new accuser adds to a list that protects the accused. (x2)
- [pitfall] Accusers gaining financially or through revenge, grudge or property may give sincere-seeming false testimony, making a suspect look more convenient than evidence supports. When: When a witness has a personal stake in the outcome. Why: Revenge or self-interest gives a reason to lie.
- [pitfall] Motives offered by authorities or by a client's guesses may not match the facts, and accepting them uncritically misleads the inquiry. When: When others suggest why an event happened. Why: Confident suggestions from others are easily taken as established.
- [fact] Accusations are fed by blaming illness on hidden causes, seeking attention, sympathy or notoriety, mischief, revenge, self-protection, grudges, feuds, local disputes, theological bitterness or professional jealousy.
- [fact] A witness who is also the accuser gives testimony that is strong evidence yet hard to dispute on the spot.

## suspect statement analysis
- [behavior] A suspect's tidy excuse, such as a sudden claim to have discarded a ticket, should be treated as a claim to test against physical details, not accepted as fact. When: suspect offers an excuse checkable against physical details.
- [behavior] A suspect who sees the investigator already knows the details is more likely to give a full, honest account. When: during a confrontation with verified evidence. Why: lying gains nothing when gaps will be exposed.
- [behavior] A suspect under pressure often shifts to charm or frankness and presents their actions as provoked, placing blame on the victim. When: A cornered suspect is questioned about their motive. Why: Self-justification protects the suspect's standing and shapes their account.
- [behavior] A listener who cannot check a stated fact at the moment tends to accept it as true. When: when verification is unavailable during the exchange. Why: unchecked claims feel settled.
- [behavior] Culprits often assume that small concealment steps are safe, but a discarded item or replacement object can point to a particular person or habit that the culprit did not anticipate. When: a suspect tries to erase or replace evidence. Why: The culprit judges risk from their own view and misses knowledge other people hold about common objects.
- [behavior] A teller filters what an audience learns by deciding which details count as unnecessary.
- [behavior] A suspect may accept only part of a theory, so the remaining gaps must be followed up rather than assumed resolved. When: Questioning yields partial agreement. Why: People often accept the parts that fit their experience and resist the rest.
- [behavior] Silence about one's movements protects against self-incrimination but tends to make a case look worse to observers. When: A suspect declines to explain their movements. Why: Observers read unexplained silence as concealment.
- [behavior] A person who never complained of pain leaves vague or inconsistent testimony about it, and that absence of complaint is weak evidence. When: Witnesses describe the health of someone who did not report symptoms. Why: Witnesses who did not watch the person's health closely cannot give complete accounts.
- [behavior] Players read an innocent suspect's self-incriminating remark as guilt until a later clue gives it another meaning. When: a wrongly accused character says something that fits the accusation. Why: the remark matches the accusation on first reading.
- [rule] A suspect's own testimony is weak when they are implicated; whether they are watched matters more than whether they are detained. When: When a suspect gives an account of their movements. Why: An implicated person has a motive to shape their account.
- [rule] Test each element of a gossip-driven theory against physical facts, and do not dismiss a stock type that has direct support. When: When a story seems to account for everything. Why: Stock roles are false only when used in place of evidence.
- [rule] Treat a fact reported by one interested party as unproven until someone who did not hear it from that party confirms it. When: A key fact rests on one person's word. Why: Shared origin makes apparent corroboration unreliable.
- [rule] Treat a generous or insistent alibi as a reason to probe further, not as a settled answer. When: When an alibi is offered with unusual force or detail. Why: Insistence can signal a stake in the outcome rather than accuracy.
- [heuristic] A sudden change in a speaker's voice, manner or routine can make an explanation sound less natural and signal a shifting account worth closer checking. When: A speaker's demeanor, voice or routine changes while explaining, or a witness's behaviour changes. Why: Altered conduct often precedes or accompanies a change in what someone will say.
- [heuristic] When a suspect volunteers a statement that seems unnecessary, ask why they mentioned it, since the motive can reveal what they want to conceal. When: A suspect offers an unprompted or unexplained remark. Why: Unrequested statements often serve to steer or hide something.
- [heuristic] Compare a suspect's strong reaction with the excuse they give afterward, since a gap between them points to a concealed matter. When: A suspect's reaction is out of line with their stated reason. Why: A thin excuse covering a strong reaction suggests something hidden.
- [heuristic] Test a claimed precaution against the actual actions, since an item that contradicts the stated care reveals the claim as unreliable. When: a witness claims someone took care to avoid notice. Why: actions that undercut the stated precaution expose the explanation as false.
- [procedure] 1) Identify a checkable claim and locate a physical trace, such as a room, schedule or empty space. 2) Compare the claim with the trace; if it contradicts, discount the claim and treat the witness as mistaken or untruthful. 3) Eliminate candidates whose known features differ from the trace. 4) Test alibis against independent records. When: A character or witness gives an account that can be checked directly against physical traces. Why: Physical traces are more reliable than unverifiable testimony, and contradictions between accounts and evidence are the basis for new leads. (x2)
- [procedure] 1) Separate what the witness saw from what they heard, and mark verdicts and theories as opinion. 2) Keep the few concrete, checkable facts and discard verdicts. 3) Verify each fact against independent evidence. 4) Record conflicts and who could know what. When: A witness is talkative, emotional, or has a stake in how others are judged, or multiple witnesses give conflicting or emotionally charged accounts. Why: Fluent detail can hide that most of an account is opinion or second-hand report. Judgments cannot be tested, but facts can be corroborated, so separating them keeps the usable evidence.
- [procedure] 1) Separate minor claims from the main claim. 2) Identify each item's single source. 3) Check the source's stake in the outcome, since a party profiting from an accusation has reason to press it. 4) Compare claims against records or independent witnesses. 5) Check claims against observable facts such as stated income versus spending. When: When judging an accusation or evaluating testimony or papers in a case. Why: Separating and tracing claims shows which parts are supported and which depend on one biased source, since stated interest can bias what a witness reports.
- [procedure] 1) Ask whether the information was actually read or heard. 2) Ask its general drift. 3) Ask the source's identity. 4) Ask who it concerns. Each answer narrows the inference. When: Learning a secondhand or accidental disclosure. Why: Stepwise narrowing tests reliability before inference.
- [procedure] 1) List what the person knew at each step. 2) Ask what they would plausibly do if they held each candidate belief. 3) Compare the resulting route with their stated account. When: When checking whether a suspect's movements fit their story. Why: Reconstructing actions from beliefs exposes mismatches that the account hides.
- [procedure] 1) Start from a known event. 2) Ask what the suspect must do next given their danger and options. 3) Give each step a plausible reason. 4) Check each step against the time available and the evidence. When: When facts are incomplete and the suspect's options can be listed. Why: Anchoring each step to a motive and a deadline turns a story into a testable sequence.
- [procedure] 1) Break the account into individual details. 2) Test each detail against physical traces in the scene. 3) Compare it with the teller's earlier statements. 4) Note which details fail. 5) Keep each verified part separately. When: A detailed account can be compared with objects, traces or other testimony. Why: A detail that fails one check can expose the rest of the account, while true parts may still survive.
- [procedure] 1) Have the suspect state their account fully first. 2) Hold back a fact that contradicts it. 3) Once committed, state the fact flatly without argument. 4) Observe whether the suspect retracts or escalates. When: You hold one reliable fact contradicting a stated alibi. Why: Commitment before disclosure forces the suspect to choose between retraction and a harder denial, revealing more than an argument would.
- [procedure] 1) State a relevant fact to the suspect in a controlled setting. 2) Compare the suspect's verbal claims with their manner or word choice. 3) Note any slip that conflicts with a claim of ignorance. 4) Check the slip against other evidence before concluding. When: Testing whether a character is concealing knowledge. Why: A reaction that contradicts stated words can expose concealed knowledge, but needs corroboration.
- [pitfall] Vague, open-ended answers fit many questions, so they can seem to match a question more than they actually do. When: A source gives short, interpretable replies. Why: Broad answers let the listener supply the match themselves.
- [pitfall] A suspect's rationalisation of an earlier failure can sound convincing in the moment, so its internal contradictions must be checked before it is accepted. When: When an exposed suspect offers an explanation for past conduct. Why: Plausibility in the moment is not evidence of truth.
- [pitfall] An unseen accomplice blamed by a suspect should not be accepted without a way to test the claim. When: When a suspect attributes wrongdoing to unidentified others. Why: Untestable claims can shift blame without producing evidence.

## counter-accusation and deflection
- [model] A target who anticipates the investigator can reverse the situation by staging a false scene that draws the pursuer away.
- [definition] A counter-narrative is an official or flattering account that fills the gap left by the truth, and the audience can compare it against earlier evidence.
- [mechanism] A counsel-like NPC can challenge the weakest link in the player's case, forcing the player to reinforce that link before accusing. When: the player makes an accusation in a trial-like scene. Why: the challenge exposes gaps the player must fill.
- [behavior] A pressed suspect may accuse someone else to draw suspicion away, often with an early defensive remark before any accusation; timing and reaction to questioning reveal it. When: when pressed by questioning, or when a defensive remark is raised before any accusation is made. Why: diverting attention is an instinctive response to suspicion; fear of being named prompts early redirection. (x2)
- [behavior] Confronted accused often counter-accuse, shifting focus onto the questioner; such rebuttals read as malice, and an accuser lacking complete proof can find the dispute turned against them. When: when confronted before evidence is complete, or when a rebuttal names others. Why: rebuttals naming others read as evidence of malice; the accused can reframe the dispute by attacking the accuser's conduct. (x2)
- [behavior] A guarded suspect may answer with counter-questions, humor or remarks on the questioner's restlessness, leaving the question unanswered; the questioner must keep returning to the point. When: when a guarded character is asked about intentions or a sensitive question is deflected. Why: redirecting keeps control of the exchange and avoids disclosure; humor and counter-questions dodge exposed points.
- [behavior] Confronted with contradicting evidence, a suspect may attack its source: claiming a plot, blaming the informant, or saying investigators manufactured the evidence, rather than addressing the claim. When: when physical evidence is presented to an articulate suspect, or a stated account is contradicted and its source is identifiable. Why: shifting doubt onto the source weakens the evidence's force and redirects attention from the suspect's own story.
- [behavior] A character hiding a fact can offer lesser scandals in its place, so the questioner collects many stories but no verified fact. When: a character must hide a fact without appearing evasive. Why: a lesser scandal absorbs attention.
- [behavior] Listeners who correct a speaker's phrasing tend to scrutinise the wording and examine the substance of the report less. When: a relayed claim is challenged on grammar or form. Why: Attention moves to the form of speech.
- [behavior] A courteous, specific explanation can lower an audience's guard toward an unexplained entry, even when the only support is the stranger's own word. When: An unexpected visitor arrives without a clear reason. Why: Politeness and detail make the account feel trustworthy.
- [behavior] Digressions from the main question often reveal tensions a direct question would not elicit, because the speaker is not guarding those topics. When: talkative witness. Why: unguarded talk lowers the speaker's defences.
- [behavior] Grooming or checking a mirror during questioning can be deflection, signaling the subject treats the topic as of secondary interest.
- [behavior] A character who confidently dismisses ghost talk with a natural cause may be nervous, so the rational argument can serve as a defense. When: When a character rejects a supernatural explanation with visible unease. Why: Rational explanations can mask fear.
- [behavior] A person under questioning may avoid a direct answer through silence, or by making a counter-demand that turns the exchange toward the relationship between them. When: An interviewee has a reason to postpone the truth. Why: Silence and counter-demands shift the exchange and buy time without outright refusal.
- [behavior] A witness who does not want to answer may fill the conversation with flattery, physical courtesies, changes of subject and appeals to an earlier promise. When: A person is avoiding a question they would rather not answer. Why: Charm and digression move the talk away from the subject, so the question loses its grip.
- [behavior] A suspect who offers a rival suspect often invents a motive for that rival to answer an objection, and invented motives tend to show strain such as hesitation and repetition. When: A suspect points at an innocent party with a thin case against them. Why: A motive built to fill a gap is assembled under pressure rather than remembered.
- [behavior] A subject who keeps changing the topic may be steering away from the real matter on purpose, so the questioner should keep the goal in view. When: A subject diverts a line of questioning. Why: Diversion can be deliberate evasion rather than a random whim.
- [behavior] A suspect who believes no trace was left may become overconfident, mocking, and careless, and may unknowingly leave evidence behind. When: A suspect's confidence rests on a belief that evidence cannot be found. Why: Certainty that no trace exists reduces vigilance against leaving one.
- [rule] Accused NPCs answer a player's accusation with a counter-claim opening a new dialogue branch; the accusation succeeds only with proof, otherwise dialogue turns against the player. When: Designing confrontation dialogue. (x2)
- [rule] Use a quick-deflection dialogue option to flag a topic the NPC wants to avoid, inviting the player to investigate it.
- [rule] Offer a character-reference NPC as a counterweight, so the player chooses which witness to trust.
- [heuristic] Give the obvious suspect a counterweight of comparable weight, such as another suspect with a hidden motive, so the answer is not handed over too easily. When: the obvious suspect has a clear motive. Why: an equal rival keeps the reader or player from settling early.
- [procedure] 1) Collect the alibi witnesses' accounts of time and place. 2) Check the claimed travel against the schedule. 3) Weigh a precise, multi-witness alibi above a single witness's claim of an earlier meeting. When: An accused person has an alibi that conflicts with a prosecution witness. Why: A precise alibi supported by several witnesses and a fixed schedule is harder to dispute than one uncorroborated claim.
- [procedure] 1) Find one half of an action. 2) Search thoroughly for its missing counterpart. 3) Record whether the counterpart was found before drawing conclusions. When: a small trace implies a second part of an action.
- [tradeoff] Deflecting a direct question signals concealment, but it is not proof of guilt alone, since fear, pride or indifference can also cause evasion; weigh it with other evidence. When: when a character changes the subject when asked directly. Why: evasion signals concealment to the listener, but evasion has many causes and proves nothing alone. (x2)
- [pitfall] A counter-narrative that contradicts clues the audience has accepted, without showing why, weakens the mystery.
- [pitfall] A plausible alternative explanation, such as suicide or delirium, offered by a suspect can divert players from the decisive clue. When: when a suspect offers a convenient explanation. Why: a ready explanation feels like a resolution.
- [fact] A counter-witness is not automatically decisive; many defense depositions can still fail to overturn a prosecution.

## object evidence and reliability
- [behavior] A tiny swap of one object for a look-alike can strongly signal to observers that someone acted deliberately in that space. When: when an observer notices a changed object in a familiar setting. Why: small unexpected changes stand out against a known baseline.
- [behavior] A visible object can reveal that its wearer expected a specific threat, such as a blow, so its state carries information about preparation. When: an object's protective features suggest anticipated danger. Why: Equipment chosen for a particular threat signals what its wearer foresaw.
- [behavior] Listeners trust a story that seems plausible to them, even when their own expectations shaped how it was rebuilt. When: A rumour or report fits what the listener already expects. Why: Expectations fill gaps in what was actually observed.
- [behavior] People repeat what was printed in the press as if it were settled, so press reports strongly shape a group's views.
- [behavior] Whether an object is noticed depends on its setting: the same item goes unseen in a crowded, shabby place but is remembered in a quiet one. When: The item's surroundings contain many similar items or people. Why: Ordinary detail blends into its context, while an item that departs from its surroundings draws attention.
- [behavior] When rumours contest an in-world document, players must judge its truth themselves, so credibility stays an open question until the end. When: The document's claims are publicly disputed. Why: Competing rumours force players to weigh evidence.
- [rule] Do not assume an object was used in a crime just because it was found near the scene; check its custody chain first. When: An object is found near a crime scene. Why: Proximity alone does not show use or ownership in the act.

- [pitfall] A victim's silence about an attacker can mean many things, including shielding someone unrelated to the crime. When: when interpreting a victim who refuses to name an attacker. Why: Silence has many motives and does not identify the culprit.
- [pitfall] Someone already looking for a culprit may read a suspect's sternness, silence or dark smile as evidence of guilt. Why: Ambiguous expressions become suspicious when the accuser wants a culprit.

- [pitfall] A visible reaction from a target can be read as confirmation of a guess, but it may mean something else, so treat it as a signal rather than proof. - [pitfall] A strong reaction can point at the wrong person, so the first reading of a reaction may not be the full story. When: interpreting a dramatic response in a confrontation. Why: emotion can be directed at someone other than the presumed culprit.

- [pitfall] An official report presented as a finished solution can end the player's search early, so it should be checkable for contradictions. When: when an authority presents an explanation as complete. Why: a trusted source makes the question seem settled.

## logical inference and proof
- [behavior] A single reaction, such as to a staged reveal, can mean surprise, fear, recognition, shock or guilt, so judge it only against other facts and later accounts. When: A single reaction is used as evidence about a person's knowledge or guilt. Why: The same visible reaction has several possible causes.
- [behavior] An investigator often accepts a smooth explanation that resolves an anomaly and moves on without rechecking the contradiction it covers. When: A plausible explanation is offered for a contradiction. Why: Resolving the anomaly reduces the urge to check further.
- [behavior] Investigators should test human explanations, such as access, motive and timing, before accepting an uncanny cause, because a mundane cause is usually ruled out only through checking. When: A supernatural explanation is tempting but a human one has not been fully ruled out. Why: Premature acceptance of an uncanny cause skips checkable evidence of access and timing.
- [behavior] Players and investigators can infer a hidden opening from the sound of breaking glass or from a character's habits, even when no opening is visible. When: A room's apparent seal conflicts with an audible or habitual clue. Why: Sound and habit are indirect evidence that reveal a route the layout hides.
- [behavior] Players are persuaded more by the pattern of several matching dates than by any single date. When: When several independent records are presented on one timeline. Why: Accumulated matches form a convincing whole that single matches cannot.
- [behavior] Players who accept a sealed room as impossible stop questioning who controlled access, so the assumed seal becomes the real lock on the solution. When: a sealed room is presented as proof of an impossible death. Why: trust in the seal suppresses checks on the person who opens it.
- [behavior] When an intruder vanishes from a sealed space, audiences first assume a hidden passage, keeping belief in a physical explanation rather than the impossible. When: a figure disappears from a space with all exits closed. Why: a hidden route feels less impossible than no route at all.
- [behavior] Players who decode a partial code by pattern can deduce a missing entry and feel progress, then commit to a theory before its contradictions appear. - [heuristic] Separate what can be known from a silhouette, its outline, from what cannot, its interior details, and state the distinction explicitly. When: When reporting or judging an identification from a backlit view. Why: Blending outline with assumed detail produces unwarranted confident identifications.
- [heuristic] An alternative explanation of evidence still needs its own corroboration before it can be accepted. When: offering a counter-reading of an item. Why: a counter-theory can overreach by relying on unverified claims about who touched what.
- [procedure] 1) List each apparent proof against a suspect. 2) Test each separately. 3) Mark each as confirmed, weak or contradicted. 4) Judge the suspect only on the points that survive testing. When: A case rests on several apparently strong points. Why: Chains of plausible points can hide weak links.
- [procedure] 1) Compare what was found with what the situation leads you to expect. 2) Check the absence against the normal routine. 3) Note the anomaly. 4) Confirm that every fact needed for the inference is already available to the player. When: Solving a case from a gap such as a missing item, entry or step. Why: A deduction is only fair when the facts that support it are already on the page or in the scene.

When: testing a case built on several incriminating items. Why: alternative explanations expose whether each item actually supports the theory.
- [pitfall] A reconstruction shows that a method is possible, not that it was actually used. Drama can make a plausible method feel proven, so check it against physical evidence. When: When evaluating a staged reconstruction or presenting one as an explanation of an event. Why: Feasibility and drama do not establish actual cause.
- [pitfall] Calling a chain of reasoning elementary does not make it correct, and overconfidence makes a sound method seem trivial even when it solves serious cases. When: Presenting or judging a deduction. Why: Simplicity of statement is mistaken for weakness of reasoning.
- [pitfall] Once a legal principle is declared settled, participants may be told that questioning it is not their role.
- [pitfall] An investigator who tests a prediction may read a confirming result as proof while overlooking evidence that the prediction failed. When: Interpreting the outcome of a reaction test. Why: Investigators tend to favour results that match their own view.
- [pitfall] Lack of reaction can mean inattention or masking, not absence of the event. Treating silence as proof that a claim is false is a mistake. When: Others present did not respond to a reported action. Why: Noise, distraction, or preoccupation can hide an event from observers.
- [pitfall] Consistency between two baits can be mistaken for proof that both are true. When: when a target evaluates linked stories. Why: Agreement between separate sources feels like corroboration even when both are planted.

## threat and dread
- [mechanism] A fear state can make it unclear whether an action was chosen or forced, which creates doubt about one's own agency. When: A character is under fear or stress. Why: Impaired judgment blurs the line between intent and impulse.
- [behavior] Long waiting in darkness or silence, with something seeming imminent, raises tension and sharpens the senses before any event occurs. - [behavior] Fear from an uncanny sight or eerie setting can persist after its mundane explanation is known, so feeling and cause can be separated. - [behavior] A hinted or unstated threat unsettles more than a stated one, because listeners fill the gap with their own worst-case conclusion. When: a threat is suggested or hinted at rather than shown or stated. Why: the mind supplies its own worst-case picture and suspicion.

- [behavior] When two observers independently reach the same unease about one event, the shared reaction reads as stronger evidence that something has happened. When: Two characters react to the same disturbance without coordinating. Why: Independent agreement reads as corroboration.
- [behavior] A lone watcher in a dark, still building tends to imagine footsteps and grows more fearful as the wait goes on. When: Watching alone in a dark, silent place. Why: Isolation and silence amplify the perception of small sounds.
- [behavior] A visible looming threat keeps players expecting a payoff during a stalled investigation. When: the investigation pauses without resolution. Why: Anticipation sustains attention while progress is slow.
- [behavior] Deaths and livestock losses are attributed to people who earlier made threats or curses, linking misfortune to grudge.
- [behavior] Players waiting for a promised event tend to imagine threats worse than any real danger, which produces dread. When: An anticipated moment is delayed while nothing visible happens. Why: Uncertainty leaves a gap that the imagination fills with worst cases.
- [behavior] A person who senses that others are watching or suspicious tends to feel hunted, and that fear can grow and push them to overreact. When: When a suspect perceives observers' attention. Why: Perceived scrutiny raises anxiety, which can change behavior and reveal more.
- [behavior] A target can become terrified by a completely invented threat, such as a watcher who does not exist, so the fear comes from the story alone.
- [behavior] A sudden quiet after repeated noise is treated as more threatening than the noise itself, because silence can hide a threat. When: A threat has been signalled repeatedly and then stops. Why: Players infer that a quiet period may conceal danger.
- [behavior] A victim who reasons from a belief that the threat is contained will dismiss a credible warning, letting the threat proceed. When: a warning arrives that conflicts with the victim's belief about the threat. Why: People trust a prior conclusion over contrary evidence when that conclusion is comforting.
- [heuristic] Physical reactions described in a scene, such as a sick feeling or prickling skin, heighten the sense of threat. When: Building tension in a frightening moment.
- [pitfall] Uncanny sensations or disturbing images, such as dread or unease, are not evidence of a cause and must not be taken as proof of one. When: a character or player relies on feelings or a disturbing image to identify a source. Why: Sensations are shaped by expectation and cannot be traced to a specific origin; odd sights can stand in for evidence.
- [pitfall] A setting's mood can make an observer read more danger, or a supernatural cause, into an ordinary event than the evidence supports. When: interpreting a dark, tense, or threatening location, or an unexplained event within an atmospheric setting. Why: Atmosphere heightens perceived threat and primes people to read ambiguous sensations as unnatural, independent of actual evidence.
- [pitfall] Silencing a witness through threats hides the trail from the investigator and removes evidence.
- [pitfall] Fear can decide how an ambiguous clue is read before the evidence settles it, producing a confident but unsupported interpretation. When: A character or player is frightened while interpreting a scene. Why: Emotional state biases interpretation toward the most threatening reading.

## withheld information

## single-detail test design
- [behavior] A witness with incomplete memory often recalls forgotten details only when given a specific prompt, not general questions.
- [behavior] A single damning fact can revive suspicion of a figure already thought settled, so suspicion can rise and fall as evidence arrives. When: when a suspect appears cleared. Why: one fact can reopen a question closed by earlier reasoning.
- [behavior] A vivid, specific detail makes an admission feel credible and hard to disprove, though the detail may also be invented to persuade. When: A suspect explains a past action. Why: Specificity signals honesty to listeners, whether or not the detail is true.
- [behavior] Players and characters quickly judge a trap by its results, so a failed sting is often read as proof the bait was never real. When: A trap yields no capture. Why: Absence of outcome is interpreted as a flaw in the scheme, damaging the investigator's standing.
- [rule] Treat demeanour, manner or belongings as hypotheses, not proof; test them against stronger evidence such as records or dental or physical checks. When: When inferring a suspect's background from behaviour or choosing how to confirm an identity. Why: Manner is easily performed and can mislead; stronger tests are less easily faked than personal items.
- [rule] Ask a targeted question that could confirm or refute a new lead, such as asking staff whether they have seen a person with a specific feature. When: When a new lead needs checking. Why: A specific question produces a result that clearly supports or contradicts the lead.
- [heuristic] Reward players who test the weakest link in a claimed schedule instead of the whole story at once. When: When designing alibi or timeline puzzles. Why: Focused testing is how players progress through a chain of claims.
- [procedure] 1) Hold one independent fact that you know to be true or false. 2) Ask the witness to restate the whole account. 3) Press on the single checkable point rather than the whole story. When: When you have one independent fact that can test a witness's account. Why: Full restatements expose inconsistencies, and a single checkable point gives a clear test without needing outside corroboration of everything.
- [procedure] 1) Pick one fact only an informed person would react to. 2) Reveal it and observe the reaction. 3) Repeat the same claim and see whether the reaction changes. 4) Choose the next question from the response. 5) Hold back other details for later. When: You need to know whether a suspect is informed about a named object or person. Why: A consistent or changing reaction to repeated cues indicates knowledge; held-back details preserve leverage.
- [procedure] 1) Have a witness commit to a description first. 2) Reveal the matching object suddenly, without explanation. 3) Watch for a start or change in composure, and in voice and body. When: a witness has already described an object or person. Why: prior commitment to a description makes a match to a sudden object meaningful.
- [procedure] 1) Speak a trigger word or place where the suspect can hear it, without addressing them. 2) Watch for a small stop in movement. 3) Record the reaction as a probability, paired with other evidence. When: a suspect denies knowledge of a place, name or event. Why: an unaddressed trigger reveals recognition without alerting the suspect.
- [pitfall] Building a theory or actor profile on one detail, such as a back wound or odd clothing, misleads: the detail may have an innocent cause or other evidence may disagree. When: When an odd detail or single feature is the main basis for a conclusion or profile. Why: Many details have ordinary explanations that only wider evidence can separate; a single detail can mislead when the rest of the scene points elsewhere.
- [pitfall] Searching only at one scale, such as tiny fragments, causes an investigator to miss evidence in plain view. When: Surveying a scene for evidence. Why: Attention fixed on small items overlooks large, conspicuous objects.
- [pitfall] Revealing a clue too early wastes the chance to observe how the target reacts to it. When: Deciding when to show evidence to a suspect. Why: Once the target knows the clue, the reaction it would have produced is lost.

## suspect selection and checking
- [behavior] Players move suspicion to the next candidate only after the prior one is cleared, and hold each new suspect until a test or physical detail contradicts it. When: a mystery with several plausible suspects or competing explanations. Why: Each dismissal or collapse opens a new lead and earns trust in the next layer; each accusation feels resolved until contradicted. (x2)
- [behavior] Players feel progress when a candidate list shrinks through checks they perform themselves, even when the remaining uncertainty is still high. When: A suspect search narrows through in-world checks. Why: Each elimination gives visible evidence that the search is advancing.
- [behavior] A speaker who asks what they revealed while feverish shows they are weighing the risk of exposure. When: someone who was feverish or raving. Why: they cannot tell how much was kept back.
- [behavior] Accusations tend to fall on neighbours with unusual habits, unpopular standing or irregular lives rather than on strangers. When: A community is building a case against someone.
- [behavior] Public sympathy for one suspect can steer which person is pursued, even when other suspects have comparable opportunity. When: A sympathetic suspect is set against unsympathetic ones. Why: Sympathy shapes whom people are willing to investigate.
- [behavior] Investigators hold suspicion on one suspect until a link to a wider group forces a fresh look, which can revive a previously cleared suspect.
- [behavior] A verified alibi clears a suspect, but a later question about it can flip belief back to suspicion and then to release. When: when a suspect's alibi is checked and then challenged. Why: each new test of the claim moves belief again.
- [behavior] A late evidence cluster can point at the least-suspected character, because the player has spent suspicion on others. When: after several suspects have been cleared. Why: Earlier suspicion was exhausted on other characters.
- [heuristic] Shared common traits, such as a recurring habit, age, height or build, narrow suspects but do not prove identity, since many people share them. When: Matching a disguised person or description to a known profile. Why: Shared traits apply to large numbers of people, so they are weak evidence on their own.
- [heuristic] Identify the evidence that actually supports a suspicion, then press it with questions to see whether it survives. When: Evaluating a theory built on a suspicious detail. Why: Suspicions often lack the evidence that would support them once examined.
- [heuristic] A single unusual physical trait can narrow identity sharply, but only after it is checked against the original missing-person record, since the record may omit it. When: Using a distinctive trait to match a found body or item to a candidate. Why: A rare trait is strong evidence only if it was actually recorded earlier; an unrecorded trait cannot confirm or exclude anyone.
- [procedure] 1) Start with the last people who housed or employed the subject. 2) Record what each saw directly versus heard secondhand. 3) Collect any letters or papers they can provide. 4) Fix dates using dated sources. 5) Note where each trail ends and pick the next lead from that gap. When: A person has moved away and their past survives only in others' memories and papers. Why: Each witness covers only a partial window of contact, so the full history is assembled from overlapping partial accounts.
- [pitfall] Treating an access rule, such as a required presence, as absolute can hide gaps that a timed test reveals. When: An investigation relies on a rule about who may enter a room. Why: Rules of presence often have timing or procedural gaps.
- [pitfall] Checking only the suspects you already favour causes missed culprits. When: When an investigator has a preferred suspect. Why: Narrow focus skips other people who had the same access and opportunity.

## timeline and chronology
- [mechanism] A witness who cannot recall a date may recall an event when it is linked to a separate, vivid detail such as a harvest, a garment, or a season. When: Trying to recover a memory that cannot be reached by direct date questions. Why: Concrete associated cues give memory retrieval a route that dates alone do not provide.
- [mechanism] A traveller who sleeps or cannot see the route misjudges how much time passed, so their estimate of duration can conflict with people who were present. When: A witness was asleep or enclosed and cannot see the route or clock. Why: Sensory cues are limited, so duration is inferred from sound and feel.
- [behavior] Sincere witnesses' recalled times and durations are approximate; they can misjudge by hours without noticing, especially when tired or asleep. When: A witness reports a duration or time of an event from memory. Why: Subjective time perception is unreliable and the error is not felt by the witness.
- [behavior] A cluster of similar events can look random until the intervals between them are calculated and compared. When: When players review a log of repeated events. Why: Interval patterns are only visible when the entries are listed together and measured.
- [behavior] Players keep suspecting a person whose clues are real but whose timing is misread, until an alibi breaks that reading. When: when physical clues fit a suspect but times conflict. Why: Real clues carry weight even when their timing is wrong.
- [behavior] Players spend their attention on suspects' motives rather than on timing evidence, so a clock-based mechanism can stay hidden. When: mysteries where suspects are chosen on motive. Why: Motive draws focus to people, away from the clock.
- [rule] Do not dismiss a clue as fake merely because it is convenient; test its timing first. When: When a clue seems too neat to be genuine. Why: Convenience alone does not prove staging, and a suspicious detail may be innocent.
- [heuristic] Check a witness's time references against each other and against the event sequence; descriptions that point to different periods signal a conflict to resolve. When: A witness's timing matters to a theory about who was present. Why: Timing contradictions can rule out accounts that cannot fit the sequence.
- [heuristic] A covert act timed to a climax, when attention peaks, is more likely to pass unnoticed than one done during a quiet moment. When: Planning or designing a covert action during a scripted event. Why: Peak attention is directed at the main moment.
- [procedure] 1) Order events chronologically. 2) Attach each event to its source. 3) Draw conclusions only after the ordered sequence is complete. When: Reconstructing a case from multiple sources. Why: Ordered, sourced events make each conclusion traceable to evidence.
- [procedure] 1) Ask the witness for the longest time they were away. 2) Record each answer as an upper bound, keeping the tightest ceiling. 3) Ignore digressions that do not answer the question. 4) Compare the bound against a fixed event. 5) Conclude whether the time was sufficient for the purpose. When: A witness's timing matters but their memory is unreliable or their attention was elsewhere. Why: A maximum bound is more robust than a precise guess, and fixed events anchor the estimate.
- [pitfall] Treating an approximate medical estimate as an exact time, or missing that a fast-acting method implies the perpetrator was close to the victim. When: When reasoning from a medical timing estimate. Why: Overprecision and missed implications lead to wrong suspect lists.
- [pitfall] A delay that seems arbitrary to the audience can lose their goodwill, especially when the request to wait irritates them before its purpose is clear. When: a reveal is postponed without a visible reason. Why: unexplained waiting reads as stalling.
- [pitfall] A witness who slept between checks cannot vouch for the hours in between, so those intervals must stay unverified.

## interview silence and tells
- [mechanism] Severity or a sudden harsh tone halts a frightened witness's answers, so the interviewer should avoid it. When: Questioning a witness who is already afraid. Why: Fear reduces willingness and ability to speak.
- [behavior] Players denied an answer may read the delay as evasiveness or as being mocked, especially when they already suspect the investigator. When: an investigator withholds conclusions. Why: withholding looks like hiding the result.
- [behavior] A brief pause or change in a person's routine action when a key word is spoken can signal that the topic matters to them. When: Questioning someone who has hidden knowledge. Why: Involuntary reactions reveal interest even when the person says nothing.
- [behavior] Concessions such as 'I can't say' give little information but still signal that the witness is holding something back. When: A witness deflects a direct question. Why: Evasion is itself a reaction that reveals constraint or concealment.
- [behavior] A calm reaction to a known name can mean guilt, practiced composure, or innocence, so a single reaction is ambiguous. When: A suspect shows no visible reaction to a name the investigator tested. Why: Several causes produce the same calm surface.
- [behavior] In an interview both sides may hold information back to test the other, so disclosure often proceeds in alternating turns. When: Two parties each hold information the other wants. Why: Each side fears giving away an advantage before learning the other's position.
- [rule] Record repeated 'I don't remember' answers as information rather than as empty results, and stop pressing once further questions yield nothing. When: When a source repeatedly claims no memory of an event. Why: Persistent non-recall can signal a sensitive area, and continued pressure produces diminishing returns and distress.
- [rule] Wait out a pause instead of rushing to the next question, since silence often prompts the witness to speak. When: A witness has stopped answering. Why: Silence creates pressure that can draw out information.
- [heuristic] Witness emotional shifts, such as fear turning to cunning, may signal concealed knowledge or reflect fear or grief; use them to choose where to press, not as proof of guilt. When: Observing a witness during questioning. Why: Emotional signals are ambiguous and can have innocent causes; they indicate where to look, not evidence in themselves.
- [heuristic] Weigh a remark by the target's observed reaction rather than by the words alone; a jest that visibly lands marks a real personal sensitivity. When: When interpreting teasing or jokes between people. Why: The same words carry different weight depending on how they affect the listener.
- [pitfall] Silence or a partial reaction can look like a confession when it actually reflects fear or loyalty, so it must be weighed against other evidence. When: When interpreting a witness who avoids the central point under pressure. Why: Emotional states such as fear or loyalty produce the same outward signs as guilt.
- [pitfall] Bluffed disdain or feigned ignorance can be read by the other party as puzzlement, so bluffing does not reliably hide knowledge. When: A party uses feigned ignorance to conceal knowledge. Why: Odd manner signals uncertainty rather than control to the listener.

## motive and self-interest
- [mechanism] Sincere piety and a sense of justice, good in themselves, can push passions toward harsher action when combined with personal motives.
- [behavior] Dependents or sources with a stake in the outcome may shape accounts to keep favor, shifting after promises and offering rumour, or steering questioners toward or away from people. When: Assessing testimony from servants or dependents tied to someone in authority; when a source controls access to information or to people needed for questioning. Why: Continued favor gives the witness a stake in the outcome; a source's own stake shapes what they reveal and withhold while still appearing helpful.
- [behavior] A person under heavy debt may pursue a costly claim because it may be the only realistic way to repay what is owed. When: debts are pressing. Why: Limited alternatives raise the value of the claim.
- [behavior] Once one suspect fits an expected pattern, investigators tend to dismiss other motives for the same reason, leaving those leads unexamined. When: A suspect looks obviously guilty early. Why: Confirmation of the favoured suspect lowers interest in alternatives.
- [behavior] A concealed family relation can explain a witness's interest in the victim, since fear of exposure creates a motive to stay close to the case. When: When a witness hides a relationship with a party in the case. Why: Concealment reveals the stakes the witness is protecting.
- [behavior] Denials about a missing object can reveal more than admissions, and a contradiction can give others a motive to name a rival. When: when witnesses are questioned about an item. Why: Being caught in a contradiction shifts suspicion onto others.
- [behavior] A person who pushes past others to reach a scene shows a strong need to control it, which is worth noting.
- [behavior] People often read a change of fortune that favors them as a reward for their own merit, which colors their guess about the motive behind it. When: A character learns of a decision that benefits them. Why: Self-interest biases the interpretation of another's reasoning.
- [behavior] A person who hides to protect someone will break cover when they fear that protected person is in danger. When: A hidden person's concealment is driven by loyalty to someone else. Why: Protective motives can outweigh the wish to stay hidden.
- [behavior] Relatives with a financial stake in an estate tend to keep guarded distance from each other and show mutual suspicion. When: an inheritance is at stake.
- [behavior] Strong attachment can produce contradictory behavior: contempt for a partner can coexist with refusing to leave, so both signals point to the same underlying bond. When: when interpreting a character's conflicting feelings and actions. Why: Attachment alone can keep people in relationships they openly resent.
- [behavior] Players suspect whoever stands to lose most from the truth, so motive-linked suspicion guides their reading of ambiguous evidence. When: when a mystery hinges on who benefits from concealment. Why: players assume that a loss creates a motive to lie.

## partial information
- [behavior] A person who hears only part of a tense private exchange misreads it, filling gaps with their own fears or jealousy. When: overhearing fragments. Why: missing context is filled by emotion.
- [behavior] Each person reads the same events from partial information, so accounts diverge depending on what each was told. When: people share an event but not its hidden details. Why: withheld facts change interpretation.
- [behavior] A witness who watches the wrong side of a scene can miss a remarkable event entirely, so partial sight can hide what actually happened. When: A witness's position covers only part of the action. Why: A witness is only as reliable as the angle they look from.
- [behavior] A suspect who has lied earlier may later admit part of the lie with a sincere-sounding reason while still denying the central accusation. When: Witnesses already contradict the suspect's first account. Why: Partial admission reduces the cost of the lie and keeps the core denial intact.
- [behavior] A confirmed but tangential secret offers a ready explanation for odd behaviour, so people treat the case as explained and stop testing the rest. When: A partly true personal secret is revealed alongside a suspicious act. Why: Partial truth makes the remainder easier to accept.
- [behavior] A speaker who starts to reveal something and then stops leaves the group guessing and holds their attention.
- [behavior] Partial credit for correct observations gives players a sense of progress even when their final accusation fails. Why: credit for valid reasoning is felt as achievement.
- [behavior] In shouting, drunken or confused crowds, a listener may catch only one firm fact while everything else stays lost in noise. When: when an argument is conducted loudly. Why: overlapping voices prevent a full account.
- [behavior] People recognise an item from a partly visible label by its word shape, not a full reading, and this recognition can be wrong. When: text is only partly visible. Why: recognition relies on familiar word shapes.
- [behavior] A witness who leaves out what they cannot say shows it through hesitation and changed manner, and attentive listeners can sense the omission. When: A witness gives a partial account of a painful or difficult event. Why: Emotional resistance shows in behaviour even when the words are incomplete.
- [behavior] Players accept a false suspect when clues arrive as isolated fragments, because no frame connects them until the final reconstruction. When: Clues are scattered and held back until the end of a case. Why: Without a frame, each fragment looks like a separate oddity rather than part of one plan.
- [pitfall] A source's knowledge is bounded by their role and when they joined, so a denial may reflect limited access. Ask what they could have seen before judging them unhelpful. When: when choosing which NPC to question, or when a source denies knowing something. Why: Role and timing determine access to information, and a denial may reflect limited access rather than absence of information.

## trust and credibility judgments
- [behavior] A depicted face can suggest a character's hidden traits, but exaggerated color and hard detail shape the impression the viewer forms. When: A viewer reads a portrait as a character clue. Why: Stylistic choices bias the reading.
- [behavior] People tend to accept newcomers who look polished and admirable without checking their claims, so appearance can persuade more than verification. When: When a newcomer presents a credible look and a letter or claim, and no check is made. Why: Polish and appearance create trust that bypasses scrutiny.
- [behavior] Quick, over-eager agreement with a point can be a sign that the speaker is concealing something. When: A witness agrees too readily with an interviewer's statement. Why: Unusually fast endorsement can signal an attempt to steer the conversation.
- [behavior] Identity clues tend to arrive in a fast cluster after a long buildup, so understanding accelerates near the climax.
- [behavior] Players dismiss a place's folklore or ghost reputation as a hoax, treating a hidden room as rumor until physical evidence shows it is real and reachable. When: When a location has a supernatural reputation and no physical evidence yet. Why: Players discount unverified stories about haunted places, so physical proof is what changes their belief.
- [behavior] Audiences move suspicion away from whoever seems most helpful, so a helpful figure can hide the true antagonist. When: a helper is also a potential suspect. Why: Helpfulness reads as loyalty and lowers scrutiny.
- [behavior] Players read a sudden death under a relative's care as a family quarrel over money until evidence contradicts that reading. When: early in a case involving a will or an inheritance.
- [behavior] A protagonist's own misconduct weakens the credibility of their accusations, so players discount what that character claims afterward. When: the protagonist has been caught in wrongdoing earlier.
- [heuristic] Give a starting persona a visible idle trait, so later careful play feels like growth rather than a sudden skill jump. Why: A visible baseline makes improved play feel earned to the player.
- [heuristic] A published description of a person that is unusually detailed can reveal how much the authorities already suspect. When: Reading a public description issued in a missing-person case. Why: Detail beyond what a search needs signals private suspicion.
- [pitfall] Keeping the accuser powerless for too long drains player interest.
- [pitfall] A surprising claim made by a credentialed expert can seem settled before the other evidence has been weighed. When: When an authoritative figure presents an unexpected conclusion. Why: Perceived expertise lends weight that the evidence has not yet earned.

## bystander and audience reactions
- [behavior] Audience reactions such as cheering, acceptance, laughter, or oaths and mockery signal a speaker's social standing, and listeners weigh them as evidence when judging credibility. When: when a case is judged in front of a crowd or a speaker addresses a crowd. Why: people treat others' acceptance as evidence; group reactions indicate social standing.
- [behavior] A strong reaction in a shared space draws bystanders and can unbalance the witness, so the probe has social consequences. When: the probe is made in a public or shared place.
- [behavior] Other people nearby may notice an exchange and draw their own conclusions from it, so an observed reaction can spread.
- [behavior] Bystanders often ask their own questions about why someone is asking, and may hold back where the asker comes from. When: A stranger is questioned about another person. Why: Curious bystanders weigh the asker's motives.
- [behavior] When an audience splits behind rival explanations, each camp rejects the other's theory and may refuse proof that goes against its own. When: A public investigation offers several competing explanations. Why: Committed supporters defend their chosen theory against contrary evidence.
- [behavior] Onlookers accept a brief absence as ordinary but grow suspicious as the wait lengthens, turning confusion into rumor and scandal stories. When: a character leaves a public gathering without explanation. Why: A short gap has innocent explanations, while a long unexplained gap does not.
- [behavior] A group that falls silent and turns away on the player's approach signals hidden activity or a nearby concealed area. When: A group of NPCs is encountered in a space. Why: Sudden silence and avoided eye contact read as concealment, prompting the player to search.
- [behavior] A person whose dramatic act goes unnoticed may doubt their own perception and adopt the explanation that restores their composure, even against the evidence. When: A loud or significant action draws no visible response from the group. Why: People prefer explanations that protect their sense of self and calm.
- [procedure] To test a claimed event, check whether the people present reacted to it. Missing expected reactions weigh against the claim but do not prove it false, since attention is directional and most of a crowd may have been facing elsewhere. When: One witness reports a dramatic event that others seem not to notice. Why: Real events usually leave traces in other people's behaviour.
- [pitfall] Shared emotion between two witnesses can make a coincidence feel like proof of a connection. Why: Emotional resonance feels like confirmation even when it is not evidence.
- [pitfall] The mood of spectators can run ahead of the evidence, so their awe or hostility shapes how testimony is heard and read. When: testimony is delivered in front of a charged audience. Why: Emotional pressure leads listeners to read implication as accusation before facts support it.

## temperament
- [behavior] People instinctively attribute a series of deaths to the supernatural, which lets an ordinary killer strike unnoticed among them. When: when a run of deaths is believed to have a supernatural cause. Why: the instinct to blame curses draws attention away from human action.
- [behavior] A body stiffens after death, holding its posture until limbs are moved, so the pose at discovery must be recorded first. When: A corpse is found in a seated or posed position. Why: Rigor holds the position until someone straightens the limbs.
- [behavior] A person's temperament governs how they carry out a deed; an impulsive, bold person acts openly and leaves different traces than a planned, careful one. When: Judging whether a method fits a suspect. Why: Temperament shapes the style, care and visibility of an action.
- [behavior] A character acting on strong emotion makes poor tactical choices, such as a reckless chase, which draws attention and undermines their goal. When: A character is pursuing a target while emotionally aroused. Why: Emotional intensity overrides careful planning, causing collisions and exposure.
- [behavior] A disguised or changed person tends to drift back toward old habits and speech, while deeper character structure remains stable. When: A person returns to ordinary life after a major change or disguise. Why: Surface change such as opinion or manner fades, while stable traits persist.
- [behavior] A person's temperament predicts the kind of crime they would commit: someone whose values are already satisfied by fantasy is unlikely to commit an act that conflicts with them. When: when judging whether a suspect fits a crime. Why: people act on the motives their temperament makes salient.
- [heuristic] Let interrogation dialogue use temperament tags, with silence and private scenes available as tools the player can choose. When: Designing a suspect interrogation system.
- [heuristic] Physical strength does not guarantee the temperament to harm someone directly, so ability alone should not settle a theory.
- [pitfall] Assigning suspects to stock character types lets every trait be read as evidence for the type, and the type then absorbs contradictory facts. When: When a theory is built from stereotypes rather than observed facts. Why: Once a type is assumed, nearly any detail can be fitted into it.
- [pitfall] Impressions of temperament are easily mistaken for facts about a person, so they should not be recorded as identifying traits. Why: Impressions reflect the observer's reading as much as the person.

## witness testimony reliability
- [model] Testimony arrives in layers: physical facts first, then household staff, then an intimate confidante whose account narrows the field of suspects. When: a case is built from several witnesses of differing closeness to the victim. Why: Each layer adds knowledge and closer witnesses reveal more about motive and who knew what.
- [behavior] A distressed witness may be unable to track times, and answers can shift when a tangible reward is offered. When: Questioning a witness whose fear distracts from the facts. Why: Distress consumes attention, and rewards change how willing the witness is to answer.
- [behavior] A minor hesitation in a witness's answers can shape how the rest of their testimony is read. When: Evaluating a witness's account of events. Why: Small signs of uncertainty color the perceived credibility of everything else they say.
- [behavior] Testimony from people who observed a suspect closely can contradict the suspect's own account, revealing the real motive. When: when a suspect's self-description conflicts with observers' accounts. Why: observers see the suspect's conduct, which a self-description can hide.
- [behavior] Emotional display in a confession, such as tears, tells observers little about truth, since it can accompany false confessions. When: a confession is delivered with visible emotion. Why: Emotion is not diagnostic of honesty, yet observers tend to read it as sincerity.
- [heuristic] Weigh an informant by checking specific, verifiable details against other evidence; precise, checkable particulars count for more than general or sympathetic accounts. When: Several sources describe the same person and each can be checked on at least one point. Why: Specific claims can be confirmed or refuted, while vague or emotionally colored claims cannot.
- [heuristic] When several sources describe the same events differently, record every conflicting version rather than choosing one early, then sort the contradictions to see which claims can hold together. When: Two or more accounts of the same events clash and no single source is clearly reliable. Why: Early selection hides contradictions that may reveal the true sequence.
- [heuristic] When witnesses report hearing nothing, ask what physical arrangement would explain the silence rather than discarding the answer. When: a witness says nothing unusual was heard or seen. Why: A lack of sound or sight is informative when it points to a constraint such as distance or separation.
- [procedure] 1) Meet the witness in a neutral place away from others. 2) Offer payment to secure cooperation. 3) Reassure them they will not be punished. 4) Ask open questions first, then narrower ones. 5) Build a timeline of when events occurred. 6) Follow up contradictions until the account settles. 7) Do not reveal what you already know. When: Questioning a frightened, lower-status witness who saw something relevant. Why: Neutral setting, reassurance and payment lower fear and encourage honest, sequenced answers.
- [procedure] 1) Discover a contradiction in a witness's account. 2) Hold it back rather than confronting immediately. 3) Use it at a later confrontation. When: Questioning a witness whose account conflicts with known facts. Why: Withholding a contradiction preserves leverage for a stronger later challenge.

## reputation and trust
- [mechanism] A reputation for helpfulness can raise a player's trust in a charming NPC, so the player's trust becomes part of the trap. When: NPC reputation system influences dialogue trust. Why: Trust based on reputation makes the hidden threat harder to see.
- [behavior] Audiences trust a helpful or credentialed investigator's conclusions and rarely check them, so one revealed as the culprit hides in plain sight and makes a sharper twist. When: the investigator or expert is also the culprit and their authority or helpful role goes unchallenged. Why: Trust in the helper's visible role or authority lends the account credibility the audience rarely checks, which hides the culprit.
- [behavior] An investigator may prefer a harder solution because it earns more credit, so judgment is partly shaped by reputation rather than facts alone. When: When a person has professional incentives tied to the outcome of an investigation. Why: Expected recognition for a difficult answer can pull conclusions away from the evidence.
- [behavior] When the protagonist cannot tell whether information is real, the audience comes to share that doubt. When: a character faces unverified information. Why: Keeping the character's uncertainty visible links the audience to the character's state.
- [behavior] A bad reputation makes ordinary losses and accidents read as accusations, so suspicion attaches to a person's name rather than to any specific act. When: when a community already distrusts someone. Why: people blame a known figure for unexplained misfortune.
- [behavior] A true observation can be dismissed when the witness has a poor reputation or when a plausible cover story was offered at the time, and survives only as gossip. When: when a witness report conflicts with a stable reputation or fits a familiar explanation. Why: Reputation and a ready explanation make the report seem like eccentricity rather than evidence.
- [heuristic] A sympathetic reading of a relationship can outweigh technical proof with some decision-makers.
- [heuristic] A witness who readily admits not knowing, and reacts openly to a question, tends to be more reliable.
- [pitfall] Reputation can mislead, and the most suspicious-seeming candidate may turn out to be innocent.

## offender self-betrayal
- [mechanism] Under threat and exposure, a suspect's words and movements can reveal knowledge they meant to hide. Watch for sudden lunges, insistence, or statements that go beyond what the evidence allows. When: A suspect is cornered and the situation is under the investigator's control. Why: Stress lowers control over what a person says and does.
- [behavior] Audiences may overestimate an investigator's confidence and assume a puzzle is equally puzzling to the investigator, which misleads them. When: a narrator reports a puzzle they cannot solve. Why: the narrator's uncertainty is projected onto the investigator.
- [behavior] A frightened person often acts on instinct, so their actions may not follow a neat plan and may contradict a rational explanation. When: Judging an act made immediately after a violent event. Why: Instinct rather than reasoning drives action under fright.
- [behavior] Offenders often give themselves away by spending or celebrating freely soon after a crime. When: shortly after a crime. Why: the sudden change in behaviour draws notice.
- [behavior] Pride in a method can lead an offender to disclose it, for example by writing to show how clever he was. When: An offender who considers his method clever or has grown conceited. Why: Vanity drives the wish to be recognised, which exposes the offender.
- [behavior] A person accused of a wrong may defend it by weighing one questionable act against many blameless ones, a common self-justification. When: when defending a single misdeed. Why: tallying good acts feels like a fair balance.
- [behavior] A person is more likely to reveal guilt when a relevant subject comes up unexpectedly while they are off guard. Why: Surprise lowers the composure that normally hides guilt.
- [fact] Offenders may betray themselves through vanity, a need to confess, flight without covering tracks, reckless confidence, or a return to the scene.

## scene clues and details
- [mechanism] Changing the light on a scene, such as moving a candle to throw light through a gap, can reveal details previously hidden and raise the observer's certainty. When: When an observer reports a detail that was hard to see under the original lighting. Why: Lighting determines what is visible, so a new angle can confirm or refute an earlier observation.
- [behavior] Players who observe NPCs in a room can match behaviour to the room's objects, confirming or rejecting an inference about its use. When: NPCs act within a furnished space that carries routine-revealing objects. Why: Matching behaviour to objects lets players test their inferences against evidence.
- [behavior] An investigator's own movement can disturb a barrier or clue, so the investigator becomes a possible source of the disturbance. When: The investigator moves near a protective barrier or evidence. Why: Physical movement can knock or shift objects without intent.
- [heuristic] Ask whether the occupant usually locked the room. If the normal habit is locking, an unlocked door at the later check is an unusual change worth investigating. When: When a door's state at a time of discovery conflicts with expectations. Why: A departure from routine marks a change in the scene that may be significant.
- [heuristic] A vehicle can be identified by tire marks and damaged plates, and a report of a found vehicle may match it. When: Physical traces of a vehicle exist at a scene. Why: Distinctive marks narrow the set of possible vehicles.
- [heuristic] Note where objects are kept, hidden or repeatedly handled, since a concealed or often-touched item can reveal intent. When: observing a subject's relationship to objects. Why: concealment and repeated handling suggest significance.
- [pitfall] A room left unsearched is a blind spot, and a missing item may still be there, so the gap must be recorded rather than ignored. When: a house-wide search excludes a room. Why: Unsearched space can still hold the item being sought.

## stealth and timed risk
- [mechanism] Security that relies on keys, passwords or records held by individuals can be defeated by someone who obtains copies of those items while using the system legitimately. When: A system's access control depends on items held by people rather than on the system itself. Why: Legitimate use exposes procedures, credentials and duplicable items that an attacker can gather and reuse.
- [rule] A test subject who bears real discomfort should be compensated, for example with rest, and the test should not cause lasting harm. When: Using a companion or character as a test subject. Why: Ethical limits protect the subject and keep the test valid.
- [heuristic] Let players discover the difference between real and assumed locks, so their access assumptions can be tested. When: Designing doors whose lock status differs from what players assume. Why: Discovering that an assumed lock is not real rewards testing and revises the player's model of access.
- [heuristic] Judge a person's routines, such as meals and daily habits, to choose a visit time when they are reachable and not disturbed. When: Planning to approach a character for information. Why: Routines determine when people are available and receptive.
- [pitfall] A searcher risks being caught, and the consequences of discovery add tension to a slow, careful search. When: When a player searches someone else's belongings. Why: Fear of exposure raises the stakes of each action.
- [pitfall] Acting alone to test a theory carries real risk and fear, which should be planned for in the design of a solo investigation. Why: Solitary risk raises stakes that the player must manage.
- [fact] A plan that depends on a short window of opportunity is fragile, so each step needs a fallback route. When: when a lure depends on a brief moment of absence. Why: Delays or early returns can break a plan that has no alternative.

## crowds and social pressure
- [mechanism] A deliberate pause after a key point lets the audience fill the silence with its own reaction, raising tension without further input. When: When pacing a revelation in front of a group. Why: Silence invites the audience to supply the emotional response themselves.
- [mechanism] Viewing a familiar image inverted can change its emotional character; a pleasant face may look sinister when seen upside down. When: A known image or object is shown in an unusual orientation. Why: An unusual orientation disrupts recognition and changes the affective reading of the image.
- [behavior] Casual teasing among acquaintances often reveals personal sensitivities, because speakers do not guard what they say in social settings. When: When listening to conversation in social settings. Why: Relaxed speakers reveal rivalries and tensions they would hide under direct questioning.
- [behavior] Unguarded moments, such as speech during sleep, can reveal what a subject fears more directly than deliberate answers. When: A subject is not in control of their speech. Why: Without deliberate control, fears surface in speech.
- [behavior] Witnesses who glance toward a group, or mention a name, draw crowd glances and shrugs, and the crowd begins to read them as guilty. When: When a suspect's conduct is observed by many people at once. Why: Public observation shifts attention to the person named or glanced at, even before facts are weighed.
- [behavior] Witnesses may withhold information from social pressure, and silence can itself function as testimony. When: Witnesses are with people whose opinion matters to them, such as at an inquest. Why: Social obligation can keep people quiet even when they know something.

## strain distorts time
- [behavior] Under exertion, fear, disorientation, or disrupted events, people misjudge elapsed time and distance, so a timing gap or conflict with records is not necessarily a lie; check against a dated record. When: when a player or witness is under strain or disorientation, when reconstructing how long ago events occurred, or when a witness's timing conflicts with records. Why: strain and disorientation distort the sense of time and distance. (x2)
- [behavior] Sedatives or altered states can stretch or shorten perceived time, so a short action may feel far longer or shorter than its measured duration. When: when a witness was under drugs or extreme stress. Why: Altered states distort the sense of duration.
- [behavior] A witness overwhelmed by a strong emotional moment may miss what happens next, so their account of the aftermath can be incomplete. When: When a witness is present during an intense scene. Why: Emotion narrows attention.
- [behavior] A traveller can lose track of elapsed time while absorbed in a task, so a casual remark about the hour can signal lost attention. When: A character is absorbed or distracted during a timed scene. Why: Attention on a task removes awareness of passing time.
- [pitfall] An observer's sense of responsibility can add strain that distorts what she later reports. When: an observer is under pressure to produce a correct identification. Why: stress and responsibility bias recollection and reporting.

## suspense and reveal pacing
- [behavior] Leaving two plausible wrong theories open at once keeps the audience uncertain about who is responsible and what happened. When: Midpoint of a mystery with competing explanations. Why: Neither explanation yet fits all the clues.
- [behavior] Accusers who joke about their own fits or treat them as sport reveal that the display was deliberate rather than involuntary. When: witnesses display levity about the accusations. Why: A casual attitude indicates performance.
- [heuristic] Visible progress toward a reveal, such as a widening gap or a countdown, builds suspense because players anticipate the outcome. When: Staging a slow reveal of a hidden object or a timed reveal in play. Why: Visible progress raises anticipation as players track the approaching moment.
- [heuristic] An in-game warning before a major reveal raises the stakes and gives the player a chance to stop, which can work as a difficulty choice. When: Designing a dramatic discovery the player may not want to face. Why: Advance warning lets the player decide whether to continue, which increases investment in the choice.
- [procedure] 1) Stage the event with a stand-in at the original position. 2) Have a witness help set it up. 3) Trigger the mechanism as it originally occurred. 4) Reveal the hidden part through a visible lever or catch. 5) Reset and repeat the trigger to show it works. When: When a verbal explanation would be hard to believe or physical proof is available. Why: Showing the mechanism persuades witnesses more than describing it, and a witness-run reset proves function.

## status and suspicion
- [behavior] Social standing shapes who is believed and who is suspected: higher standing carries credibility, while poor, marginal or disliked people draw suspicion away from the investigator. When: when a land quarrel between unequal households is revisited, or during suspect rotation. Why: higher standing carries more credibility in the community. (x2)
- [behavior] Listeners overwhelmed by a self-claimed title or praised talent accept it without probing; confident delivery and high-status labels substitute for verification. When: a speaker announces or describes their own high rank, prestige or talent. Why: Status, a confident manner and welcome praise substitute for verification in the moment.
- [behavior] Suspicion moves from a visible enemy to a trusted ally, because players trust the character closest to the protagonist's desires. When: Late stages of a mystery with a hidden betrayer. Why: Trust in a close helper lowers guard.
- [pitfall] Judging rank from manners, servants and an elegant carriage produces false confidence in a stranger's identity. When: An observer infers social status from outward signs. Why: Appearance signals can be staged or misleading, so they are not proof of identity.

## guard procedures
- [behavior] A private conversation with no witnesses feels like candid talk rather than arrest, which lowers a suspect's guard. When: An investigator questions a suspect alone. Why: The lack of witnesses makes the exchange feel safe and informal.
- [behavior] Publicly disciplining someone who investigates deters others from investigating, even without any physical barrier. When: A community punishes an individual who seeks outside information. Why: Social cost works as a lock, so people avoid inquiry to protect themselves.
- [rule] Enforce questioning procedure when public curiosity pressures it, so that testimony remains usable. When: Public or crowd pressure disrupts an interview. Why: Procedural breaches can make testimony unusable.

## other topics
- [mechanism] Impressions made from one fixed template repeat each other exactly, while genuine ones vary, so uniformity across a set can expose forgery. When: Several impressions are available side by side. Why: A flat template produces identical results, whereas a natural source varies with each contact.
- [behavior] Owners of a puzzle often assume it was missed for lack of skill, but clues kept in separate sources are rarely joined. When: A puzzle's parts sit in different documents. Why: No one holds all the parts together.
- [behavior] Under group questioning, people interrupt or steer talk toward their own concern, so interruptions reveal who wants to control the topic. When: A group discussion turns to sensitive matters. Why: People with something to protect try to direct the conversation.

## repeated review failure
- [pitfall] Re-examining the same unchanged clue, even while angry, adds no information; repetition rarely reveals a hidden link without new evidence or a different frame or test. When: Stuck on a puzzle with no new angle, or a player keeps revisiting one clue without new evidence or changed state. Why: The clue's content is fixed and the unchanged frame keeps directing attention to the same details, so only new evidence or a different test changes the conclusion. (x2)

## status from threat
- [behavior] Figures whose standing rises by fighting a threat may have reason to keep people afraid of it. When: a role's status depends on opposing an enemy. Why: their standing rises as fighters against that enemy.

## sudden protective act
- [behavior] A protector facing exposure of someone they shield may act in fear, so a killing can be sudden and unplanned rather than premeditated. When: a protector faces exposure of a loved one. Why: Fear of consequences can drive an impulsive act.

## temporary concealment
- [mechanism] Concealment through distraction ends when attention returns, making the hidden danger visible to everyone at once. When: When a cover depends on directed attention. Why: Cover based on distraction is temporary by design.
