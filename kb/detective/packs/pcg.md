## clue sourcing and verification
- [model] A frame journal puzzle is a player-found manuscript whose margins or erasures contradict its own claims, and the contradiction is the solvable clue. When: when designing a document-based clue in a dungeon. Why: Contradictions in the record let the player solve the puzzle from the document itself.
- [mechanism] Confirming a lead by checking its source, such as a rack or locker, can be an optional sub-puzzle that either narrows or closes a path. Why: Each check either adds a suspect location or removes one from consideration.
- [mechanism] A clue with a misleading surface reading, corrected by a second source such as a note or log, keeps first-look interpretations from deciding the outcome when rewards wait for confirmation. When: When designing clues in a generated level. Why: Requiring corroboration before reward prevents players from acting on the first, wrong reading.
- [mechanism] NPC testimony that includes a checkable detail, such as a garment, arrival time or false name, lets the player confirm the claim against an item or location. When: Generating clues for an investigation dungeon. Why: Verification against game objects turns testimony into a playable deduction.
- [mechanism] Identifying marks visible only under a scanner or x-ray view can be placed in a level, and matching them to a records dossier lets each match confirm identity independently. When: Designing body-search or identification puzzles. Why: Hidden information rewards the right tool and reading, and independent matches build conviction.
- [mechanism] A detection device can give an accurate reading while the player's interpretation of its meaning is wrong, producing a false solution even though the instrument itself is trustworthy. When: Designing a puzzle or mystery built on a sensor, detector or lantern signal. Why: The error lies in the assumption attached to the reading, not in the reading, so the false conclusion feels earned.
- [mechanism] In an interrogation system, unverifiable explanations raise suspicion, while verifiable explanations lower it. When: A player questions a suspect who offers an account of their actions. Why: Verifiable claims can be checked and cleared, while unverifiable ones leave suspicion in place.
- [rule] Give a forged letter, ledger or message a checkable property (paper from the wrong room, ink the victim would not use, typewriter, seal) that matches or contradicts a record elsewhere. Why: The player can verify the forgery only by visiting the other location; a cross-reference gives a concrete way to test authenticity. (x2)
- [rule] Let players test a legend or partial NPC explanation against physical traces or a later record (ledger, dated inscription) before trusting it, since it may be partly false. When: Designing legends or NPC lore for a mystery. Why: Legends and partial explanations can be partly false, so physical traces or later records verify them. (x2)
- [rule] Split in-game documents and NPC testimony into observed facts and interpretation, feeling or gaps, so players can cross-check which parts to trust. When: Designing found journals, letters or NPC testimony in a generated world. Why: Cross-checking against logs, objects or other NPCs lets players judge reliability instead of accepting text or testimony whole.
- [rule] Mark some NPC testimony as drawn from an unverifiable source, so players must cross-check it against physical clues. When: when designing mystery testimony. Why: unverifiable claims need independent support.
- [rule] Link a financial clue to a later witness so the player must cross-check two sources before accepting a motive. When: When placing motive evidence in a quest. Why: Requiring corroboration makes the motive feel earned.
- [rule] Before placing a planted fact such as sight range or light level as a clue, verify it against the world's own rules.
- [rule] Tag false objectives or traps as staged, and build clue trails from timing and placement so careful players can detect them.
- [rule] Track means, motive and opportunity as separate verified flags, so the player sees which part of an accusation is still unsupported.
- [rule] Give an NPC portrait or wall painting a subtle flaw as a hint, and tune its art style so the hint is easy to misread. When: Generating environmental visual clues.
- [rule] Show overlaps between two sources only after the player has collected both. Why: Premature overlap removes the discovery of the link.
- [rule] Keep names hidden from the player until a second, independent source confirms them. When: A dialogue system reveals identities tied to a secret. Why: Early disclosure removes the deduction and a single source may be unreliable.
- [rule] Let players uncover the truth by spotting a mismatch, such as a weight too light, an initial belonging to someone else, or a room too clean. When: Designing a discoverable deception. Why: Noticing an inconsistency rewards careful observation.
- [rule] Let a journal or ledger list several named witnesses, each giving a separate detail, so players can cross-check the story. When: Designing in-world records of events. Why: Multiple sources let players verify the account.
- [rule] A puzzle can present a note that must be matched to a character using two visible features, such as slant and letter shapes, that differ on the surface. When: when designing a document-matching puzzle. Why: Two features give the player a concrete comparison to make.

- [rule] An NPC can give a true report that other NPCs dismiss. Let the player verify it through a physical object, which then restores the NPC's credibility. When: Designing NPC reports that peers reject. Why: Physical verification gives the player grounds to trust the report over social consensus.
- [rule] Plant a hidden weak point in a concealment, such as an item moved too late, as a thread the player can pull to expose it.

- [rule] A document puzzle can let the player compare an item's physical property against each suspect's stored samples, and false names on documents should be made misleading. When: Designing document or item clues in a game. Why: Property comparison gives players a fair, checkable path to attribution.

- [rule] Let the player accept or challenge a confession, and record whether it was extracted, so its reliability can be checked later. When: confessions are used as evidence in a generated world. Why: The game can later reveal whether trust in that evidence was warranted.
- [rule] Show players when a testimony lacks corroboration, but still require them to act on it to progress. When: a quest relies on unverified claims. Why: Players can recognise the risk while the choice still matters.
- [rule] Have one NPC echo another's testimony and let the player catch the echo by comparing the two statements. When: generating NPC testimony. Why: Comparison turns coaching into a discoverable clue.
- [rule] Hide an omitted passage in a copied document, so a player who compares the copy with the original discovers what was removed.
- [heuristic] Have two separate sources, such as a journal and an NPC's memory or a second independent NPC, confirm a location, name or detail before the player acts on it. When: When a lead is important enough to need confidence, or testimony gates player action. Why: Independent confirmation raises trust in the lead and lets players corroborate testimony before committing. (x2)
- [heuristic] Tag each log entry and NPC statement with its source type (first-hand or relayed), confidence and hearsay depth so players can weigh how far to trust each and what to cross-check.
- [heuristic] Mark an NPC's answer as unverified until the player checks it in the world, with a risk that a quick confession alerts enemies. When: Generating information from captured NPCs. Why: Verification keeps players from trusting coerced answers without evidence.
- [heuristic] Design each trace to fit a route the player can test, such as a gate, a wall or a path visible from a window. When: Building routes that a suspect must have used. Why: Testable routes let players confirm or rule out access.
- [heuristic] Scale verification to stakes: a minor clue may accept a written note, while a critical claim requires the witness present in the scene. When: when deciding how a player can verify a claim. Why: higher stakes justify costlier confirmation.
- [heuristic] Give a witness NPC a confident answer the player can test against a map, log or actual location, even when the NPC has no direct knowledge. When: Generating witness NPCs whose statements should be checkable. Why: Testable answers reward investigation and expose unreliable witnesses.
- [heuristic] A clue found by a trusted character carries more weight in a scene than one found by a stranger. When: assigning which character reveals a document. Why: Who discovers a clue shapes how much the player believes it.

- [heuristic] A portrait or sketch can be matched to a historical record to identify a hidden antagonist, with the matching done by the player. When: Designing identification puzzles for a hidden antagonist. Why: Player-driven matching turns identification into an active deduction.
- [heuristic] Plant a decoy trail, such as a note or dated item, that points to an NPC with a clean alibi, and let the player test that alibi. When: generating a misleading lead for the player. Why: Testing an alibi turns a false lead into a verifiable step that rules out the NPC.

- [heuristic] Hide a clue in content that fits its genre, such as an essay or poem, so it reads as ordinary material to casual readers. When: when a clue should blend in and be decoded only by a knowing reader. Why: Readers skim genre content without suspicion, so the clue survives casual reading.
- [heuristic] Use an artifact from an outside source, such as a clipping or log, as the verifying key for an in-world manuscript, so the player can cross-check the account. - [heuristic] Use a recurring clue as a generated seed so that the same name or object reappears in later roots or documents. When: generating connected clues across a level. Why: Repetition of a seed object links rooms and keeps earlier clues relevant.
- [heuristic] Give the player a document or object that must be tested with a specific tool before it can be trusted. When: Building an evidence-based puzzle in a generated level. Why: Testing the object, not accepting it, turns a passive find into an active deduction.

- [heuristic] Plant evidence that the player can check later, such as footwear sized to match tracks, so that a late trap pays off a mid-game clue. When: Generating clues for a multi-stage mystery. Why: Evidence placed early and verified later creates a payoff the player can follow.
- [procedure] 1) Give an item or symptom that contradicts the obvious poison. 2) Provide a diagnostic test that reveals the masking agent. 3) Use the mismatch between result and expectation as a second test or pointer to the hidden substance. When: When building a poison or pill puzzle, or a poisoning puzzle with a diagnostic tool. Why: A symptom that disagrees with the obvious cause points the player to a hidden substance.
- [procedure] 1) Tag each scene object as fitting or contradicting the main theory. 2) Rank leads by the number and strength of contradicting tags. 3) Present the contradicting items as the primary investigation targets. When: building a clue system that lets players rank their leads. Why: Ranking by contradiction directs the player toward the most informative anomalies.
- [procedure] 1) Create in-world logs, ledgers or diaries. 2) Have each confirm or contradict an NPC claim. 3) Let players compare the record with the claim. When: Designing NPC testimony that players can verify. Why: Written records give players an independent check on NPC statements.
- [procedure] 1) Place a contradictory detail, such as an unexplained item on a shabby NPC, in dialogue or an inventory. 2) Log it for the player. 3) Give the NPC a plausible reason to skip follow-up. 4) Keep the flag available for a later check. When: Designing NPC dialogue or inventories that hold mysteries. Why: The clue stays discoverable and revisitable without forcing an immediate answer.
- [procedure] 1) Have an NPC tell a fable embedding real vault details, such as trap order or key location. 2) Let the player match the fable against the map to deduce the route. When: Designing puzzle-driven vaults where NPCs hold partial knowledge. Why: Players can reconstruct real layout from a disguised account they can verify.
- [procedure] 1) Make each NPC statement checkable through a physical action the player can take. 2) Let the player combine several partial statements to rebuild events. 3) Track each suspect's position through a shared timeline. When: When designing NPC testimony in a mystery game. Why: Checkable and combinable statements let players reconstruct events from partial, sensory-limited accounts.
- [procedure] 1) Have the player gather a testimony from one NPC. 2) Require verifying it against archive documents. 3) Only then allow naming the culprit. When: Designing a culprit reveal in a mystery quest. Why: Cross-checking testimony before naming the culprit makes the reveal earned and fair.
- [procedure] 1) Collect sample items from each suspect's location. 2) Run a comparison test at a lab node against the evidence. 3) Use the match to reduce the suspect list to one person. - [procedure] 1) Take a control sample from a neutral site. 2) Take a target sample from the suspected site. 3) Compare the two so the difference is easy to read. When: when a scenario uses environmental sampling to find a hidden agent. Why: A paired control makes the signature in the target sample clear.
- [procedure] 1) Let the body's identity be inferred from items at the scene. 2) Place a late item, such as a letter or receipt, that reverses the identification. When: Designing a dead-NPC investigation puzzle. Why: A late reversing item rewards players who tracked the scene's details.

## timelines and timestamps

When: designing timed objects in generated rooms. Why: a mechanism's recorded state can be ambiguous, so it must be cross-checked.

- [mechanism] A body left undiscovered until environmental conditions finish the job makes the player work backward from the delay to find who staged it. When: Generating a death whose timing depends on the environment and a staged activity. Why: The delay separates the apparent time of death from the actual one, which the player must reconstruct.
