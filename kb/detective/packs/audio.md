# audio

## distraction
- [mechanism] Hearing an event without seeing its outcome, or hearing it from a locked area one cannot reach, withholds information and raises tension and a barrier to action. When: An action's outcome is out of view or an event is audible but unreachable. Why: Tension depends on withheld information and barriers to action, not on the event being displayed.
- [mechanism] A visible event elsewhere, such as a failed light, draws guards and visitors away from the true route.
- [mechanism] A cry cut off mid-note locates danger without showing it.
- [mechanism] A character kept busy with a task can cover a second action, hiding its real purpose when the audience sees events through one biased viewpoint. When: events are shown through a single character's perspective. Why: the audience's attention and bias are directed at the distraction.
- [mechanism] Keep a fetched fact's purpose hidden until a later scene, creating a why-mystery that pays off later.
- [mechanism] Gloomy atmosphere and strange furnishings absorb attention and pull it away from the trail the player needs. When: An area is dense with striking background detail near a key clue. Why: Striking surroundings compete with the important clue for the player's focus.
- [mechanism] An NPC whose movements are heard but not seen leaves unobserved gaps that players can exploit through hidden routes. When: An NPC's schedule is only audible. Why: Unseen movement is not constrained by what the player can watch.
- [mechanism] An audible hint, such as a sob or footstep, lets players locate a hidden target without a full sweep, ending the search early. When: A hidden NPC or target emits sound.
- [mechanism] Sensory signals of approaching pursuers, such as knocks, voices, footsteps and a lock being worked, tell the target that escape options are closing. When: when a target is being pursued toward capture. Why: Each cue signals proximity and removes an option.
- [mechanism] Ambiguous sounds that are first dismissed and later reinterpreted leave the listener unsure whether another actor is nearby, building suspense. When: ambient footsteps or noises occur where nobody is visibly present. Why: reinterpretation reopens the question of who is nearby.
- [mechanism] Entertainment and playful speech can steer attention away from the serious matter at hand, so listeners end up engaged with the diversion. When: A suspect uses amusement while business is in motion. Why: Diversion lowers the audience's guard on the real topic.
- [behavior] Silence right after a startling sound, such as a scream cut off at once, extends tension and keeps the mystery open, feeling more threatening than the sound itself. When: immediately after a startling sound.
- [behavior] Faint small sounds, such as animals moving, become audible in a silent room and heighten the tension of a scene. When: when a scene is quiet. Why: Silence makes minor sounds prominent.
- [rule] Do not use a diversion that could harm a bystander or expose the person who arranged it, such as one character's noise drawing scrutiny to that character.
- [rule] Let players test audibility, such as closing a door and listening from another room; do not assume no sound carried, but test that claim rather than trust one informant. When: Designing a timed puzzle built on witness sounds. Why: Active testing lets the player verify which sound matches the event.

When: Pacing suspense through sound or read-aloud material, or using synchronized audio cues that mirror narrated events. Why: Apparent coincidence escalates tension when spaced and repeated.
- [rule] Use ambient sound cues such as a creak or footstep to raise tension without guaranteeing that an enemy is present. When: Building suspense in an area the player must explore. Why: Unconfirmed sounds keep players uncertain, sustaining tension without repetitive confrontations.
- [rule] Check an apparent distraction against physical traces, such as footprints, before accepting it as genuine. When: An event looks like a real emergency or distraction. Why: Physical evidence can show whether the event was staged.
- [rule] Hide your purpose from witnesses who could be harmed or alarmed by knowing it. When: Approaching a witness in a sensitive matter.
- [rule] Keep the real purpose of a feint hidden from its target, since the trick works only while the target does not see the trap.
- [rule] Use an unplanned disruption to a prepared scheme to make the player suspect the wrong person.
- [rule] Let an unreliable narrator admit one bias openly while concealing another, so careful players can find the gap. When: designing unreliable narrators.
- [rule] Make fog or darkness a level mechanic that scrambles audio cues, so the player must triangulate the source rather than trust a single sound. When: Designing a level where weather or darkness limits sensory information. Why: Requiring triangulation turns degraded perception into a challenge the player solves by combining cues.
- [rule] Place a locked cabinet or posted rule as ambient detail, with NPC dialogue that names what it contains. When: Populating a room with clue objects. Why: Named contents make the object usable later.
- [rule] Trigger a major event in a busy area, such as a festival, so the player learns of it amid noise and distraction. When: designing a story beat in a level. Why: Distraction shapes how the event is received.
- [rule] Use sounds such as bell pulls and rattled handles to pace an encounter, escalating in strength before the visitor is seen. When: designing a visitor encounter behind a door. Why: escalating sound builds tension without visuals.
- [heuristic] Create a distraction so that a searcher can work unobserved. When: a search must happen while others are present.
- [heuristic] Place ambient distractions, such as a guard animal or a cheerful host, to draw the player's attention away from a restricted area. When: When designing a restricted area that should stay hidden. Why: Competing stimuli shift attention away from what is concealed.
- [heuristic] Leave the cause of an ambient sound open, so the player hears it without the game settling on a single source.
- [heuristic] Use pauses in narration or ambient audio to control suspense rhythm, letting tension build with repetition. When: Timing reveals or threats in a scene. Why: Silence between cues sets pacing and anticipation.
- [heuristic] Hide a second reading in an inscription's phrasing that emerges on closer inspection, so a player who re-reads it gains new meaning. When: Writing a note, letter or inscription that the player will examine. Why: A reading that changes on re-examination rewards careful attention and deepens the message.

- [pitfall] A distraction that draws attention to the searcher's own actions can alert the suspect.
- [pitfall] Comic disruption dulls attention to the point that follows it, so a key clue should be placed after the interruption, not inside it. When: when pacing a scene with a comic interruption. Why: the listener's attention is pulled away by the disruption.
- [fact] The investigator cannot know whether a diversion is genuine or staged, which adds uncertainty to the plan.

## sound propagation
- [mechanism] Walls, closed doors, bolts and bars attenuate sound, so witnesses in different rooms hear different things and a sealed-room listener may miss activity next door. When: an event produces sound inside a building with doors and halls. Why: walls and doors attenuate sound. (x2)
- [mechanism] Windows, vents, doors, glass and curtains muffle ordinary dialogue, so outside listeners recover only fragments, with clarity depending on distance and obstruction.
- [mechanism] Shared pipes, shafts and flues carry sound unexpectedly: neighbors outside the exchange can hear speech, and a muffled report missed on one floor may be clear below. When: A hidden channel is shared with other spaces. Why: Sound travels along the same route as the message; structures channel sound along unexpected routes.
- [mechanism] Hearing a cue before seeing its source builds dread and anticipation, as the audience imagines the unseen cause and prepares for the reveal. When: an unseen threat or distressed character is present, or danger is hidden from view. Why: Audiences need time to prepare for a discovery to land with force.
- [mechanism] Opening a window or shutter slightly improves hearing, but the gain is limited because the action makes noise that can jar the listener. When: when a window is raised as a lever. Why: The gain is limited by the risk of a sudden jarring noise.
- [mechanism] In a quiet, empty space small sounds carry and stand out, so an approaching footstep is heard before its source is seen and a faint noise builds suspense. When: when ambient sound is sparse or a scene is kept still and quiet. Why: Without competing noise, faint sounds remain distinct and draw attention.
- [mechanism] Hearing depends on position: a corridor, a noisy room or distance from a door decides which words reach the listener.
- [mechanism] A clue heard as a snippet through a door or wall can hold back its full meaning for a later scene.
- [mechanism] A sound cue such as a voice or shout points to a region without naming a character, so players must weigh it against each candidate's route. When: designing audio clues for investigation. Why: an unnamed sound narrows location but leaves identity open.
- [mechanism] Flagged dialogue can trigger listener NPC reactions, such as turning away or denying, that the game records as clues.
- [mechanism] A sound that draws a guard away from a chokepoint leaves the post empty, opening a timed gap that others can exploit.
- [mechanism] Distance, several stairs, long halls and heavy doors together can keep a gunshot from reaching other floors or the street, especially with closed windows.
- [mechanism] Closing a window or door cuts off all sound at once, so a closed barrier can end the flow of information at a dramatic moment.
- [mechanism] Listening through a shared wall can reveal plans, so proximity or sound checks can change what nearby NPCs learn or say. When: Designing stealth or information gathering through adjacent rooms. Why: Sound passes through partitions, so proximity determines what a listener hears.
- [rule] Play dialogue through walls or doors so only tone and fragments are audible, forcing players to infer what is happening inside. (x2)
- [rule] Base elimination on sound observed facts, not on a hunch dressed up as logic, since the method depends entirely on the reliability of its facts.
- [rule] Ask a witness directly whether they could swear to a sound, forcing them to separate firm impressions from doubt.
- [rule] Let players test whether noise carries between rooms by making a sound and observing which guards react. When: when a level's stealth depends on sound propagation. Why: observable reactions make the propagation rule learnable.
- [rule] Abandon an eavesdropping position if targets sit far back inside a room, since little of their conversation will be audible. When: when choosing a listening spot. Why: Distance cuts sound too much to be useful.
- [rule] Model sound propagation by room and by door, so listeners hear only what their position allows and audio clues can be checked on the map. When: designing sound-based clues. Why: Audibility should match the physical layout.
- [heuristic] A sound heard without a time anchor stays unreliable until a later sound, such as a gate clicking shut, pins its timing or marks a second party's arrival or departure. When: Movements happen out of sight and the timeline needs an anchor. Why: Sounds are heard without being seen and can fix events precisely.
- [heuristic] Before accepting an explanation or count for a reported sound, check it fits the sound's nature and timing; one heard shot may be a muffled one or two close shots. When: A witness attributes a noise to a common cause such as a door closing, or reports gunfire or noise. Why: Plausible-sounding explanations are often assumed without testing.
- [heuristic] A sound constrains timing but does not identify its maker or a hidden object's contents; confirm with more evidence before assuming. When: a sound is linked to a suspect or an object is hidden from view. Why: Sounds can mislead; timing evidence narrows events, not identities.
- [heuristic] Verify a heard sound by measuring distance and sightline from the listening position on site, rather than assuming it was audible.
- [heuristic] Make sound a clue, such as a groan behind a locked door that the player must interpret, and include a harmless noise as a decoy. When: Designing audio-based evidence. Why: Ambiguous sounds reward interpretation and test the player's judgment.
- [heuristic] Use ambient sounds such as ticking clocks, breathing or creaking floors as cues that signal danger before anything is seen. When: darkness or stealth segments. Why: Audio gives the player information in a dark space where visuals are limited.
- [heuristic] Sound-alike fragments of a foreign phrase can resolve into a place name, after which other details can be read as parts of a route. When: Partial speech is in an unfamiliar language.
- [heuristic] Give a guard or NPC who overhears half a conversation a plausible but wrong reading, so players must assemble the full picture from other sources. When: designing eavesdropping NPCs. Why: partial information produces believable error.
- [heuristic] Do not assume walls block sound where rooms share open corridors or streets, since sound carries across them.
- [procedure] Test a witness's claimed identification of a sound by comparing it with that sound's known source before accepting it. When: When a witness says they recognised a particular noise.
- [procedure] 1) Drop a small object into the hidden shaft or cavity. 2) Listen to the impact and its timing. 3) Judge depth or emptiness from the sound, without opening the structure. When: when a hidden space must be checked without opening it.
- [procedure] 1) Form a guess about an unexplained sound. 2) Reproduce the sound with a candidate action. 3) Repeat the test before concluding. When: a sound inside a room has no obvious source. Why: reproducing the sound tests the guess directly, and repetition guards against a single coincidence.
- [procedure] 1) Stand behind an obstacle that blocks the most watchful person's sight. 2) Map speakers by voices and silhouettes. 3) Improve hearing with small adjustments. 4) Withdraw at once if the window is opened by another. When: when a closed room holds a plan and direct entry is impossible.
- [pitfall] Treating sound as proof is a common error: a closing door shows it closed, not who was on either side, so sound deductions need later confirmation or a contrary detail. Why: A sound confirms an action occurred but does not identify the actor or their location.
- [pitfall] An unexplained sound left unresolved frustrates players. It needs a later explanation or a tie to another clue. When: when a mysterious sound is used as a clue.
- [pitfall] A sound or sign that appears without prior setup has no emotional effect.
- [pitfall] Footsteps carry far on still, quiet roads, so a follower can be given away by sound even when out of sight. When: A follower moves on a quiet road or open ground. Why: Sound travels further when the surrounding environment is still.
- [fact] Speakers close to an open window carry their voices outward; a long conversation cannot be fully whispered, so a nearby listener is likely to hear it.
- [fact] Distance, a wooden door, and an enclosed horn aimed at the door each blur a recorded voice's timbre, making it hard for a listener to identify it.
- [fact] Singing carries farther than speech, so sung lines are often the only words a distant listener can make out clearly.
- [fact] Sound carried by wind can travel a long way, so a distant source can seem close. When: Judging the distance of a sound in an open environment. Why: The transmitting medium distorts apparent proximity.

## sound-based identification and recognition
- [mechanism] A threat that depends on a small audible cue, such as a weapon being cocked, gives the listener a brief advantage if they react to the sound first. When: Two parties face each other in darkness and one can hear the other's preparation. Why: Sound arrives before a visible action, giving reaction time.
- [mechanism] A sound from an offscreen event can bridge a reveal, letting the audience connect distant events through audio cues. When: the cause of an event happens out of view. Why: Audio carries the link when the audience cannot see the source.
- [behavior] Sounds through walls are hard to localise and can be attributed to a different location or person, so verify the source before tying audio cues to a location. When: A sound comes from a hidden or adjacent space. Why: Sound through walls is hard to localise. (x2)
- [rule] Let footsteps or voices identify nearby characters, so a player can confirm a target without line of sight.
- [rule] Give hidden NPC state a distinct audio signature, such as footsteps, a lighter or smoke, so players can infer it without sight. When: designing stealth encounters. Why: audio lets players read state without seeing it.
- [rule] Use an imitated ally's voice as a lure only when players should learn to question what they hear. When: designing a dangerous room with a deceptive sound.
- [rule] Let players verify a false voice by finding the real character elsewhere, so the reveal feels fair rather than arbitrary. When: using an imitated NPC voice as a lure.
- [heuristic] When sight is blocked, sounds such as footsteps, chair creaks, locks, lift sounds and doors reveal an occupant's identity, activity or route. When: Stealth or detective scenes with limited sight. Why: Sound conveys presence and action. (x2)
- [heuristic] Do not assume two voices mean two people, since one performer can imitate a second voice; separate what is heard from how it was produced. When: when speech is heard through a barrier.
- [heuristic] Trace a repeated phrase to its source before treating it as a name; it may be a routine like counting, a trap countdown, or a monster mimicking a voice. When: when a fragment of overheard speech seems to name someone.
- [heuristic] A distinctive sound heard earlier can trigger memory and make a place feel concrete on a return visit.
- [heuristic] A sound's distinctive feature, such as an echo that differs from the original, identifies its source more reliably than its loudest part.
- [procedure] 1) Capture a sequence of sounds, not a single step, since gait and cadence identify the mover. 2) Rule out candidates whose movement type or rhythm does not fit. 3) Consider why the sequence would occur for the remaining candidate. When: Deducing an unseen mover from audio, such as a suspect out of sight with a repeated sound signature. Why: Gait and cadence carry identifying information; the full pattern reveals what no single step does.
- [procedure] 1) List known candidate sources of the sound; do not accept the first plausible one untested. 2) Reject each candidate whose expected features do not match. 3) Match the sound's unusual feature to the remaining candidate. 4) Confirm that source with a check. When: when a sound's features do not match its expected source. Why: Accepting the first plausible source untested leads to a wrong identification.
- [pitfall] A half-remembered match between a sound and a known source is only a lead, not proof, and should be confirmed before acting on it. When: A player recalls a sound they think they have heard before. Why: Recall is unreliable and can be wrong.
- [pitfall] A false voice that is too easy to spot feels cheap; give a subtle clue, such as a wrong position or a missing reply, instead.
