When: a single authority controls access to a witness or account. Why: Without outside contact, no one can test or contradict the account. (x2)
- [mechanism] A watcher's report lets their employer prepare for the expected location before the observer arrives there. Why: reports pass knowledge to whoever gave the order.
- [mechanism] A visible watcher turns an ordinary departure into a moment of danger and reminds the audience that the antagonist is still active.
- [mechanism] In a game, delivery systems delayed by watched routes make players reason about who observed whom and when. When: messages travel through surveilled routes. Why: observation limits what senders can send unnoticed.
- [mechanism] Observers placed near a hiding place make retrieval dangerous, discouraging a culprit from returning to recover the evidence.
- [mechanism] Hidden listeners can be made detectable through noise and changed NPC behaviour, creating suspense around what is overheard. When: Designing a scene where a character may be eavesdropping. Why: Signs of a listener let the player sense danger without seeing the listener.
- [mechanism] A guard NPC can be set to surveil a suspect without alerting him, and questioning can be unlocked only when the suspect's state allows it. When: Designing stealth or investigation encounters around a suspect.
- [mechanism] A single-entrance area can be contained by posting a watcher at each route and having each record passers-by, which narrows the suspect pool and makes the timeline checkable. When: suspect pool is small and the area has few entrances. Why: Each watcher's log constrains who could have been where, so the reports together fix the order of entries.
- [mechanism] Npcs that track a player's movements turn each visible action into a possible risk, so stealthy inspection costs more than an unobserved one. When: when a player inspects a site under watch. Why: watchers notice who is looking at what and report it.
- [mechanism] A hidden recording device that captures NPC conversation can turn private talk into an actionable location clue for the player. When: Designing an information-gathering trap in a mission. Why: Overheard NPC speech gives the player a concrete target to act on.
- [behavior] An alert target quickly notices a follower whose movement is unnatural, such as zigzagging, sideways moves near walls, answering while walking away, or staying in one place. When: a target is alert to being followed, or observers pretend not to notice a player. Why: unnatural movement draws attention. (x2)
- [behavior] Watchers kept on a week of nights grow worn out and their motivation fades; some nearly quit while others persist out of stubbornness. When: a long stakeout with no result. Why: fatigue accumulates over repeated nights.
- [behavior] A target who suspects or discovers observation alters habits or acts for the watcher, so covert watchers are unsafe and lose value once noticed, and quiet records may mislead. When: when a target suspects or discovers observation. Why: Awareness of surveillance alters habits, and the suspect adjusts to the observer.
- [behavior] Uncertainty about being observed produces a constant sense of being followed, so a person sees danger everywhere and tension stays high, even without any identifiable watcher. When: when someone senses surveillance without proof or suspect, or a suspect is kept waiting with no active questioning. Why: Feeling threatened does not require an identified source; uncertainty about being observed produces the feeling of surveillance.
- [behavior] A motionless figure seen in dim light is ambiguous; the observer cannot tell calm from sleep or waiting, so stillness itself breeds dread. When: A figure is visible but poorly lit. Why: Uncertainty about intent fills the gap with fear.
- [behavior] A character who controls the delivery point can read incoming letters first and conceal the tampering, so the recipient never learns the message was seen. When: When a character positioned at the door or mail basket receives correspondence meant for another. Why: Physical control of the delivery point gives first access to messages and leaves no visible trace.
- [behavior] A target who has already seen a stranger may form a poor first impression and watch that stranger closely, so the approacher is also being investigated. When: the target has had an earlier encounter with the approacher. Why: a spoiled first impression persists and makes the target suspicious.
- [behavior] A secret faction known only through its effects seems all-powerful, because its methods stay hidden and the mind fills the gap.
- [behavior] A watched party who notices surveillance often redirects attention, dismissing the observer or closing off the view rather than confronting the watcher openly. When: a person realises they are being observed.
- [behavior] Those who defend a contested site bear repeated intrusions, which can turn neighbors into enemies. When: a site is claimed by more than one party. Why: defending it puts the occupants in ongoing conflict.
- [behavior] An observer infers an unseen occupant from physical traces such as a worn seat or a disturbed chair. When: Examining a room after an event. Why: Traces imply the presence of someone who is not visible.
- [behavior] Approaching a person from hiding frightens them, while approaching along a route they can see lowers that risk. When: when confronting a subject from a concealed position. Why: A sudden appearance causes alarm; a visible approach gives warning.
- [behavior] People trying to go unseen keep close to walls and stay out of the sightlines of windows. When: moving past watched windows or doorways. Why: Windows reveal movement along open lines.
- [behavior] Watched people and bystanders may notice a watcher through small traces such as a face at a curtain, a repeated figure or a lingering stare. When: a watcher is posted at a fixed point. Why: fixed watchers leave repeated, visible traces.
- [behavior] A searcher who is confident an object is absent tends not to recheck, so an object hidden in a previously searched place can go unnoticed. When: A person has already searched a container or space. Why: Confident denial stops further checking.
- [behavior] A target who believes a rival is dying may visit to gloat, revealing intent in person. When: The target has a motive to see the victim and a vindictive or vain character. Why: Vanity and vindictiveness make the target want to witness and confirm the outcome.
- [behavior] A person standing and gazing into shop windows looks occupied rather than loitering, so a stationary watcher draws less notice. When: Waiting on a public street with a subject in view. Why: Browsing gives a plausible reason to stay in place.
- [behavior] When trapped occupants smell burning or see smoke, urgency rises and they force a breakout rather than wait for rescue. When: Occupants are trapped in a room with smoke present. Why: Danger raises urgency.
- [behavior] People stop watching someone they find laughable, so a trivial or ridiculous persona can protect an investigator from scrutiny. When: An investigator must move among people who would stop talking if they knew who he was. Why: A ridiculous appearance lowers others' attention and suspicion.
- [behavior] Observers who see a condition as beyond ordinary human capacity tend to accept supernatural explanations for it, even without independent evidence. When: when a bodily or behavioural feat looks impossible to the observers. Why: A perceived impossibility makes a natural explanation seem unlikely.
- [behavior] A watcher's attention keeps returning to particular visible cues, such as clothing, which reveals how the watcher picks targets. When: Observing a watcher in a crowd.
- [behavior] When people abandon ordinary business to watch a stranger, and resume it once he leaves, the routine itself is a sign of surveillance. When: NPCs stop routine activity when the player is nearby. Why: Idle gathering that dissolves on departure signals watching.
- [rule] Assume a concealed watcher can itself be observed, since targets may notice others in nearby cover, and feigned sleep or hiding gives the watcher away.
- [rule] Route contact with a witness through a controlled intermediary or private channel when official attention may make them withdraw. When: when a witness may change or retract an account under exposure. Why: control keeps the lead protected.
- [rule] Look for signs that a subject has noticed observation, and reread later quiet reports in that light. When: When a subject may have detected surveillance. Why: Detection changes the meaning of subsequent negative results.
- [rule] Do not offer a release that removes the investigator's duty to the wronged party without that party's consent.
- [rule] Restrict handling of key papers to one holder, so the delivery stays controlled and traceable.
- [rule] Watch a suspect rather than question him directly when a direct question would alert him and end any chance of learning more. When: A suspect may be helping a fugitive or hiding something. Why: Alerting the suspect removes the investigator's advantage.
- [rule] Ask each watcher whether anyone else entered during the watch, phrasing the question to cover the whole period rather than a single moment. When: when establishing who could have passed a guarded point. Why: a narrow question leaves gaps the witness may not think to mention.
- [rule] Place a watcher out of sight of the entrances people use, so the watcher observes unnoticed. When: observing a suspect or a meeting. Why: Visibility to others would expose the watcher.
- [rule] In crowd scenes, let NPCs look toward a suspect or hidden clue, and make these glances a detectable pattern the player can track. When: Designing crowd scenes that hint at a hidden connection. Why: A consistent gaze pattern gives the player a readable signal to follow.
- [rule] A watched home location can serve as a trigger for a return event.
- [rule] Wait until the target is fully inactive before acting, and keep the observer hidden in an adjoining space. When: A character must ambush a sleeping or stationary target. Why: Acting early exposes the observer and alerts the target.
- [rule] Log each NPC's position over time so the player can check which witnesses could see a given doorway at a given moment. When: a stealth or investigation system tracks witnesses. Why: Logged positions let the player verify alibis and witness coverage.
- [rule] Keep the investigator out of view when the target is alert to watchers and would recognise them.
- [heuristic] Watch the suspect rather than the object or crowd everyone else watches, since the audience is distracted and the hider's reaction shows what others miss. When: when observing a reaction in a group, or a suspect reacts to an arrival or event. Why: the crowd's attention is fixed elsewhere; audience attention drifts.
- [heuristic] Signal a hidden participant or overlooked detail before the reveal; otherwise a late reversal feels arbitrary, and unease about a missed detail builds suspense. When: a reversal depends on a hidden participant, or a mystery approaches its reveal. Why: An audience that saw a trace accepts the reversal, and a sense of a missed detail pulls attention toward the answer.
- [heuristic] When an object is probably carried on a person, watch for the exchange instead of demanding it, since a demand invites denial or destruction. When: when the holder could deny or hide the object.
- [heuristic] A character who watches a sleeping NPC or a sealed door can signal danger before any battle starts.
- [heuristic] A witness who watches one fixed spot for hours can bracket long periods of a suspect's absence and presence.
- [heuristic] Setting a small controlled test and watching the reaction reveals more than asking directly, because the response shows what the person knows. When: a suspect will not answer directly. Why: reactions expose knowledge that statements conceal.
- [heuristic] Leaving a suspect unpressured while watching their reactions can reveal a hidden party they expect to reach. When: A suspect is a likely channel to someone behind the scheme.
- [heuristic] A clue that disappears and returns after the room empties suggests someone watching that room who knows its routine. The watcher's identity can remain unknown. When: A clue is moved only during quiet periods. Why: A watcher who waits for an empty room avoids being seen.
- [heuristic] Detect watchers through small sounds such as a rustle or creak, then keep acting unaware while adjusting the plan.
- [heuristic] Check the vantage points a watcher would choose, such as a lobby view of the elevators, when looking for a follower.
- [procedure] 1) Release a detained suspect under watch. 2) Wait for the suspect to contact associates or recover resources. 3) Move in with a prepared warrant at the decisive step. When: A suspect has a hidden group, the investigation has stalled, and the expected payoff justifies the risk. Why: A suspect lacking money, housing or contacts will eventually act, creating observable signals.
- [procedure] 1) Search the hair, beard and mouth for hidden glass or notes. 2) Recognise these as the usual means of cutting bars or passing messages. When: A prisoner is searched on booking. Why: These are common hiding places for escape tools and messages.
- [procedure] 1) Stop suddenly in a quiet place and look back. 2) Double back past suspected watchers. 3) Note which one moves away and which stays. 4) Memorise the face of anyone waiting nearby. When: Suspecting being followed in a city. Why: Sudden movement exposes pursuers who must react.
- [procedure] 1) Start from the last confirmed sighting. 2) Check each standard source in a fixed order, such as ticket offices, staff, inns and official posts. 3) Record every negative result as carefully as positive ones. 4) Stop when the list is exhausted and state the matter is unresolved. When: A trail has a clear starting point and a fixed set of sources. Why: A fixed checklist with recorded dead ends prevents premature conclusions and shows what has been ruled out.
- [procedure] 1) Let the suspect believe he is leading the investigators. 2) Watch his route from outside vantage points. 3) Give him a reason to enter, such as a low perceived risk. 4) Close in once he is committed. When: A suspect is expected to act on a plan and can be observed doing so. Why: Letting the suspect lead keeps him unaware of being followed while his moves can be tracked.
- [procedure] 1) Send an operative to a place where gossip circulates. 2) Have them identify the person who attends the target. 3) Have them find that person's weakness. 4) Set a fallback goal if the key item is elsewhere. When: the investigator is stalled and must choose whom to watch. Why: the vulnerable associate is often easier to reach than the principal target.
- [procedure] 1) Restrict access to the scene. 2) Forbid touching anything. 3) Keep the central evidence hidden from newcomers. 4) Observe everyone present as they react. When: when a crowd of possible suspects is on site and none can yet be ruled out. Why: unprompted reactions reveal more than answers to direct questions.
- [procedure] 1) Infer where targets will sit from what they plan to inspect. 2) Count windows and note which are empty, occupied or lit. 3) Compare approaches and choose the least risky. 4) Remove noisy gear and extinguish lights. When: when a listener must overhear a conversation unseen. Why: Each step reduces the chance of detection.
- [procedure] 1) Choose a cover position with a view of the meeting place, such as behind a grave or hedge. 2) Wait until the participants leave. 3) Wait until their voices fade before moving, then follow by a route that stays out of sight. When: A hidden observer must watch a meeting and then follow the participants. Why: Waiting for the parties to leave keeps the observer unseen, and sound cues show when it is safe to move.
- [tradeoff] Leaving a suspect free may gather evidence when they act, but it risks further harm during the wait, so the danger must be weighed.
- [pitfall] A watcher absorbed in duties such as running equipment or answering calls, or in a loud performance, loses track of who is absent and for how long, leaving gaps. When: a witness was busy with a task during the alleged period, or a loud performance coincides with another's absence. Why: Divided attention leaves gaps an alibi-holder could use; attention is captured by the visible performance.
- [pitfall] Inferences from a figure's stillness rest on the observer's theory of that person and are often wrong.
- [pitfall] Dropping a recurring figure without any resolution leaves the audience a loose thread that can feel unfinished.
- [pitfall] Watching a family home can alienate the household and drive the sought person away.
- [pitfall] A visible observer or badly timed test disturbs the encounter and spoils the identity check. When: Setting up a staged identity test. Why: The subject reacts to the watcher or misses the planned moment.
- [pitfall] A companion may never be fully identified, so the player must accept partial results from a tail.
- [pitfall] A delay before a search gives time to remove or add items, so evidence found later in an unsecured space proves less.
- [pitfall] Any change to a body or prop can be noticed later, so even careful handling can reveal that someone interfered with the scene. When: When examining evidence before the official inquiry. Why: Investigators compare the scene against what they recorded earlier.
- [pitfall] A person who watches a room is not necessarily the one who committed the crime. Keep surveillance and guilt as separate conclusions. When: Inferring a suspect from observation alone.
- [pitfall] A watch lapses when the guard favors a particular person; keep watchers impartial and instructed not to relax.
- [pitfall] Watching an object while ignoring the people who can reach it or summon help leaves gaps in the watch.
- [pitfall] A claim that a power has vanished, never demonstrated, cannot be tested and should not count as evidence.
- [pitfall] Long, quiet waiting dulls attention, so a watch must stay alert for the moment that matters. When: Designing a stakeout or long waiting sequence. Why: Extended monotony reduces the player's readiness at the critical moment.
- [pitfall] Unconfirmed sightings stack into a theory when separate reports are merged, so observed facts must be kept apart from inference. When: Multiple weak reports point at the same suspect. Why: Merging unconfirmed reports makes an assumption look like evidence.
- [pitfall] An observer with limited view may miss or misidentify a watcher, since seeing the watched area requires a particular angle.
- [pitfall] Watchers can be disabled before a watch begins, so the plan must not depend on every observer being active. Why: an incapacitated observer leaves a gap in coverage.
- [pitfall] Watchers working the same ground can spot each other, so an independent route risks detection by an unseen patrol.
- [pitfall] Leaving a door shut or other traces visible can alert a watcher, so control what is visibly left behind.
- [pitfall] Warning a watched target ends the surveillance before it yields any information, so observation of NPCs must stay hidden. When: Player tailing of NPCs.
- [fact] Silence from a busy watcher is weak evidence: an arrival matters only if the witness was placed to notice it, and a missed route is not proof none exists. When: when judging an unseen arrival or using guard or sentry reports to rule out entries. Why: Silence only means something when the witness could have seen; observers have blind spots.
- [fact] A suspect's eager consent to a test and a non-matching result only exclude the suspect from that physical evidence, not from the crime itself. When: When interpreting a suspect's cooperation and a negative forensic comparison. Why: Cooperation and non-match address one piece of evidence, not overall guilt.
- [fact] A back route that staff do not watch can explain how someone left a building unseen during a gap in the timeline.
- [fact] An action performed where no one can see it leaves no witness, so only the actor knows its outcome until someone notices the result.

## tailing and shadowing
- [model] Pursuers rely on relays and pre-agreed markers; fleeing targets rely on decoy vehicles and crowd cover.
- [mechanism] Obstacles blocking a pursuer, such as crowds or a moving vessel forcing a swerve, cost it time and lead; each lost moment widens the target's head start. When: a pursuit passes through a crowd or along a route with moving hazards. Why: Each lost moment widens the gap the target can use; blockages and forced detours cost pursuers time, narrowing or reversing their advantage.
- [mechanism] Pursuers converging on one crowded choke point block each other and fall behind, so do not assume a pursuer keeps pace through it.
- [mechanism] Reusing a place where an event occurred before lets the audience anticipate its return and raises tension.
- [mechanism] Letting players reach a partial objective easily signals through the route itself that a stronger challenge, such as a boss, waits ahead. When: Designing a path that leads toward a major encounter. Why: Ease of progress conveys the intent of the level without explicit warning.
- [mechanism] Each delay in recovering a lost item or suspect lowers the chance of finding it, so timers or pursuit states raise pressure during investigation. When: when a search has a time limit or a moving target.
- [mechanism] Patrolling guards or follower NPCs can lose track of the player when the player moves through many corridors.
- [mechanism] A fuel or stamina limit caps how far a mobile agent can travel, so its reachable area can be estimated from the resource remaining. When: when an agent's movement depends on a consumable resource. Why: Once the resource runs out, the agent cannot go farther without a refill.
- [mechanism] Rain, age, and heavy traffic can wash out or blur a scent trail, and the tracker should ignore competing smells and keep to the strongest remaining signal. When: A scent trail is old or crosses busy areas. Why: Environmental factors weaken the signal and add noise to it.
- [mechanism] Following by vehicle lets a watcher keep distance and pursue at once if the target changes transport. When: when a target is likely to travel by vehicle.
- [mechanism] Pursuers divide: some follow the direct trail into a valley while others circle to cut off the target, so the fugitive's lead is measured in time. When: a group pursues across broken ground with a glen or gully between them and the target. Why: searchers cover both the direct route and possible escape routes.
- [mechanism] Pursuers that follow the most recent or loudest cue can be drawn away from a route by a planted decoy sound.
- [mechanism] Pursuit AI that logs repeated sightings of the same character lets a player break contact by changing route or vehicle.
- [mechanism] When a tailed target vanishes, offer a fallback NPC who gives a partial lead to keep the player progressing.
- [mechanism] A stealth tail mechanic can have a patrolling NPC follow the player, with route loops or second exits as tests of whether it keeps pace. When: Designing a stealth or tail mechanic.
- [behavior] A wary target sends a scout ahead and stays put when something looks wrong, so an ambush must anticipate both scouting and delayed movement. When: when the target suspects it is being watched. Why: Targets protect themselves by checking the route and waiting out danger.
- [behavior] A subject avoiding main roads by taking side streets produces a zigzagging trail that the tracker must follow turn by turn. When: The target is trying to avoid observation. Why: Evasive routing breaks the straight line a tracker would otherwise follow.
- [behavior] A tracker that circles at a corner and then picks up a stronger scent and resumes indicates the subject changed course at that point. When: Reading a tracker's pause at a junction. Why: A pause marks a decision point where the signal changed.
- [behavior] Enemies that tail the player can break off when the player turns to confront them, which rewards patient play over sudden pursuit. When: when the player reacts to being followed.
- [behavior] A lured target's first question reveals what they assumed about the sender, which shows what the trap relied on.
- [behavior] A pursuer relying on a single sighting at a fork can fail if the target has unobserved alternatives or a head start. When: When the pursuer predicts a target's route from limited observation. Why: Unobserved options and a head start can let the target reach the goal before the pursuer intercepts.
- [behavior] Following a target across open ground is much harder than in crowded areas, since cover is scarce and a follower's movement is conspicuous. When: A tail must follow a target through open countryside. Why: Few obstacles exist to hide the follower from view.
- [rule] Cover every exit of a building before waiting at one; an unplanned route, such as a stairway, lets the target bypass all observers. When: A target enters a building with more than one entrance. Why: A second route can defeat a single watch; uncovered routes let the target bypass all planned observers.
- [rule] Do not tail a target who knows your face or can see the whole area; use a two-person tail for a sole lead, not when it knows one investigator. Why: A familiar face or open view exposes the follower; recognition ends the tail.
- [rule] Vary a tail's distance and position rather than keeping them fixed, so a target who turns back does not see the same figure repeatedly. When: following a target on foot. Why: a fixed position makes a tail obvious when the target turns.
- [rule] Define enemy escape routes in level scripts so the flight path is predictable and the player can cut it off. When: designing stealth or ambush encounters. Why: a known route gives the player a chance to plan an interception.
- [rule] Plot each patrol's sightlines and timing so players can learn coverage gaps, and reward that learning by letting them exploit the gaps. When: Designing stealth patrols. Why: Learnable gaps turn observation into a skill.
- [rule] Let an NPC tail mission log each stop it makes, so the player rebuilds a timeline from scattered reports. When: When designing tracking missions.
- [rule] A tail mission can require the player to follow a target unseen, with failure detected by distance or line of sight.
- [rule] Let an NPC who follows the player after a conversation create suspicion without explaining the purpose.
- [rule] If an enemy's appearance misrepresents its speed, give a readable visual cue so players can learn its real pace. Why: Players cannot anticipate speed they have no way to read.
- [rule] Use counter-surveillance when a tail is suspected or when moving a valuable person or item across a city.
- [rule] A waiting partner should not speak to the one inside, in case the target is watching.
- [rule] Assign a second unseen agent to follow the suspect so movement is still recorded after the first watcher is withdrawn. When: A suspect is tracked covertly and the first watcher may be exposed or pulled away. Why: A concealed second observer keeps the trail alive without the suspect knowing.
- [rule] Require a tailing mission to follow an NPC to a payment point, with timing set by the NPC's known motive. When: Designing a stealth or tailing objective. Why: Motive-driven timing makes the target's movement predictable and learnable.
- [heuristic] Follow by positioning rather than speed: keep a greater distance on quiet streets, while crowds, fog and dusk let a follower close in unnoticed. When: tailing a suspect on foot. Why: quiet areas expose the follower, crowds and cover conceal them.
- [heuristic] Tail a suspect when its movements or contacts are the main evidence; observed behaviour can confirm or refute circumstantial evidence, and a mismatched destination exposes a false story. When: when an NPC's stated plans are doubtful or its movements are the main evidence; designing surveillance gameplay. Why: Observed movement can contradict spoken claims; observed behaviour can confirm or refute what the evidence suggests.
- [heuristic] A pursuer that predicts your choices acts a step ahead; ask what you would do in its place and plan your move one step ahead of its prediction. When: when a pursuer reasons about or models your movements, such as enemy AI modelling player decisions. Why: A pursuer who thinks as you do will likely take the obvious route; each side's model of the other shapes the next move.
- [heuristic] A recurring hazard that works only when the target is exhausted can be designed as a readable clue chain the player can learn. When: designing traps tied to a character's state.
- [heuristic] Encode readable tells, such as wound angles or stain patterns, that reveal where a hidden enemy stood.
- [heuristic] Splitting a pursuit lets one watcher question a witness while the other keeps the subject in view.
- [heuristic] Let a tailing NPC question a local and learn of a second exit, then lose the trail, so the player can break a tail through a building with a rear door. When: Designing a tailing or stealth chase. Why: Locals provide information that resolves a pursuit, rewarding player observation.
- [heuristic] An NPC who ignores the player in an unexpected way can start a tail sequence; the player's follow confirms a destination that adjusts the next encounter. When: designing NPC follow sequences. Why: a surprising action invites investigation.
- [heuristic] Tie an interrogation subject's health or stress to a limit on questions, so the player faces time pressure before the subject collapses.
- [heuristic] Check the surface a trail lies on: a trace on a footpath suggests a walker, while one on a roadway may come from a passing vehicle. When: Interpreting a trace of movement. Why: The surface constrains what kind of source made the trace.
- [heuristic] Fresh footprints in soft ground lead directly to a hiding place, so follow them straight instead of searching the whole area. When: tracks are visible in soft or wet ground. Why: Fresh tracks mark the most recent path, so following them narrows the search quickly.
- [heuristic] When a pursuer describes a target's clothing down to small items, the trail has been followed closely rather than guessed. When: judging how closely pursuers track a target. Why: fine-grained knowledge requires recent observation.
- [heuristic] A blocker in the path can be cleared with a quick improvised move, which can leave pursuers behind.
- [procedure] 1) Keep to the side of the street with a clear view, switching sides roughly every fifty paces. 2) Stay about two or three yards behind, outside the subject's line of sight. 3) Stop and look, then resume at a distance that keeps the subject visible without contact. When: A follower must track a moving subject on foot along a public street without being recognised. Why: Distance and cover keep the follower unnoticed while maintaining the trail; these values keep it near enough to track but not obvious.
- [procedure] 1) Keep the subject in view. 2) If the subject is lost, reason about where they must go. 3) Learn their schedule from people who know their routine. 4) Arrive at the destination first and wait. When: Tracking a subject whose movements are predictable. Why: Anticipating destinations recovers from lost tracking and keeps the pursuer in position; a pursuer given a learned schedule can arrive before the target does.
- [procedure] 1) Notice whether the same person recurs in several places; a single glimpsed face is often a false alarm. 2) Confirm by walking to a set destination, or by crossing and recrossing to loop back. 3) Someone who keeps pace through the detour is probably following you. When: You suspect a follower, or a face is seen during an escape. Why: Repeated presence across unrelated locations is unlikely by chance and a repeat test confirms it; false alarms waste time and effort.
- [procedure] 1) Turn away rather than chase openly, since chasing reveals your interest and can end the pursuit. 2) Hire a second vehicle at a distance, or go to the likely destination and wait. When: when a watcher is spotted. Why: Patience avoids revealing interest and lets the watcher come to a known point.
- [procedure] 1) Keep a set distance behind the target. 2) Switch sides of the street periodically. 3) Re-acquire the target at turns. 4) Stay out of the target's direct line of sight. When: following someone without being noticed. Why: a moving target can be followed without alerting it.
- [procedure] 1) Enter one vehicle. 2) Leave it by its far door. 3) Board a second vehicle that departs at speed, shedding the follower. When: A target is tailed closely in a street where vehicles are available.
- [procedure] 1) Halt the vehicle in traffic. 2) Step out into the crowd. 3) Board a second vehicle that heads the opposite way. When: a pursuer is following by vehicle.
- [procedure] 1) Arrive before the target and observe from a spot you cannot be seen from. 2) Buy a ticket covering every likely destination. 3) Let the target pass before following. 4) Confirm the destination at a checkpoint. 5) Note late arrivals and where they board. When: the target is unaware and exits are limited.
- [procedure] 1) Identify the path between two points. 2) List spots along it where a waiting party could hide. 3) Compare those spots with physical obstacles such as fallen debris that may block or reveal them. When: testing whether an ambush or interception was feasible. Why: Physical layout limits where a hidden party can wait unseen.
- [procedure] 1) Agree on visible marks before the chase begins. 2) If one watcher must break off, mark each junction on walls, shutters or shopfronts. 3) Mark the direction with a long streak. When: Dense urban pursuit where a watcher may lose the subject. Why: Pre-agreed marks let a partner keep following through crowds, quick turns and busy streets.
- [procedure] 1) Return to the point where the tracker hesitated. 2) Find where two trails diverge. 3) If they run opposite ways from one point, the wrong branch was taken. 4) Follow the other branch. When: A trail seems to break or split. Why: Recovers the true line after a false lead.
- [procedure] 1) Follow at a distance. 2) Remove noisy footwear. 3) Test each surface before putting weight on it. 4) Wait until the target is absorbed in a task. When: Pursuing someone over noisy ground at night. Why: Noise can betray a pursuer, and a distracted target gives cover.
- [procedure] 1) Follow the moving target with a second vehicle, keeping out of sight at the end of the route. 2) Record each location, who enters and leaves, and what is carried in. 3) Report to the coordinator before acting. 4) Obtain legal authority before entering a private location. 5) Plan authority first, then approach. When: A suspect is moving and the location is uncertain. Why: Watchers stay hidden and the decision to act is made by someone else.
